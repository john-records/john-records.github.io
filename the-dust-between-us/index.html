    Chapter One

    The Field Between Towns

    The man came out of the desert walking.

    He led a roan horse by the reins. The horse was in worse shape than the man, which was saying something. Three days of dust, in the creases of his shirt, in the lines around his eyes, worked into the rag he'd been using for a kerchief and given up on. He wore a black hat pulled low and no gun that anyone could see. The bedroll behind the saddle was thin. The canteen hanging from the horn was empty.

    He walked because the horse needed the rest and because he had walked farther than this before and figured he would again. The sun was behind him, throwing his shadow long and thin across the sage and red rock. Ahead, the town of Perdido sat low in the valley, brown adobe against brown earth, no bigger than a thumbprint from this distance but getting larger with every step.

    He did not hurry. Men who hurried into towns were usually running from something, and he had stopped running three years ago. What he was doing now was something else. He hadn't found a word for it yet.

    The road into Perdido passed through a gap in the low hills, and as he came through it he saw the valley spread out below him like a map scratched in the dirt. North and south. The river cut through the middle, thin and green, a ribbon of cottonwoods marking its line. North of the river the land rose into good grazing country, dark with grama grass. South of the river it lay flatter and drier, but workable, if a man knew what he was doing. The mountains stood to the east, blue with distance, capped with snow that would be gone by July.

    He stopped and let the horse blow. His name was Arjun Tucker, and he had not spoken it aloud in three weeks.

    The town came into focus as he descended: four streets of packed earth laid out in a grid somebody had planned and nobody had finished. Adobe buildings, most of them, with a few frame structures that looked newer and sat less easily on the land. One saloon. One general store with a false front that said VARGAS MERCANTILE in letters that had once been red. One church with a bell tower of wood warped by the sun. A livery stable. A handful of houses scattered at the edges, dropped there and forgotten.

    People moved in Perdido, slow in the building shadows. A woman swept the porch of the mercantile, her broom making small arcs in the dust. Two men sat on the saloon porch, chairs tipped back, not talking. A boy led a mule down the street and disappeared around a corner. They had the look of people waiting on something they could not name.

    Nothing looked wrong.

    Nothing looked right, either.

    Arjun led the horse to the livery stable and found a man there, old and brown as saddle leather, sitting on a crate with a piece of harness in his hands.

    "Water," Arjun said.

    "Trough over there. Two bits for the stall, grain extra."

    Arjun led the horse to the trough and let it drink. He took the empty canteen from the saddle horn and filled it from the pump, drank deep, filled it again. The water was alkaline but cold.

    "Passin' through?" the old man asked.

    "Maybe."

    The old man made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "That's what they all say. Most stay longer'n they mean to. Or shorter."

    Arjun loosened the cinch and pulled the saddle off. The roan shook itself and blew dust from its nostrils. He found a curry comb on a nail and went to work, long strokes, working the dried sweat out of the hide. The old man watched him without speaking.

    "You're the first man in a month who curried his horse before he curried himself," the old man said.

    "Horse carried me here. Figure I owe him."

    "Most men figure it the other way."

    "Most men are wrong about most things."

    The old man smiled. Three teeth. Didn't seem to miss the others. "You'll fit right in. Name's Corbett. Corby."

    "Arjun."

    "Arjun." He tested it. "That's one you don't hear every day."

    "My mother was from India."

    "India." He tried that one too. "Long way from New Mexico."

    "Every place is a long way from somewhere."

    Arjun paid for the stall and the grain and left the roan with Corby, who promised to turn it out in the corral once it had cooled. The horse was done in. It would be weeks before it carried a man again, and Arjun told the old man to let it take the weeks. Then he walked out into the late afternoon sun and stood a moment, letting the town settle around him.

    The mercantile was the biggest building on the main street. He pushed through the door into a room that smelled of coffee and drying chiles and old wood, leather goods stacked along the far wall. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, tinned goods, bolts of cloth, boxes of ammunition. A counter stretched the length of the back wall, and behind it a woman was sorting through a crate of canned peaches.

    She looked up when he came in.

    Dark hair pulled back. A face that had been handsome once and still was, but harder now, the way a blade gets handsome after enough sharpening. Her eyes were dark and missed nothing. She sized him up quick and thorough, a measuring most women never bothered with.

    "Help you?" she said.

    "Looking for work."

    "What kind?"

    "Any kind that pays."

    She straightened and studied him openly. "You know cattle?"

    "I know cattle."

    "Horses?"

    "Better than cattle."

    "Guns?"

    The word sat between them. Outside, a horse shifted weight in the street, iron shoe scraping packed earth.

    "I know guns," Arjun said.

    "You're not wearing one."

    "No."

    She let that stand a moment, reading him the way Corby had read his name. "Everybody in this country knows guns. That's not what I'm asking."

    "What are you asking?"

    "Whether you know to keep one in the holster when you need to and draw it when you have to, and whether you know the difference between the two."

    Arjun let the quiet hold. A fly worked the window glass, buzzing against it, getting nowhere.

    "I know the difference," he said.

    She nodded, once. "Beatriz Vargas. This is my store. Freight wagon's coming in from Santa Fe tomorrow. I need a man to unload and stack. Pays a dollar and dinner."

    "I'll be there."

    "You got a place to stay?"

    "Not yet."

    "There's a room behind the store. Cot and a roof. Use it tonight, same rate as tomorrow's work. You work out, we can talk about something longer."

    "Why?"

    "Why what?"

    "Why hire a man you've never met and put him up in your back room?"

    Something shifted at the corner of Beatriz Vargas's mouth. Not a smile, exactly. "Because you curried your horse before you came in here. Corby told me. In this town, that counts for something."

    She went back to sorting peaches.

    .

    The saloon was called the Dry Gulch, and it made its name honest. Floor of packed earth. Bar was a plank laid across two barrels. The bottles behind it held things that would do a man more harm than good, which didn't stop anyone. The place smelled of spilled beer, coal smoke, and the particular sourness of men who worked outside in the heat all day.

    Arjun took a table in the corner, back to the wall, and ordered beans and coffee. The coffee was bad. The beans were worse. He'd had worse of both and ate slowly, watching the room.

    Half a dozen men. Two playing cards near the window. Three at the bar talking low, one of them catching Arjun's eye in the mirror and looking away. The sixth man sat alone near the door, nursing a whiskey, watching nothing in particular.

    The bartender had forearms like pine knots and a face that had taken punches and given back more. He came over to clear the plate.

    "You're the new man," he said. Not a question.

    "Just passing through."

    "Beatriz hire you?"

    "Freight work."

    He nodded. "She's a good woman. Don't cross her."

    "Don't plan to."

    "Nobody plans to. It happens anyway." He picked up the plate and started to turn away, then stopped. "Name's Gus. I own this place, such as it is. You got questions, ask. Got opinions, keep 'em. That's the rule."

    "What's the other rule?"

    Gus considered him a beat. "Don't pick a side till you know what the sides are. Once you know, don't pick anyway unless you're ready to bleed for it."

    He walked back to the bar.

    Arjun sat with his coffee while the card players argued about something that didn't matter and the lamps came on. Outside, the sun was sliding toward the mountains, turning the window gold. The man near the door hadn't moved.

    Then the door opened and a man came in who changed the shape of the room.

    Not the biggest man Arjun had ever seen, but he filled the doorway the way bad weather fills the sky. You felt it before you knew what you were feeling. Tall, wide through the shoulders, face carved from something harder than bone. Town suit, brocade vest, watch chain, but the hands hanging at his sides were working hands, thick-knuckled, scarred. Behind him came two riders, lean men with tied-down guns and the flat eyes of men who didn't talk about what they'd done.

    The man in the suit stood just inside the door and let his eyes adjust. When they did, they found Arjun at the corner table. Something went tight and careful in them, recognition, maybe, or its possibility, and then it was gone and the easy look was back.

    "Gus," the man said. "Whiskey. The good bottle."

    Gus reached under the bar and poured three. The man in the suit took one and his riders took the others. The card players had stopped playing. The men at the bar had stopped talking. The man at the door table had set his glass down, and his hand had drifted toward his belt.

    The man in the suit leaned against the bar, easy, a man with no doubt about what he owned. His eyes moved around the room and came back to Arjun.

    "You're new," he said.

    "Passing through."

    "What's your name?"

    Arjun set down his coffee cup. "Arjun Tucker."

    The name did something to the room. The man near the door looked up. The card players traded a glance. One of the riders shifted, a small movement that was about the rig on his hip.

    "Arjun Tucker," the man in the suit said, tasting it. "Of the Border Wars."

    "I fought there."

    "That's not what I heard. I heard you killed fourteen men in three days at Devil's Arroyo. I heard they gave you a medal and you gave it back. I heard you haven't fired a gun since."

    "You hear a lot of things."

    "I make it my business to know who comes into my valley." He put down his glass. "My name is Silas Brandt. The north valley is mine. The men with me are mine. Soon enough, the rest of the valley will be mine too."

    Arjun said nothing.

    "You're wondering why I'm telling you this." Brandt smiled. It was a genuine smile, which was the worst thing about it. "Because you're a smart man, and smart men know which way the wind blows. You want work, come see me. I pay better than Beatriz Vargas. Better than anyone. A man with your talents shouldn't be unloading freight."

    "I'll keep that in mind."

    "No you won't. But I had to ask. A man who doesn't ask for what he wants doesn't get what he deserves."

    He set his glass down with a soft click and walked out into the fading light. The riders followed. The door swung shut.

    A beat of silence. Then the card players went back to their cards and the men at the bar went back to their drinks, and the man at the door table pushed back his chair and walked out without looking at anyone.

    Gus came over with the coffee pot and refilled Arjun's cup. "Still just passing through?"

    "I haven't decided."

    "You will."

    Arjun drank his coffee. Still bad, but there was a new edge under it now. He couldn't put a name to it.

    "Who was the man by the door?" he asked.

    Gus glanced at the empty table. "Martinez hand. Rides for one of the spreads south of the river. They lost two cows last week, found 'em with their throats cut up on Brandt land. Nothing anybody could do about it."

    "Why not?"

    "Because Brandt's got thirty riders and the law in his pocket and a judge who won't lift a finger. Because the south valley's got five families who can't agree what day it is." Gus set the pot down on the table, and his voice went flat, the way a man's voice goes when he has said a thing so many times inside his own head that saying it out loud is nothing. "Because four years ago three of Brandt's men beat a man to death in his own yard, and his widow watched it happen, and there was an inquest, and nothing came of it. The men who did it still ride for Brandt. One of them drinks in here every Saturday. His name is Pruett, and I pour for him, because that's the business I'm in."

    "Whose yard?"

    "Rafael Vargas." Gus picked the pot back up. "Beatriz's husband."

    Arjun looked at the coffee in his cup. Dark as the bottom of a dry well.

    "Why are you telling me this?"

    "Because she hired you. Because sometimes a man ought to know what kind of town he's walked into before he walks too far in."

    He went back to the bar.

    Arjun sat in his corner and let it settle. The kerosene lamps threw shadows on the walls. He thought about fourteen men in an arroyo, and three years of walking, and a woman who'd sharpened herself into something harder because there'd been no other choice.

    After a while, he got up and walked outside.

    The night was full dark and the stars were out in numbers a man never saw from under a roof. Dust and sage and the thin, clean smell of the river. A dog barked at the edge of town, twice, then quit. From the church, a bell tolled once and fell silent, the quiet after it longer than the sound.

    He walked the length of the main street, past the mercantile, the livery, the church. At the edge of town he stopped and looked out at the valley.

    Dark.

    The Brandt ranch was up there somewhere, north, invisible in it. He could feel it out there, a pressure with no shape to it yet. The south-valley lights were scattered and dim, a handful of them, far apart.

    He'd ridden through a dozen towns like this after the Border Wars, looking for work, looking for quiet. Most were just towns. But some were fields, not towns at all, the ground between two armies, and the ground knew what was coming before the people standing on it did.

    He walked back to the mercantile. A lamp burned in the back window. Beatriz Vargas stood in the doorway.

    "Thought you might have left," she said.

    "Thought about it."

    "What stopped you?"

    "Horse is too tired to carry me anywhere."

    She almost smiled. "Come in. I've got coffee. Better than Gus's."

    The back room was small and clean, cot in one corner, table in the other, a stove putting out more heat than its size had any right to. She poured from a pot on the stove and handed him a cup.

    "You met Silas Brandt," she said.

    "I met him."

    "What did you think?"

    "I think he's a man who's used to getting what he wants."

    "He is. He's been working on the south valley since he came. He'll have it in another year if nothing changes." She set down her cup. "Gus told you about my husband."

    It wasn't a question, and Arjun didn't treat it like one. "Some of it."

    "Then you know enough for tonight." She looked at him directly, nothing soft in it. "What do I want from you. That's what you're sitting there trying to work out. I want you to unload my freight. The rest of it, I don't know yet. You don't know yet. We'll find out."

    She left him the coffee pot and went back into the store. He heard the front bolt slide, then her footsteps in the room above him, slow and steady, until they stopped.

    He sat on the cot with his coffee and listened to the town. Wind working at the adobe. Coyotes in the distance, one calling and another answering, then both of them quiet.

    He thought about the arroyo. He'd been trying not to think about it for three years. Fourteen men. Three days. The fourth morning with the smell of gunsmoke that wouldn't lift, the bodies lying where they'd fallen, and himself walking out of it knowing something about himself he'd been better off not knowing.

    He hadn't stopped walking since.

    What he'd told Beatriz was true: he knew the difference between keeping a gun in the holster and drawing it. What he hadn't told her was that knowing the difference didn't make the choosing any easier. It made it harder.

    In the morning he'd unload freight. In the morning he'd be a man passing through.

    He lay back on the cot with his eyes open, listening to the town breathe, and thought of the valley the way he'd seen it from the gap in the hills: two kinds of country and a river between them, drawn out below him like a map scratched in the dirt.

    Some ground is just ground. And some ground is a field, and waits.

    Chapter Two

    Nothing Happens on a Tuesday

    The blacksmith came in on a Tuesday, though Arjun could not have said why the day mattered except that nothing in Perdido ever happened on a Tuesday, and a man who arrived when nothing was supposed to happen was a man worth watching.

    He came from the south road, which meant he had crossed the same stretch of desert Arjun had crossed three days before and found the same silence on the far side of it. He led a mule the color of old iron, and the mule pulled a cart built by someone who understood weight and balance and had no interest in ornament. On the cart, strapped down under canvas, rode a portable forge, a bellows, an anvil wrapped in burlap, and enough iron stock to keep a town in shoes and hinges for a year.

    The man himself was dark, darker than the traders who came up from Sonora with pottery and blankets. He might have been forty. He might have been sixty. The desert does what it wants to a face, and it had been at his for a while. Hair black and cropped close to the skull. Hands that had worked hot metal every day of a long life, the skin thick and darkened at the knuckles from old burns. He wore a loose cotton shirt, canvas trousers, and boots resoled so many times the original leather was more memory than substance.

    But it was the eyes that made Arjun set down the crate he was carrying into Beatriz's store.

    Quiet eyes. Not soft. Quiet. The kind of quiet a man earns after he has seen enough hard things that nothing needs staring at anymore. When the blacksmith turned those eyes on the town of Perdido, Arjun had the strange feeling that the man was seeing all of it at once, and that all of it had been seen before.

    The blacksmith stopped his mule in the middle of Main Street. He did not glance around, did not hunt for a sign or a friendly face. He sat still and waited, like a man who had arrived exactly where he meant to and was only deciding whether the town was ready.

    Beatriz came to the door of the store and stood with her arms crossed. She said nothing. She had learned to let strangers declare themselves before offering welcome.

    The blacksmith nodded to her, once. Then his eyes came to Arjun, still holding the crate, and rested there a moment longer than they had rested on anything else in the street.

    "You're tired of carrying something heavy," the blacksmith said.

    Not a greeting. Not a question. A statement set down in the open air, the way a man sets a tool on a bench.

    Arjun put the crate on the boardwalk. "Most men are."

    "Most men carry the wrong things." The blacksmith swung down from the mule with the ease of a man who had been doing it since before saddles were invented. "I'm looking for a place to set up. Behind the livery, if the liveryman doesn't object. Behind the church if he does. Behind nowhere at all if nobody wants a blacksmith, but I'd rather not sleep in the open when there's a roof close by."

    Beatriz uncrossed her arms. "Livery belongs to Corby Corbett. He won't object. He's got three horses that need shoeing and a mule that's been favoring the left foreleg for a month."

    "Then I'll set up behind the livery." He studied her a moment, and then Arjun, without hurry. "My name is Kai."

    "Beatriz Vargas. This is my store."

    "I know."

    Her eyes narrowed. "How?"

    "It's the only store. And you're standing in front of it the way a woman stands in front of something she built."

    Beatriz was quiet a moment. Then she laughed, short and surprised, a sound that seemed to startle her more than anyone. "Coffee's inside when you're set up."

    "Thank you."

    The blacksmith led his mule toward the livery, the cart creaking behind him. Arjun watched him go with a feeling he could not have put a name to. Not unease. Something nearer to recognition, though he had never seen the man before in his life.

    "You know him?" Beatriz asked.

    "No."

    "You looked at him like you did."

    Arjun picked up the crate. "I don't know him," he said. "But I think he knows me."

    .

    He found Kai behind the livery in the late afternoon, after the shelves were stocked and the water barrels filled for the evening. Kai had set up with the economy of long practice, the anvil placed so the light would cross it from the east in the morning and the west in the afternoon, the bellows angled for a right hand, the iron stock sorted by length and thickness on a strip of canvas mended so many times it was more thread than cloth.

    The forge was already lit. Smoke rose straight in the still air, and the smell of coal and hot iron filled the alley and settled there like it meant to stay.

    Kai did not look up when Arjun came around the corner. He was working a piece of iron with a hammer that fit his hand the way few things fit anything, no gap, no excess, no question about it.

    "You came to ask how I knew about the weight," Kai said.

    Arjun stopped. "I came to see if you needed anything."

    "That too."

    The hammer rose and fell. The iron sang under it, a high clean note that hung in the air a beat longer than it should have.

    "You carry a gun you don't wear," Kai said. "That's the first thing. Men who carry guns they don't wear are carrying something else instead. It's always heavier."

    "I'm not wearing a gun because I don't own one."

    "You own one. You just haven't seen it in a while." Kai turned the iron with the tongs and checked the curve of it against the light. "Second thing is how you walk. Like a man trying not to make noise. Which means there was a time you needed to make noise, and you regret it."

    Arjun said nothing.

    "Third thing is your hands. I've shod horses for every kind of man there is, and the callus tells the trade. Yours is a gun hand. But it's been hauling freight, which means you set the gun down on purpose. That's not a small thing to do."

    "You got all that from watching me carry a crate."

    "I got it from watching men for a long time. You're not the first I've seen trying to outrun himself. You won't be the last." He set the iron back in the coals. "Most men running are running from something outside. You're running from something inside. The inside of a man is the one place he can't leave."

    Arjun leaned against the corner of the livery and watched the fire. Heat came off the forge in slow waves, and the light was going amber in the alley.

    "You talk a lot for a blacksmith."

    "I talk enough. You listen less than most men, so I'm talking more than usual."

    There was no answer for that. Arjun stood a while in the smoke and the ring of the hammer, and then he walked back to the store, and the sound followed him partway down the street like something that had decided to keep him company.

    .

    Tom Harken rode in at dusk with three Brandt men behind him.

    Arjun saw them from the store window, shelving tinned goods. He saw the dust first, four horses coming easy off the north road. Then he saw the shape of the lead rider, and his hands stopped on the shelf.

    Four years. Tom Harken was four years older and something else besides. The slight forward lean in the saddle was the same, and the left hand loose on the reins while the right stayed free. The hat with the brim turned up on one side where a bullet had clipped it at Resaca de la Palma.

    Arjun had answered that bullet. Not the one that clipped the hat, the man who fired it. He had killed him, and Tom had clapped him on the shoulder and said, Faster than I thought you were.

    That was Tom. He made killing something clean, something a man did and set down. He never seemed to carry it afterward. Arjun had never understood how.

    The horses stopped in front of the saloon. Tom swung down and dropped the reins, and the claybank stood where they fell as though tied to iron. The Brandt riders laughed at something among themselves. Tom did not laugh, but he smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from a hundred campfires.

    Then he glanced across the street at the store window, and the smile went out.

    Arjun did not move. He stood in the window with a can of peaches in his hand and looked back at the man who had taught him everything he knew about staying alive.

    The silence stretched. The Brandt men felt it and went quiet.

    Tom Harken crossed the street.

    He stopped at the edge of the boardwalk and looked up through the window glass.

    "Open the door," Tom said.

    Arjun set down the peaches and opened the door.

    Tom was the same. Older around the eyes, a line between his brows that had not been there, but the same square jaw, the same blunt hands, the same way of standing that said here was a man who had never been pushed off balance and never expected to be.

    "Arjun."

    "Tom."

    Neither smiled.

    "Thought you were in Texas," Tom said.

    "Was."

    "Thought you'd stay."

    "Didn't."

    Tom nodded, slow, taking in the store, the shelves, the apron Arjun had put on to keep the dust off his shirt. His eyes stayed on the apron a beat too long.

    "You're working for the Vargas woman."

    "I'm working."

    "She's south valley."

    "I didn't know about valleys when I took the job."

    "You know now."

    Arjun said nothing.

    Tom shifted his weight. A small movement, but Arjun caught it the way he caught trail sign. The man was doing something he did not want to do.

    "Brandt's going to take the south valley," Tom said. "Not today. Not next month. But he will. He's got the men, the money, and the patience to wait until the south families break on their own. They will. They always do."

    "And you ride for him."

    "I do."

    "Why?"

    Tom's jaw tightened. "He saved my life. Before the wars. That's all you need."

    "So you owe him."

    "I owe him."

    "How long?"

    Tom looked away. It was the first time he had broken, and it told Arjun more than the words did. "Long enough to know I shouldn't have started."

    Silence. From the saloon came the laughter of the Brandt men, a hard careless sound that belonged to men who had never been asked to account for anything.

    "You should leave," Tom said.

    "Just got here."

    "Leave anyway. Go back to Texas. You don't want to be here when this breaks."

    "I don't want to be a lot of places."

    Tom studied him, and between them stood the ghost of a hundred conversations, a hundred campfires, a hundred nights sitting back to back watching the dark for movement.

    "You're still carrying," Tom said.

    "I'm not."

    "You are. Different from most men, but you carry it. I can see it in how you're standing. In how you opened that door. A man who's truly put the gun down doesn't move like you. He doesn't stand where he can watch the whole street. He doesn't keep his right hand clear when he's shelving goods."

    Arjun glanced down at his hands. The right hand was free.

    "I'm not looking for a fight," he said.

    "No. But you're ready for one, and readiness has a way of finding what it's ready for." Tom stepped back off the boardwalk. "I'm not going to say this again. Leave."

    "And if I don't?"

    Tom was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into a register Arjun remembered, not the man who rode for Brandt, but the man from before.

    "Then I'll see you on the other side of something I don't want to see."

    He walked back to the saloon. One of the riders said something. The others laughed. Tom did not. He went inside and did not look back.

    Arjun stood in the doorway while the dust settled back into the ruts. The sun was gone. The first stars were coming out over the eastern ridge.

    Beatriz had been listening. Of course she had.

    "Who was that?" she said.

    "Tom Harken. Used to be my friend."

    "And now?"

    He watched the saloon windows, lamplight behind glass, the shadows of men drinking and not thinking about it.

    "Now he rides for the other side."

    Beatriz said nothing. After a moment she went back inside, and Arjun took off the apron and hung it on the door hook. He did not go to the saloon, and he did not go out to the barn to test the latch. He walked behind the livery, where the forge was cooling and the coals were going down and the blacksmith was waiting.

    Kai sat on an upturned crate, watching the embers. He did not look up when Arjun came through.

    "You heard," Arjun said.

    "I heard."

    "He wants me to leave."

    "Yes."

    "What do you think?"

    Kai reached out with the tongs and drew the coals together, banking them for the night. They pulsed under his hand, orange going to cherry going to gray at the edges. "Watch that," he said. "Coals remember every color they've been before they go."

    It was not an answer, or it was, and Arjun could not tell which. He sat down on the ground across from the forge. The night was coming on cool, and the silence between them had weight, but not the kind that pressed. The kind that held.

    "He taught me everything," Arjun said. "How to shoot. How to read sign. How to stay alive when the odds were bad. He was better than me at all of it and never once made me feel it. Just taught."

    "And now?"

    "Now he rides for Silas Brandt. Now he came and told me to leave."

    "Did he mean it?"

    Arjun remembered Tom looking away. The voice dropping into that lower register. Long enough to know I shouldn't have started.

    "He meant it the way a man means a warning. Not a threat."

    "There's a difference," Kai said.

    "There's a difference."

    Kai took up the tongs again and turned a piece of iron that still held a dull glow at its edges. It was the piece he had been working that afternoon, and it was not going to be a horseshoe. It had length now, and a spine, and the first suggestion of an edge.

    "You didn't answer me," Arjun said. "What do you think?"

    "I think you already know the answer. You just don't want to say it out loud yet."

    Arjun looked at the dark iron and saw himself in it, warped and indistinct.

    "I'll think about it."

    "No you won't. You'll try not to think about it. That's not the same thing." Kai laid the iron on the anvil to cool and folded his hands. "Come back tomorrow. I'll need a hand with the bellows. And you'll need to finish that blade."

    "It's a knife."

    "It's whatever you make it."

    .

    Dawn came gray and cold, and Arjun was at the well behind the store, drawing water for Beatriz's kitchen. The rope was old and rough against his palms, the bucket coming up slow, dripping silver in the first thin light.

    Kai was beside the well. There had been no sound of him coming. One moment the space was empty and the next it was not, and he set a canvas bucket on the stone rim as though he had been standing there all along.

    "You didn't sleep," Kai said.

    "I slept fine."

    "You stood in the yard past midnight and looked north."

    Arjun's hands stopped on the rope. "Maybe I like the stars."

    "Cloud cover last night. No stars. You were looking north anyway."

    The bucket reached the rim. Arjun unhooked it and set it on the stone, and the water went still, and the gray sky lay in it.

    "The Brandt ranch is north," Kai said.

    "You've been in town a day. How do you know about Brandt?"

    "A man who listens doesn't need to ask." Kai lowered his own bucket into the dark and let the rope run easy through his hands. "Twice since I came here, your hand has gone to your right hip. There's nothing on your hip. The hand doesn't know that yet."

    Arjun stood very still. The morning cold worked through his shirt, and somewhere behind the store a rooster called once and thought better of it.

    "You think the gun is the problem," Kai said. "It isn't. The problem is what you're afraid you'll be if you pick it up again. But you already are what you are. The gun doesn't change that. It only shows it."

    He raised his bucket, full, and set it on the rim, and picked it up again by the bail, all of it unhurried and exact, and walked back toward the livery through the gray light.

    Arjun stood at the well while the water went cold in the bucket.

    He worked the bellows for an hour that morning, before the store opened. Kai said almost nothing. Steady, he said once, when Arjun drove the handle too hard, and the fire will tell you the rest. Arjun watched the iron go from black to cherry to a deep waiting orange, and found that his shoulders had come down from wherever they had been riding since dusk the day before, and that he had not noticed them go.

    At midday, word came in with a Martinez wagon. Vargas wire was down along the creek quarter, south of the river. Posts pulled, the cuts clean. Nobody had seen anything. Nobody ever had.

    Beatriz took the news the way she took most things, with her mouth flat and her hands still. "First time they've touched my line," she said, to no one in particular, and went back to her ledger. But her pen did not move for a while.

    In the late afternoon Arjun walked out to see it.

    .

    The road ran south from town along the creek, and the heat had settled hard into the land, the air close and still, the sage giving up its smell in the sun. He walked because the roan was done in and turned out to pasture, and because walking was what he knew, and because a man on foot reads ground a horseman misses.

    The wire was down where they said it was. Cut clean, in three places, the posts worked loose from the hardpan and left lying. Not stolen. Not scavenged. Just cut, by men with tools and time, who wanted it known that it could be done. He read the ground a long while and then stood looking south at nothing, and the nothing looked back.

    Kai was sitting on a flat rock beside the road, half a mile out from town, when Arjun walked back. No mule. No tools. He had walked out into the heat to sit on a rock, and he was doing nothing at all, with the whole of his attention.

    "You followed me," Arjun said.

    "Went for a walk. Road goes where it goes."

    "Five roads out of town. You picked this one."

    "The others are less interesting." Kai did not stand. He sat with his hands on his knees, watching the valley floor where the light was starting to go long and gold. "Wire's cut."

    "You've been talking to somebody."

    "No."

    Arjun stood in the road with the sun on his back and the day's heat rising off the ruts. "Who are you?"

    "I'm a blacksmith."

    "Blacksmiths don't walk into the desert to sit on rocks and wait for a man."

    Kai smiled. It was the first time Arjun had seen him do it, and it changed his face entirely. Not lighter. Deeper, the way a canyon deepens when the sun finds the far wall.

    "Have you met many blacksmiths?"

    "Enough."

    "Then you know the trade. Fire, iron, silence. Silence teaches one set of things. Fire teaches another. Iron teaches the rest. Forty years of those three teachers, and a man either goes quiet for good or starts talking to the people who need it."

    "I don't need to hear anything."

    "You're wrong. But you're not ready to know you're wrong, and that's its own kind of right."

    He rose from the rock, brushed the dust from his trousers, and walked back toward town, hands clasped behind his back, at the pace of a man who had never once been late for anything that mattered.

    Arjun watched him go until the road bent and took him. Then he stood alone in the gold light with the cut wire behind him and the town ahead, and neither one would leave him be.

    .

    That night the store was quiet. Beatriz had gone up early, and her footsteps crossed the room above him, slow and steady, and stopped. The wind worked at the adobe. Far off, a coyote called once and was answered by nothing.

    Arjun sat on the cot in the back room with the lamp turned low. After a while he reached beneath the cot and drew out the trunk.

    Pine and iron, the corners bound, the hasp dark with handling. He had carried it three years through eleven towns and four territories, and he had not opened it in all that time. He knew what was in it the way a man knows what is in his own chest.

    He did not open it now.

    But he sat with his hand flat on the lid for a long time, feeling the grain of the wood gone smooth with the years, and he thought about Tom's voice dropping into the old register, and a hand that went to an empty hip without asking him first, and iron going from black to cherry to a deep waiting orange.

    He pushed the trunk back under the cot and turned down the lamp and slept.

    And when he finally slept, he dreamed of a forge that burned without fuel, and a man with quiet eyes who stood at the center of the fire and did not burn.

    Chapter Three

    The Cutting of the Wire

    The wire was cut clean.

    Arjun reined up and sat the saddle without moving, reading the ground. The posts were pulled, not snapped, worked free of the hardpan with a crowbar, and that meant men with tools and time. The wire itself was clipped at intervals of about thirty feet, too short to splice back together without more work than the fence was worth. Whoever did this wanted it to stay down.

    Third time in two weeks.

    He stepped down and walked the line. The morning sun was just clearing the ridge to the east, coming low and hard across the flats, throwing shadows from every rock and clump of sage. In that kind of light you could see sign that noon would hide. Arjun had learned to read ground in the Border Wars from a man named Tom Harken. Track a rider across stone. Tell how long ago a horse had passed by whether a crushed blade of grama had sprung back or still lay flat. The education had saved his life more than once. It had also taken lives, and he tried not to count those.

    The sign was fresh. Three horses. One man had dismounted to work the posts, heel prints driven deep, a big man or a man carrying weight. The horses had been held by the others, shod, the nail patterns clear in a patch of soft ground near the arroyo bank. He studied the prints until he had them fixed, then stood and looked south along the fence line.

    He could go back to town. That was what Beatriz was paying him for. Freight, the store, the stock. Fence riding was a thing he had taken on himself, because she could not pay anyone else to do it and because the woman had been decent to him when she did not have to be. The buckskin mare was hers too, lent to him when the fence riding started, a solid animal that put her feet down like she had thought about it first.

    He mounted and rode south, following the sign.

    He rode at a walk, keeping the tracks in sight but not crowding them. The men who had cut the wire were not running. They were not even hurrying. That was the thing that settled cold in his stomach. The confidence of it.

    Two miles south he found them.

    The land folded into a shallow draw where the fence crossed a dry creek bed. Two men were working another section of wire while a third held the horses, a bay and a sorrel and a flea-bitten gray that kept slinging its head at the flies. Hard-used range clothes, a pistol apiece. The big one working the post had a rifle in a scabbard on his saddle.

    Arjun stopped the buckskin on the lip of the draw and sat there. He made no effort to hide. There was no cover worth the name out here anyway, sage and scattered juniper and a sky that went on forever, and hiding was not what he was after.

    The man holding the horses saw him first. He straightened and said something Arjun could not hear. The other two stopped and looked up. Nobody moved. The sun climbed. A red-tailed hawk turned slow circles over the draw, watching everything and nothing.

    Arjun nudged the mare down the slope.

    He rode with both hands on the reins, easy, the same unhurried walk. Fifty feet out he stopped. The big man had stepped back from the post and stood with his hands at his sides, not quite reaching for anything, not quite relaxed either. He had a face like a fist, all knuckle and old scar, eyes set close.

    "That's Vargas fence," Arjun said.

    The big man considered the wire, then the post, then Arjun. "Don't see no Vargas brand on it."

    "It's Vargas land."

    "Don't see that neither." He smiled. His teeth were not good. "You the new man up at the store?"

    "I'm the man telling you to leave the fence alone."

    The second one laughed, short and hard, no humor in it. Younger, narrow through the face, hands that kept moving, belt, hat brim, the butt of his pistol. Nervous hands. The kind that got people killed.

    "Dobbs," the nervous one said, low, waiting on a signal.

    "Shut it, Kid." The big man kept his eyes on Arjun. "We're just riding through. Saw the fence was down. Thought we'd help."

    "By cutting more of it."

    "Making sure it was cut right." He said it flat, no smile now, and his eyes were doing the work the smile had been doing before. Measuring. "You got a name, store man?"

    "Arjun Tucker."

    The name landed. Dobbs's shoulders shifted. The Kid's hands went still, which in a man like that was louder than the fidgeting. The third one, rawboned, quiet the whole while, turned his head and spat.

    "Heard that name," Dobbs said.

    "Then you know I'm not asking twice."

    The silence stretched. The hawk called once and the sound fell away into the draw. A horse stamped. The Kid watched Dobbs the way a dog watches a hand.

    "Mr. Brandt doesn't like people interfering in his business." Dobbs's voice had gone careful. "You might want to think on that."

    "I don't work for Brandt."

    "No. You work for the Vargas woman." He let that sit. "Word is you're just passin' through, Tucker. No reason to get tangled in what don't concern you."

    Arjun took in the cut wire. The pulled posts. Three men in a draw with their hands near their guns and the easy arrogance of men who believed they could not lose.

    "You're right," he said. "It doesn't concern me."

    He turned the buckskin and rode back up the slope.

    Behind him the Kid laughed again, relieved this time, dismissive. Dobbs said something he did not catch. Then the crowbar bit hardpan and the work started up again. Arjun rode north without looking back. The sun climbed. The hawk kept circling. Nothing at all had changed.

    He came into Perdido near noon with a tightness across his chest like a cinch pulled one hole too far.

    The town was the same as always. Four streets, adobe the color of the earth it came from, dust hanging in the air. Beatriz's store sat at the corner where the main road crossed the creek, long and low, a porch running the full front. She sold everything a person could need and nothing they did not. Flour, coffee, cartridges, canvas, salt, and a kind of quiet courtesy that had nothing to do with wanting your business and everything to do with how she was made.

    She was on the porch when he rode up, sweeping dust that would be back within the hour.

    He stepped down. "Third time."

    "How much?"

    "Quarter mile. Posts pulled. They're working south."

    She stopped sweeping and rested the broom handle against her shoulder. "You see who?"

    "Three of them. Brandt men. Big one's called Dobbs. The young one they call Kid."

    "Did they see you?"

    "We talked."

    Beatriz studied him. Her face did not shift into fear or relief. It went tight and quiet, like a door being shut from the inside. She set the broom against the wall. "You talked. And then you rode away."

    "Yes."

    "Good."

    "Doesn't feel good."

    "No," she said. "It wouldn't." She went inside, and after a moment he followed.

    The store was cool and dim. Shelves lined every wall, packed in an order Beatriz understood and nobody else had ever worked out. A long counter ran across the back, and behind it stood the locked cabinets. Ammunition. Medicine. Whiskey. The whiskey was not for sale. She kept it for when men needed to talk, and she never charged for it. He had already learned that when Beatriz poured two glasses, something was about to be said that could not be said dry.

    She poured two glasses.

    "Sit down."

    He sat on a barrel of nails near the counter. She stayed standing, her back against the cabinet, the glass held in both hands.

    "My husband's name was Rafael," she said. "His family had been in this valley since the Spanish grants. The water rights ran with the land. That creek you rode this morning is Vargas water, and the spring in the north canyon is Vargas water, and the whole valley drinks from what this family held."

    She took a sip and set the glass down.

    "Silas Brandt came here six years ago with Eastern money and bought the old Henderson place. He wanted the water. He made offers. Rafael said no." Her voice did not change. "Then one evening three of Brandt's riders came to our place, and Rafael went out to talk to them. They beat him to death in our yard and I watched. The judge called it a tragedy. That was four years ago. The men who did it still draw Brandt's wages."

    Arjun sat on the barrel of nails and said nothing. The store was quiet. A wagon creaked past outside. Somebody called out to somebody else and the sound faded.

    "There's more to it," Beatriz said. "You'll not hear it tonight."

    "Why tell me any of it?"

    "Because you rode away from those men this morning, and you think you did a wrong thing, and you need to know what happens in this valley when a man does the right one." She turned her glass a quarter turn on the counter without drinking from it. "You're a gunman, Mr. Tucker. The way you move. The way you keep your back to a wall in an empty room. I'm not asking about it. I'm telling you I can see it."

    He did not answer.

    "You came here for work that didn't need a gun. I understand that and I respect it. But Brandt won't stop. He'll cut wire and run off stock and lean on the small outfits until nobody's left to lean on. Then he'll own the water, and whoever owns the water owns everyone who drinks. This isn't a dispute. It's a slow killing."

    Arjun stood. "I came here to stay out of other people's wars."

    "I know."

    "Then what do you want from me?"

    She met his eyes, and what was in her face was not anger or disappointment. It was recognition, the look of a person seeing another clearly and finding something familiar there.

    "I want you to decide," she said. "I've held this line four years and I'm tired. I'm not asking you to fight my war. I'm asking you to stop pretending it isn't yours. You drink from this creek. You sleep in my barn. It's your war whether you fired the first shot or not." She took the bottle and put it back in the cabinet and turned the key. "Think on it."

    He went outside.

    The sun was past its peak. The street lay mostly empty, a few horses at the rail, a dog asleep in the shade of the livery, dust devils turning lazy circles at the crossroads. Perdido looked peaceful, if a man did not know better.

    He knew better. Peace kept by looking away is not peace. It is a held breath.

    He walked the buckskin to the livery and unsaddled her himself, taking his time, because work was the one thing that had ever kept his thoughts from circling. Corby watched from his crate and for once said nothing. The mare stood patient while Arjun brushed her down and checked her feet and filled her trough. She was a good horse. She deserved better than a man who could not make up his mind what he was.

    .

    They found Miguel Gutierrez just before sundown.

    A boy came running into town, the Gutierrez boy, Tomás, ten years old, barefoot and wild-eyed and so far past his breath that for a full minute the words would not come. When they came, they came all at once. His father had gone out to check the south pasture and had not come back for supper. His mother sent Tomás looking. He found his father in the arroyo below the windmill, beaten so badly the boy did not know him at first.

    Arjun was at the store when the boy came in. He heard the shouting and went out. Father Rojas was already there, a small man in a black cassock who moved faster than his years, and he had the boy by the shoulders, speaking low and steady, the voice that teaches panicked children how to breathe again.

    "Where is he now?"

    "In the arroyo. I couldn't lift him. He's too heavy."

    "Is he alive?"

    "I think so. His face." The boy stopped there and could not go on.

    "All right," Father Rojas said. "All right." He looked up and found Arjun. "We need a wagon."

    Arjun went to the livery and hitched a team to the flatbed in under five minutes, his body doing what it knew while his mind stayed cold and still. Two more men joined them at the edge of town, Esteban Martinez and his eldest, Joaquin, a solemn boy of twenty with eyes that had already taken in too much.

    They found Gutierrez half a mile south of the windmill, at the bottom of a shallow arroyo that was dry now and would run fast and deep when the rains came. He lay on his side with one arm twisted under him and his face pressed into the dirt. Someone had worked him over with something hard. A gun butt, maybe, or a length of pipe. Ribs stove in. Jaw broken. His nose was flattened and both eyes had swollen shut, and when they lifted him he made a sound that was not quite a scream and not quite a word.

    "Easy," Arjun said. "Easy now."

    They got him into the wagon bed and covered him with a blanket. Joaquin held his head steady against the lurch of the road. Father Rojas sat beside him, praying too low to hear. Tomás rode up front with Arjun, staring at the road with his fists clenched on his knees.

    "Who did this?" the boy said.

    "I don't know."

    "You do know." His voice cracked on it. "Everybody knows."

    Arjun did not answer. The wagon creaked. The sun went down behind the mountains and the sky turned the color of a fresh bruise.

    .

    At the store, Beatriz had cleared a space on the floor and laid down blankets. She asked no questions. She saw Gutierrez's face and her own went hard and blank, and she set to boiling water and tearing cloth with the efficiency of a woman who had done this before. The nearest doctor was twenty miles off in Socorro, and the only doctor the valley had ever had of its own was two years in the ground. By the time anyone could ride to Socorro and back, Gutierrez would be past needing him one way or the other.

    "Will he live?" Tomás asked.

    Beatriz did not look up from her work. "He's strong. Your mother is stronger. We'll do what we can."

    Father Rojas stayed to help. Esteban and Joaquin rode out to bring the family in. Arjun stood in the doorway of the store, watching the street go dark, and did not move.

    Down past the livery, the forge was cold and the smith's cart sat closed and unlit. Wherever Kai was, he was not at his fire. It was the first night since the man came that the alley behind the livery held no glow at all, and Arjun found he missed it, the way you miss a lamp you never knew you steered by.

    .

    The families gathered that night in the church.

    Nobody called the meeting. People came, drawn by the same gravity that pulls water downhill and dust into the air. The Martinezes were there, Esteban with his hat in his hands and Joaquin beside him. Miriam Cohen came in from the western bench, a widow with a sharp face and a sharper tongue who ran sheep where everyone had told her sheep would not run. Iris Barkley came with her red hair tied back and her little daughter asleep on her shoulder, a hundred acres of bottomland worked alone since the snakebite took her husband, and everyone had said she could not do that either, until she did. The Pfeiffer brothers came in together and sat in their usual pew, Elijah up straight and ready to talk, Amos beside him with his cold pipe in his teeth and his ruined left hand resting on the wood, saying nothing, the way he said most things.

    And in the front pew sat Maria Gutierrez with her boys around her. She did not weep. She held herself straight and still, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman who had passed through grief before and knew the country on the other side of it.

    Father Rojas stood at the altar rail, his thin hands resting on the wood. Twenty-three years in Perdido, and his voice was the voice of a man who had learned long ago that shouting makes no one listen.

    "I am not going to tell you what to do," he said. "The Church teaches peace. It teaches the other cheek. It teaches that vengeance belongs to God." He paused. "It also teaches that a man who does not provide for his own household is worse than an unbeliever, and protection is provision. So I will not tell you to fight, and I will not tell you not to. I will say only this. Whatever you decide, decide it together. Divided, this valley is already his."

    He stepped back. For a while nobody spoke.

    Then Esteban Martinez stood, turning his hat brim through his hands. "We've been talking for two years. We talked when they cut the fence. We talked when they killed Rafael Vargas. We talked when the Hernandez cattle went. Now Miguel is lying on a store floor with his jaw broke, and we are talking." He turned to take in the room. "When does the talking stop?"

    "When somebody has a better idea." Miriam Cohen stayed seated, and her voice carried without effort. "I run sheep, Esteban. I know what it looks like when something is killing your animals one at a time and you keep telling yourself it's bad luck. You cannot bargain with a thing like that. Brandt doesn't want a deal. He wants the valley. Every time we bend, he takes the bend for a bow."

    "Then what?" Iris Barkley kept her voice low for the child on her shoulder, and the low made it harder, not softer. "We ride on his place with what? Twenty men, half of them old, the other half never shot at anything bigger than a coyote? He has hired guns. Professionals. We'd be buried by Sunday."

    "So we wait." Joaquin spoke for the first time, and his voice was steady, which was somehow worse than shaking. "We wait for him to come for us one at a time."

    Elijah Pfeiffer cleared his throat, a sound like gravel turned in a bucket. "Now, I have said for two years that if we keep our heads, this settles itself. Men like Brandt overreach. Territories get law eventually. I have said it and said it." He looked down at his brother's ruined hand on the pew, and then away. "I sat with Miguel an hour tonight. I am not going to say it anymore. But I am too old to say anything else, and that is where I find myself."

    Amos struck a match, thought better of it in church, and let it burn down between his fingers.

    Beatriz had been standing at the back, near the door. Now she spoke.

    "Miguel wasn't random." The room went still. "He spoke up at the last meeting. He said we should stand together, and he said it loudest. Brandt's men didn't pick him by accident. They picked him to carry a message, and the message is that whoever stands up next is next."

    Everyone turned to Arjun.

    He felt it come, the weight of eyes, men and women looking at him for the reassurance that somebody knew what came after. He had stood in this spot before. He had learned to hate it. Leading meant deciding, and deciding got people killed, and he had dead men enough behind him to fill a church twice this size.

    "I'm just a freight man," he said.

    "You fought in the Border Wars." Iris shifted the sleeping child higher on her shoulder and studied him across the pews, not angry, only looking, the same level measuring Beatriz had given him his first day. "A man doesn't stop being what he is because he changes his shirt."

    Arjun had no answer for that.

    The meeting broke without a decision. The families drifted out into the night in ones and twos, and their lanterns scattered down the dark roads like sparks off a wheel. Father Rojas stayed kneeling at the rail. Maria Gutierrez went back to the store to sit with her husband, and Beatriz set up a cot beside his blankets.

    Arjun walked to the barn alone.

    He stood in the dark, letting his eyes adjust. The buckskin stirred in her stall. Hay rustled. Outside, the town settled into the deep quiet that follows bad news, heavier than ordinary silence, weighted with everything said and everything not.

    He sat down on his bedroll and reached under the saddle tree for the small trunk. He had carried it out here from the back room the week he moved to the barn, the same week the freight work turned regular, and if Beatriz had wondered why a hired man would trade a cot for hay, she had kept it to herself.

    Pine and iron, scarred from three years of wagon beds and pack frames, the hasp held shut with a length of rawhide. He had carried it through eleven towns and four territories and never once cut that rawhide loose. His hands knew the knots anyway.

    He worked them free in the dark. The hinges creaked.

    Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a Colt Single Action Army, .45 caliber, seven and a half inch barrel, the bluing worn silver at the muzzle and around the cylinder from years of riding leather. Beneath it, longer, wrapped in canvas, the shape of a rifle he did not uncover. He had carried the Colt through the Border Wars, through places whose names he tried not to remember and remembered anyway.

    He lifted it out. The weight was familiar in a way that turned his stomach. His hand found the grip before he asked it to. His thumb found the hammer without being sent. His whole body settled into a posture he had spent three years unlearning, and it took no longer than a breath.

    He opened the loading gate and turned the cylinder slow, counting by feel. Five rounds, hammer resting on the empty chamber, the way Tom Harken had taught him. Always leave the hammer on the empty. That way it don't go off unless you mean it to.

    He had meant it to. More times than he wanted to count.

    Somewhere south of town, Miguel Gutierrez was breathing through a broken face on a store floor. Somewhere north, in a house Arjun had never seen, Silas Brandt was pouring himself a drink and considering what came next. And out on the flats a quarter mile of wire lay cut clean in the dirt, waiting on morning like everything else.

    Arjun turned the Colt once in his hands, the worn bluing catching what little light the barn held. Then he wrapped it in the oilcloth, laid it back in its place beside the canvas, and shut the lid, and tied the rawhide back through the hasp the way he always tied it. Six knots, hard and tight. A man would need daylight and ten minutes to get through them, and that was the point.

    The last knot drew down small in the dark, and the dark took it.

    Chapter Four

    Hammer Before Dawn

    The hammer woke him in the last dark before morning.

    Three strikes, a pause, one more. Arjun lay on his bedroll on the dirt floor of Beatriz's barn and listened to it come across the yards, thin and clear in the cold air that would be gone by sunup. The buckskin mare shifted in her stall and blew softly. The rhythm never varied. Three and one. A man could set a watch by it, if a man in Perdido had owned a watch worth setting.

    Kai at the forge before dawn. Most days now.

    Arjun rose and rolled the bedroll and went out to the well while the sky was still more stars than light. The water came up cold from deep in the ground. He drank two dippers and poured a third over his head and neck. To the east the mountains were a black edge with gray behind it. The dust of the road lay quiet, waiting for the first wagon. By noon the heat would settle on the town and sit there like a hen.

    He had freight to move and it was too dark to move it. He walked to the livery instead.

    Kai's rig stood in the open yard behind the stable, older than anything else in Perdido. The bellows were patched with rawhide. The anvil sat on a length of oak trunk banded with iron, the face of it worn bright as a coin from years of hammers. His tools hung from the crossbar of the cart in an order that never changed, tongs by their length, hammers by their weight, and a man could have found any one of them blind. Kai worked in the light of the forge with his sleeves rolled, turning a shoe on the horn with tongs, and the coals breathed with the bellows like a thing sleeping.

    A gray gelding stood tied to the corral, one of Corby's boarders, holding his off hind the way a horse holds a foot that hurts. He watched the fire without fear. Horses never feared Kai's fire, which was a thing Arjun had noticed and not remarked on.

    "You're up early," Arjun said.

    Kai did not look up from the shoe. "Horse doesn't care what hour it is. Lameness keeps its own schedule."

    Arjun leaned on the corral rail and watched. The metal glowed orange at the edges, dull red at the center. Three strikes, a pause, one more, and the curve came into the iron like it had been waiting in there.

    "You ever shoe a horse for Brandt?"

    Kai set the shoe in the water bucket. Steam came up and was gone. "Shod three of his last week. Bay gelding with the white stocking has a crack in the left fore. Another month, he throws a rider."

    "You know whose horses they are."

    "I know whose feet hurt." He pushed the next blank into the coals and worked the bellows twice, slow and deep. The fire brightened. "A horse doesn't choose his rider. Neither does a blacksmith."

    Arjun said nothing. The heat moved the air between them.

    "There's a man in this town," Kai said, "who thinks that because he stopped carrying a gun, he stopped being dangerous. He's wrong. He's still dangerous. Just in a different direction."

    "I didn't come here for a sermon."

    "No. You came here to stack crates." Two strikes, then the pause. "Nothing wrong with that. World needs crates stacked." He set the hammer down and studied Arjun for the first time. "But a man who can do one thing and chooses to do another, that's different from one who can only do it. The first is making a choice. The second is standing where he landed."

    "I know what I'm doing."

    "Do you." The dark eyes were steady over the fire. "You know the name of the last man you killed. The color of his shirt. What his hands did before he went down. You've run it back enough times to know how long he took to fall."

    Arjun's hands tightened on the rail.

    "You never counted the ones still walking around because you were where you were."

    "That's not the same thing."

    "It's the same thing. You just don't want to do the arithmetic."

    He took the blank from the fire and laid it on the anvil, and the hammer came down, and that was the end of it. Arjun stood at the rail a moment longer, then walked back toward the store through the first true light without saying anything.

    Beatriz was behind the counter with an invoice, pencil behind her ear. She didn't look up. "He get under your skin?"

    "Who."

    "The blacksmith. You've got that look."

    "What look."

    "The one men get after they talk to him. Like they walked into a doorframe they didn't see coming."

    She licked the pencil and went down the column of figures, and Arjun stood a moment in the smell of coffee and drying chiles and new leather. The store was cool yet. Through the front window the street was filling with light the color of straw.

    "Freight goes in the back room, stacked by weight," she said. "Flour on the bottom, and mind the coal oil, the caps leak. There's a load making up for the south valley inside the week. Flour and salt and a bolt of calico. You'll drive it with me."

    "That an order?"

    "It's a dollar and dinner. Same as everything else in this town." She wrote a figure and underlined it. "Go on. It won't stack itself."

    Arjun went out to the freight. The crates were heavy and the work was plain and he tried to let the lifting push everything else out. It didn't.

    The arithmetic. The trouble was he had done it, years ago, and the figures had come out wrong. He had counted the men he'd killed and stacked them against the lives they might have taken, and none of it added to anything clean. Every dead man had been somebody's reason for getting up in the morning. He had spent three years trying not to be anybody's reason for anything.

    By midday the heat had settled in hard, and he walked to the Dry Gulch for a meal. Beans and tortillas and Gus's coffee, which had not improved. He was halfway through the beans when the light in the doorway changed.

    Tom Harken stood just inside, thumbs hooked in his gun belt, set to move in any direction. Through the window Arjun could see the claybank at the rail, reins hanging, not tied. Tom was thinner than he'd been at the store that first evening, the hollows under the iron-gray mustache cut deeper, and the turned-up brim of his hat threw a hard little shadow across his eyes.

    "Brandt wants to talk to you."

    "I've got nothing to say to Brandt."

    "He's offering work. Real money. Better than hauling freight for a woman who can't pay half what you're worth."

    Arjun took a bite of tortilla and chewed it slow. "You're doing his talking for him now."

    "I'm doing what I'm paid to do."

    "That's not what I asked."

    Tom read the floor, then the wall, then came back. "He's hard. But he's square if you deal square with him. You could do worse."

    "I've done worse. That's the problem."

    Tom crossed the room and stood over the table, and his voice came down with him. "This thing is going one way. When it goes, you don't want to be standing where you're standing."

    "I'm stacking crates and sleeping in a barn."

    "You're working for Beatriz Vargas. In this valley that's a side, whether you call it one or not."

    Arjun set down the fork and looked up at him. The face above him was one he had trusted farther than his own, once, in country where trust was the whole outfit. "Why him, Tom."

    "He saved my life. You know what that means."

    "I know what you think it means."

    Something moved behind Tom's eyes and did not come out. He stood there a breath longer than he needed to. Then he walked out, and the claybank's hoofbeats went up the street and faded, and the saloon was quiet again. The beans had gone cold. Arjun ate them anyway.

    .

    That afternoon Kai was shoeing for one of the south-valley men, a nervous bay mare that kept shying from the smoke. She had come in wall-eyed and lathered at the girth, and her owner had stood by giving advice until Kai asked him, politely, to go sit in the shade. Then he worked slow, talking to her low and steady in words that weren't quite words, his hands never quick, never sudden, resting on her shoulder between one thing and the next so she always knew where he was. By the third shoe she stood still as a post with her lip loose.

    Arjun watched from the shade of the livery wall. He told himself he was passing time.

    "You and Tom Harken," Kai said, bent over the hoof. "Long time?"

    "Long enough."

    "He came to warn you."

    "He came to hire me."

    "Same thing, from where he stands." Kai clinched the last nail and set the hoof down, and ran a hand along the mare's flank. "A man who warns you is still on your side. He's just lost the map."

    "You talk like you know him."

    "I know the look. Man took the wrong job for a right reason and can't find the way back out of it." He gathered his tools and began wiping them with an oiled rag, one at a time, each to its place in the box. "You have the easier load, if you want to know. You carry what you did. He carries what he owes."

    Arjun picked a piece of scrap from the dirt, a hinge rusted through at the pin, and turned it over in his fingers. He set it on the anvil stump and did not say what he was thinking.

    Kai stood and stretched his back. "You're invited to supper."

    "Where?"

    "My camp. Beans and coffee."

    "You say that like you knew I'd be here."

    "You've been holding up that wall for half an hour."

    .

    Late in the day, before the light went long, a Brandt rider came in leading a sorrel with a thrown shoe. One of the hands who had ridden in with Tom that first dusk. He saw Arjun by the rail and his eyes narrowed, but he said nothing. He handed the lead to Kai.

    "Brandt says he's been favoring the right front."

    Kai ran his hand down the gelding's leg and lifted the hoof, the same as he did with every horse, slow, patient. The sorrel stood easy under him. The rider crossed his arms and shifted his weight, a young man, twenty-two maybe, a face that hadn't settled into what it was going to be.

    "You the Tucker man?" he said.

    "I am."

    "Tom says you're a fair hand with a gun."

    "Tom says a lot of things."

    The rider chewed on that. "You going to be trouble?"

    "I'm stacking crates and sleeping in a barn. That trouble enough for you?"

    The rider studied him a moment, then turned to Kai. "He going to be trouble?"

    Kai didn't look up from the hoof. "Son, if you have to ask a blacksmith whether a man is trouble, you're already in over your head." He set the hoof down and straightened. "Shoe will take an hour. Wait or come back."

    The rider waited. He sat on an overturned bucket where he could watch Arjun without seeming to. Kai worked the forge, and the hammer fell in its rhythm, three and one. He pulled the old nails and pared the hoof and set the new shoe hot to seat it, and the smoke of burned horn drifted across the yard, and the sorrel dozed on three legs through all of it. Nothing moved but the smoke and the long light. Twice the young man opened his mouth to say something, and twice the hammer started up again before he found it. When the shoe was on, the young man paid in silver, mounted, and rode north without another word. The whole thing had taken an hour and changed nothing, except that Arjun had watched it happen.

    "You charge them the same?" he asked when the dust had settled.

    "Same work, same price. The shoe doesn't know whose horse it is."

    "The shoe doesn't know anything."

    "That's right. The shoe does what it's made to do. It doesn't ask who's right. It doesn't worry what the horse does after." Kai raked the coals together and banked them with ash for the evening.

    "That's a convenient way to look at things."

    Kai turned the rake over, tapped it clean on the stump, and hung it on its nail.

    .

    His camp was pitched behind the forge. A canvas lean-to, a fire ring of river stones, a coffee pot burned black, a bedroll, a wooden toolbox with rope handles. Nothing else. The man traveled light and it didn't look like poverty. It looked like arithmetic of another kind.

    Arjun sat on a stone while Kai warmed the beans. He watched him work the fire the way he worked the forge, no wasted motion, the pot set just off the flame where the heat was right, and it occurred to him that he had never seen Kai hurry at anything, and never seen him late. The coffee was already going, and the smell of it mixed with woodsmoke and the first cool breath moving down off the mountains. For a while neither of them spoke. It was not an uncomfortable silence. It was the kind that comes when hard things have been said already and a man is resting before the next ones.

    "You believe in God?" Arjun asked.

    Kai stirred the beans. "Depends what you mean by God."

    "Father Rojas's kind. Church and candles."

    "I believe Father Rojas means what he says. That's not nothing." He lifted the pot off and set it on a flat stone. "But no. Not that kind."

    "What kind, then."

    Kai filled two tin plates and handed one across. "You ever watch a horse know things? It feels a man's fear before the man does. The legs, the hands, the sound of the breathing. It doesn't think about it. It knows. That's the kind I mean. The knowing that runs under the thinking."

    Arjun ate his beans. Better than Beatriz's, though he'd never say so. "You talk like a preacher."

    "I talk like a blacksmith. They're closer trades than you'd think. Both of them work with fire and broken things."

    The light went off the ridges and the stars came out sharp and close in the dry air. A coyote called down the valley and another answered it. The fire popped and settled.

    "You said this morning I was dangerous in the wrong direction," Arjun said. "You were right about the rest of it too. I've been afraid of what I am since the wars ended. Every time I look at my hands I see what they did. I figured if I stopped, stopped carrying, stopped riding with gun hands, stopped being the man who went in first, the remembering would stop too."

    "Has it?"

    "No."

    Kai nodded, unsurprised. "The weight was never in the gun. It's in the knowing. You know what you can do. The gun is a tool. The knowing is you."

    "If what I am is a killer, I'd rather be nobody."

    Kai set his plate aside and leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and the firelight came up from under his face and made the hollows deep. "A killer takes a life and it costs him nothing. He sleeps fine. He doesn't remember names, or what a man's hands were doing when he went down. You remember everything. That's not a killer. That's a fighting man who hasn't found his peace."

    "There's a difference."

    "All the difference there is. A killer destroys because he can. A fighting man protects because he must. You've spent three years trying to live like you're neither one. That's what's been eating you. Not the guilt. The lie."

    Arjun stared into the fire. Three years he had told himself a story: that setting the gun down was payment on a debt. He had never once asked who else was making the payments.

    "Beatriz's husband," he said. "If a man had been in that yard who knew how to fight."

    "Yes."

    "Gutierrez in the arroyo."

    "Yes."

    "Then three years of stacking crates and minding my own business isn't a virtue. It's a different kind of failing."

    Kai pushed at the fire with a stick and sparks went up into the dark and died there. "I knew a man once. Sat on a rock in the desert twenty years, getting pure. The farmer in the valley below him raised six children and half his neighbors' barns, and never asked anybody what he was."

    Arjun turned that over. The fire worked at a knot of juniper, hissing.

    "Where'd you get all this?" he said. "Rocks and farmers and the knowing under the thinking."

    "A man I worked for. A long time ago." Kai fed the stick to the coals. "He had it from somebody older. It's an old trade. It gets handed down, hand to hand, and every so often somebody drops it, and it waits for the next pair of hands."

    "That why you picked up the hammer?"

    "That's why I never put it down."

    The moon came up over the eastern ridge, thin and white, and laid long shadows across the yard. A door closed somewhere in town. A horse stamped once in the livery and was still.

    "You can't get out of doing," Kai said. "Standing still is doing. Keeping quiet is doing. The only question is whether what you do lines up with what you know."

    "And if I don't know what's true."

    "Pay attention until you do." Kai stood and rolled his shoulders. "I've got a horse at dawn. Get some sleep."

    Arjun rose and stood a moment by the dying fire, looking down at the coals going from orange to red to a color with no name. Then he thanked him for the supper, and Kai said nothing, and that was its own kind of answer.

    He walked back through town in the dark. Moonlight on the road, white and sharp, and the dust rising around his boots at every step and settling behind him. In the barn the buckskin swung her head over the stall door and he stood with her a while, his hand on her neck, feeling the big slow pulse under the hide. The trunk sat under the saddle tree where he had left it two nights before, closed, the rawhide tied. He knew what was in it. He knew what condition it was in. His hands had cleaned it all before he packed it, three years ago, and his hands had forgotten none of the steps.

    He lay down on the bedroll and watched the moonlight come through the gaps in the plank wall and lay its slow bars across the rafters. Below him the horses breathed, long and even. Somewhere north, Brandt's fires. Somewhere south, five families asleep behind thin doors.

    For the first time in three years he let the question ask itself, plainly, the way Kai would have asked it. If it came to it, what would he do?

    No answer came. The question lay there in the dark with him, and the moonlight moved along the rafters an inch at a time.

    Chapter Five

    South Valley

    The south valley ran twelve miles from the cutbank to the salt flats, and in that twelve miles lived five families who couldn't agree on the time of day.

    Beatriz told him this on the ride out. They took the buckboard because she had freight for the Martinez place, fifty pounds of flour, salt, three sacks of coffee, and a bolt of calico for Marta Martinez, who was expecting again. Arjun rode alongside on the buckskin mare Beatriz had lent him while the roan grew fat and lazy in the pasture behind the livery. The mare was solid on uncertain ground and asked no questions, which suited him.

    "The Martinezes have been here the longest," Beatriz said. "Esteban's father homesteaded that quarter-section before the territory was organized. Esteban has held it fifty years since. He thinks that makes him right about everything."

    "Does it?"

    "It makes him stubborn. Whether that's the same thing depends on who you ask."

    The road curved through a stand of cottonwoods, then opened onto a broad flat of grama grass and cholla. The Martinez place sat on a low rise with a view of the whole lower valley. Thick-walled adobe, small windows, a portal running the length of the front. Corrals behind. A windmill creaking in the morning heat.

    Esteban Martinez came out to meet them. Sixty or close to it, hands cracked and dark from a lifetime of sun, face that had squinted into every year of it. He didn't smile, but he didn't frown.

    In this country, that was hospitality.

    "Beatriz."

    "Esteban."

    He studied Arjun. "This the one?"

    "Arjun Tucker. He's been helping with the freight."

    Martinez took him in, slow and deliberate, the way he'd check a horse for splints. "I heard about you. Border Wars."

    Arjun said nothing.

    "Lot of talk in town about a gunfighter working for Beatriz Vargas."

    "I'm not a gunfighter."

    "What are you, then?"

    "At the moment, I'm unloading flour."

    Martinez's mouth moved in something close to amusement. He gestured toward the house. "Marta will want to see that calico. Come in when you're done."

    Arjun carried the flour in on his shoulder, two sacks at a time, and stacked it in the cool room off the kitchen where Marta Martinez directed him with a wooden spoon. She was younger than Esteban by a good margin, dark-eyed, far along, and she moved through her kitchen the way a pilot moves through a channel he has run a thousand times. Three boys of stairstep sizes watched Arjun from the doorway until she said one word in Spanish and they scattered like quail.

    When Beatriz brought in the calico, Marta wiped her hands on her apron before she touched it. She unrolled a yard of it against the window light, small blue flowers on cream, and held it there a moment without speaking.

    "For the christening dress," she said. "And curtains after, if there's enough. There's always a use for what's left."

    "There's enough," Beatriz said.

    Marta looked past the cloth, out the window, to where Esteban and Joaquin stood by the corral with their heads together, and the light went a little different in her face. "If we're still here for curtains."

    Nobody answered that. Marta rolled the calico back up, neat and tight, set it on the shelf above the flour where the boys couldn't reach it, and put the coffee on. Her hands didn't hurry and didn't stop.

    .

    The meeting was at the Pfeiffer place that evening.

    Beatriz explained the rotation on the ride over. The families met once a month, moving among the five spreads, to talk water rights and fence lines and whatever else needed talking. Since Brandt had started pushing, they'd been meeting every week.

    He had seen them all once before, in the church, the night they carried Miguel Gutierrez in from the arroyo. A church gathering shows you people's Sunday faces, even in trouble. A man's own table shows you the rest.

    The Pfeiffer cabin was railroad ties and river rock, and it showed. It smelled of woodsmoke, coffee, and old wool, and a single oil lamp hung from a rafter over a table of pine planks laid across sawhorses. The brothers ran their small herd on the eastern edge, where the grazing was thin but the water reliable, and they had divided the world between them long ago: Elijah did the talking for both, which meant not much talking at all, and Amos did the considering, his ruined left hand curled around his pipe like it had been made for nothing else.

    Esteban Martinez had brought Joaquin, and here on another man's ground the boy sat where he could watch the door, which told Arjun more about the last two years in this valley than the meeting would. Miriam Cohen had come down off the western bench with David, and out from behind her sharpness, at a neighbor's table, you could see what the sharpness was for: she was the only one of them keeping accounts out loud, fence cut by fence cut, because her husband Aaron had been the doctor here, the only one in sixty miles, and since the fever took him she had appointed herself the valley's memory instead.

    Iris Barkley had driven over from her bottomland with Etta asleep in the wagon bed, and had checked on her twice before the talk even started. Father Rojas sat at the end of the table with his hands folded, come to speak for the parish families on the southern edge, the Gutierrezes, the Riveras, the Moraleses. He said little. He watched much.

    Beatriz wasn't a family, but no one questioned her place. She'd earned it.

    Arjun sat near the door. He hadn't been invited to speak. He hadn't been invited at all.

    .

    "The fence," Esteban Martinez began, because Esteban Martinez always began. "Two hundred yards of it, cut clean. Posts pulled. That's the third time."

    "Fourth," said Miriam Cohen. "They cut my east line last month. You said it was probably coyotes."

    "There were no coyote tracks."

    "I know there were no coyote tracks. I'm a shepherd. I know what coyote sign looks like."

    "Then why didn't you say something?"

    "I did say something. You said, and I quote, 'Miriam, you worry too much.'"

    Esteban's jaw tightened. Joaquin found something to study in the table planks.

    Elijah Pfeiffer cleared his throat. Gravel in a bucket. "Point is, it's Brandt. We all know it's Brandt. Question is what we're gonna do about it."

    "Nothing."

    Everyone turned.

    Iris Barkley had spoken. Her voice was soft but it carried across the room like a nail going into pine.

    "We do nothing. That's what we've been doing. That's what's working so well."

    "Now, Iris."

    "Don't 'now, Iris' me, Esteban. My husband's been dead two years and I've had Brandt's men ride through my east pasture twice this month. They say they're looking for strays. There are no strays. They're counting my cattle. They're seeing how much I've got and how hard it'd be to take it."

    David Cohen leaned forward. "My mother's right. They cut our line too. And Gutierrez. That wasn't a warning. That was Brandt saying there's no line he won't cross."

    "Gutierrez was a hard thing," Esteban said. "But it was one thing."

    "He was beaten half to death on his own land," Miriam said. "While we sat here talking about fence lines."

    The room went quiet.

    The lamp flickered. Outside, a coyote called and another answered.

    Esteban spread his hands on the table. "What do you want me to do, Miriam? Ride up to the Brandt place with a shotgun and my four boys? He's got thirty men on that payroll, and a dozen of them are guns out of Laredo and El Paso. Men who'll kill for a dollar and sleep fine after."

    "So we do nothing."

    "I didn't say nothing. I said we need to be smart."

    "Smart got Gutierrez beaten," Iris said. "Smart got my husband buried."

    No one spoke. Father Rojas shifted in his chair and folded his hands the other way. Amos Pfeiffer took out a pipe and began filling it, the three-fingered hand moving with the easy economy of a man who'd made his peace with the loss.

    Esteban turned to Beatriz. "You've been quiet."

    "I'm listening."

    "You always listen. Then you do what you were going to do anyway."

    "That's because I usually know before I sit down."

    "And what do you know now?"

    Beatriz looked around the table. "I know Esteban wants to negotiate. I know Miriam wants to fight. I know Iris wants someone else to make the decision so she doesn't have to. Elijah will go along with whatever Esteban decides. And Amos thinks we're all talking too much."

    Amos Pfeiffer lit his pipe. The corner of his mouth moved.

    "And you, Father?" Beatriz asked.

    Father Rojas unfolded his hands. "There are twenty-three families who look to this table for guidance. They are not here tonight, but they will live with what is decided. I would ask you to remember that." He paused. "I would also ask you to remember that Silas Brandt does not negotiate. He dictates. The difference matters."

    "He's offered to buy my water rights three times," Beatriz said. "He calls it a cooperative arrangement. He says it the way another man says checkmate."

    Miriam laughed, short, humorless. "We could leave. All of us. Pack up and go."

    "Where?" Esteban demanded. "This is our land. My father is buried here. My sons were born here. You want me to hand that to a man like Brandt?"

    "I don't want you to. I'm saying we have to name the choices before we can pick one. Fight, negotiate, or leave. There isn't a fourth."

    "Sure there is," Elijah said. "Die where we stand."

    The silence after that had weight.

    .

    Arjun had been sitting so still that most of them seemed to have forgotten he was there. When he shifted to ease a cramp in his leg, Miriam Cohen's eyes found him.

    "You fought in the Border Wars."

    "Yes."

    "Were you any good?"

    "Good enough to still be here."

    "You've seen men like Brandt before."

    "I've been men like Brandt before."

    The room went still. Esteban's jaw worked. Even Amos stopped puffing.

    "Then if you were us," Miriam said, quieter now, "what would you do?"

    Arjun met her eyes, then took in the others, the lamplight on their faces, the hands on the table, work-cracked, folded, waiting.

    "I'm not you," he said.

    He let it stand. Miriam waited for more, and when more didn't come, something in her face closed up, not angry, just done. She turned back to the table.

    "Well," Elijah said. "There's a man knows how to end a conversation."

    Nobody laughed, but the room eased a little, the way a horse eases when the strange dog goes back under the porch. The talk moved on to the delegation Esteban wanted to send, and who would go, and what they could offer that Brandt didn't already mean to take.

    .

    After the meeting broke up, the families stood in small clusters in the Pfeiffer yard, talking in low voices that weren't quite private. Nothing had been decided. That was what these meetings did. Named the problem, turned it over, set it back down.

    Beatriz stood talking with Esteban near the buckboard, their voices low and serious. Arjun leaned against the corral fence and watched the horses. The Pfeiffers kept two fires going in the yard, a hundred yards apart, close enough to shout, far enough to breathe, and Amos had already drifted out to the far one with his pipe. The smoke rose straight in the still air, two thin gray lines against the stars.

    Iris Barkley stopped beside him. She was smaller than he'd realized, barely reaching his shoulder. Her face in the lamplight was tired but not defeated.

    "You didn't answer her question," she said. "Miriam's question. What you'd do."

    "No."

    "Why not?"

    He was quiet a moment. Inside the cabin someone was stacking tin plates, and a horse in the corral shook itself, harness rings chiming soft.

    "What I'd do doesn't signify," he said. "I've got nothing here. No land. No family. No stake. A man with nothing to lose gives bad advice."

    She studied him. "You think that's true?"

    "I know it's true."

    "Then what are you doing here?"

    He didn't answer. She waited a moment longer, then nodded, something private in it, and walked to her wagon. Etta was asleep in the back, wrapped in a quilt, her small face peaceful in a way none of the grown faces had been for months.

    .

    Arjun rode back to town alone. Beatriz had stayed to talk with Esteban, and he'd declined the offer of a bed at the Martinez place. He wanted the ride. He wanted the dark.

    The road ran along the base of the ridge, and the ridge rose black against a sky packed wall to wall with stars, more stars than dark between them. A night bird called somewhere in the chaparral. The mare moved at an easy walk and Arjun let his mind go.

    They were good people. That was the problem. Good people want to believe the world is fair. They want to believe that if they follow the rules, the rules will protect them. They'd built their lives on that, fences, deeds, water rights, things that existed because other people agreed they did. But Silas Brandt didn't agree. Silas Brandt understood that all of it was just paper.

    And paper burned.

    Arjun knew this because he'd carried the match. Not here. Not in this valley. In another valley, another territory, in the war years, riding with men who took what they wanted because they could. He'd told himself it was war. He'd told himself a lot of things. None of them survived the first sober night after it ended, lying awake, counting faces.

    Tom Harken had been with him then. And now Tom was riding for Brandt, and Arjun was hauling freight for a woman the Brandt outfit had already widowed.

    The mare snorted. Arjun looked up.

    A figure stood in the road ahead. Dark against the starlight. Still as a post.

    Arjun's hand moved toward the revolver he wasn't carrying.

    "Easy," Kai said. "It's only me."

    Arjun reined in. "What are you doing out here?"

    "Walking." Kai fell into step beside the mare, easy stride, a man with another fifty miles in him if it came to that. "How was the meeting?"

    "How do you know there was a meeting?"

    "I know a lot of things. How was it?"

    "About what you'd expect."

    "Argued. Nobody led. Nothing decided. You sat by the door and watched."

    "I'm not part of their meeting."

    "No. You're just the man who knows what they should do and won't tell them."

    Arjun pulled the mare up. "Did Beatriz tell you to talk to me?"

    "No. I was walking."

    "In the dark. In the middle of the road."

    "The road is convenient. The dark doesn't bother me." Kai stood at the mare's shoulder, and she dropped her nose toward him as if they were old acquaintance. "You're waiting for permission. Someone to tell you it's right. No one can give you that. Not Beatriz. Not the families. Not me. Only the man who has to do the thing can decide if it's right."

    "And if I decide it isn't?"

    "Then you go back to drifting, and the question rides with you. It's lighter company than you'd think. For a while."

    The mare shifted. Night wind moved through the sage.

    "The families aren't fighters," Arjun said.

    "No. That's why they need one."

    "They need a leader."

    "They need someone to show them how to survive. There's a difference. You've spent your whole life learning it. That's not a curse. It's a skill. Skills are made to be used."

    Arjun looked down at the blacksmith standing in the dark road.

    "You really believe that."

    "I believe a man who's spent three years running from what he was has finally run far enough." Kai turned toward the sage. "The question isn't whether you should act. The question is whether you can live with yourself if you don't."

    He walked off the road into the dark. Arjun sat the mare and listened to his steps fade, and then there was only the wind, and the night bird, and the long road home.

    .

    The next morning he was in the barn before dawn.

    The trunk sat under the saddle tree where it had sat since he moved his bedroll out from the back room. Pine boards, iron straps, a hasp held shut with a length of rawhide, and the rawhide tied hard and tight, the way he'd tied it the night he opened the lid and looked and closed it again. Six knots. A man would need daylight and ten minutes and both hands to get through them.

    He knelt in the straw and worked the knots loose one by one.

    He did not lift the lid. He knew what was in there. He'd known every night for three years, the weight of it in the wagon bed, the corner of it against his boot heel in the dark, the shape of it under his hand when he shifted it, and knowing was not the same as looking, and looking was not the same as taking. The lid stayed down.

    He ran the rawhide back through the hasp and tied it in a single loop. Loose. The kind of knot a man could undo in the dark, one pull, no thought required.

    He sat back on his heels and considered what he'd done.

    "Thought I'd find you here."

    He turned. Beatriz stood in the barn doorway, silhouetted against the gray light of early morning.

    "You're back early," he said.

    "Left before first light. Esteban talks in circles and the circles don't get smaller." She came toward him, boots scuffing in the straw, and her eyes went to the trunk, to the rawhide, to the single loose loop where six hard knots had been. She said nothing about it. "He wants to send a delegation to Brandt. Offer terms."

    "What terms?"

    "Whatever Brandt will accept. Which is nothing. Esteban knows that. They all know that."

    "But they have to try."

    "They have to feel like they tried. So when it comes to the other thing, they can tell themselves it was the last resort." The light was growing, filling the barn with pale gold that caught the dust floating in the air. A horse stamped in its stall. Somewhere in town, a rooster crowed. "Something happened to you since yesterday."

    "Maybe."

    "What?"

    He rose and stood a moment with his hand resting on the trunk lid, not pressing, just resting.

    "I met a man on the road last night. He asked me a question I've been three years not answering."

    "And the answer?"

    "I don't know yet. But I've quit hiding from the question."

    Something in her face eased. Not relief, exactly. More like a woman who'd been holding a door shut alone for four years and had just felt a shoulder come up against the other side.

    "Breakfast," she said. "Eggs and coffee at the store. Don't make me eat alone."

    She turned and walked out into the morning.

    Arjun stood a moment longer. The trunk sat in the corner shadow, plain pine, iron straps, one loose loop of rawhide a man could undo in the dark.

    He followed her out into the early light, and the town was waking around them, and for the first time since the desert he walked into a morning that had a place already set for him in it.

    Chapter Six

    Before First Light

    He was up before the town was, saddling the buckskin mare in the dark by feel, the way a man does a thing he has done ten thousand times. The barn smelled of hay and horse and old leather. In the corner the trunk sat where it always sat, the rawhide tied in its one loose loop, and he did not go near it.

    Beatriz had asked him the night before to ride the east line, where the last cutting had scattered a dozen head up into the broken country toward the cedar breaks. Somebody had to count what was gone and see what could be pushed back down. He had said he would go early. What he had not said was that he wanted the riding more than the errand. Three weeks in Perdido, and the town had begun to lean on him the way a crowd leans, everybody watching to see what the new man would do. A man could not hear himself think in all that watching.

    Out here there was nothing watching but the country, and the country did not care.

    The mare moved out willing under him, sure-footed in the dark, picking her way where he could see nothing to pick. Beatriz had told him she was solid on uncertain ground, and it was so. He gave her her head on the rough stretches and held her to a walk on the good ones, and by the time the east showed gray he was five miles out along the base of the ridges, the sage going from black to silver around him, the air cold enough to burn a little going down.

    This hour had been his since the wars. Not the sleeping hours, when the arroyo came back to him whether he asked it to or not, and not the working hours, when his hands were full. This one. The hour when the world had not decided yet what kind of day it would be. He rode it slow and he kept his mind where the mare's feet were, and when it wandered south toward the valley and the question waiting there, he brought it back the way you bring back a green horse, without anger, as many times as it took. Tom Harken had taught him that, though Tom had been talking about watching a back trail. Hold your mind like you hold a horse. Not tight. Just held.

    The sun cleared the ridges and the shadows ran out long and thin ahead of him. He found the first of the strays in a pocket of grama below the breaks, seven head, drifted but not wild, and marked where they were. He was working up toward the next pocket when the mare's ears went forward and stayed there.

    Arjun stopped her and sat still.

    The rider came down out of the cedar breaks a quarter mile off, letting his horse choose the footing through the rock. Arjun knew the claybank before he knew the man, and then he knew the way the man sat it, and then the hat, brim turned up on one side where a bullet had clipped it at Resaca de la Palma and Tom had never had it blocked because, he said, a man ought to keep what the world gives him free of charge.

    Tom came on at a walk and stopped ten feet off. No hail, no hand raised. Up close he looked the way he had looked in the store that first evening and in the saloon after, gray coming up through the mustache, the eyes the color of creek water and about as easy to read.

    "You didn't leave," Tom said.

    "You knew I wouldn't."

    "I knew." Tom looked past him, down the long slope toward the river. "I hoped anyway. A man can know a thing and hope against it at the same time. Most of what I've done the last few years, that's how I did it."

    The claybank stamped. Somewhere up in the breaks a canyon wren spilled its little falling song and quit.

    "You following me?" Arjun said.

    "I was riding the same country you were riding. Brandt runs cattle up through here too. Or says he does." Tom's mouth moved under the mustache, not quite a smile. "Mostly I was riding. Same as you."

    "Same as me."

    "Twice now I've talked to you with other men listening," Tom said. "Once with that storekeeper woman looking at me like I'd tracked something in on my boots, and once across a saloon table with Gus polishing the same glass for a quarter hour so he could hear. I figured the country owed us one conversation without an audience."

    "All right," Arjun said. "Talk."

    But Tom didn't, not right away. He sat his horse and studied the valley below them, the thread of the river, the green line of cottonwoods, the brown squares of the south fields laid out small and neat as a woman's garden. Arjun let him look. He had learned patience from this man too, on stands where they had waited out a whole day for one rider to show at a water hole, and it had a bitter taste now, using a man's own teaching to wait him out.

    "You remember the Johnson place," Tom said.

    It wasn't a question, and it went through Arjun like cold water. The burned ranch house on the Mexican side. Five men dead in the yard and a sixth who had crawled for the well and not made it. They had come up on it two hours too late for anything but shovels, and then they had tracked the men who did it for six days through country that did not want to be tracked, and found them in a box canyon on the seventh, camped easy, sure that nobody was coming. Tom had killed three before they got off the ground. Arjun had killed the other two.

    "I remember."

    "We were right then," Tom said. "Those men needed killing, and we killed them, and I've slept fine on it every night since. That's the truth and I don't apologize for it." He turned his head and looked at Arjun straight. "Is it the same here? That's the thing I keep turning over. You've been in that town three weeks. You've heard their side of it. Is this the Johnson place, or is it just two outfits crowding one water and everybody telling the story that flatters them?"

    "I don't know yet," Arjun said. "I'm trying to figure that out. But I'll tell you what I've seen. I've seen a fence cut three times in a month. I've seen a farmer beaten so bad he may not walk right again, a man whose whole crime was digging a ditch off his own deeded water. Nobody's crowding Brandt. Brandt has more grass than he has cows to eat it. What he wants is for nothing south of that river to belong to anybody but him."

    "He'd tell it different."

    "He'd tell it smiling. I've met him." Arjun let a beat go by. "You asked me a straight question. Ask yourself the same one. You were there for the Johnson place. Which side of that yard does this valley put you on?"

    Tom's jaw set and his eyes went away to the mountains, and for a while the wind moving in the sage was the only voice going.

    "In the saloon you told me he saved your life," Arjun said. "You said that was all I needed to know. It isn't. I rode with you four years, Tom. I never heard that story once."

    "No. You wouldn't have." Tom took off his hat, turned it in his hands, put it back on. "It was before I knew you. Before the wars. I was tracking rustlers out of El Paso, three days on their sign, and finally cornered them in a box canyon." He gave a short laugh with nothing in it. "Except I was the one who was cornered. Six of them, one of me, no way out and no way back. I was twenty years old and I was going to die in that canyon, and I knew it the way you know rain is coming. And then Silas Brandt rode in."

    Arjun sat still.

    "He wasn't then what he is now. He was just a man with more money than most and a name for being hard but fair. He had no reason on earth to buy into my trouble. I was nobody, a kid with a fast gun and not much sense. He rode in anyway. Killed two of those rustlers himself, out of the saddle at forty yards, and drove the rest off, and carried me out of that canyon across his own horse. Paid the doctor out of his own pocket. Sat by the bed two days until the fever broke. And then he rode on and never asked me for one thing. Not one." Tom looked down at his hands on the horn. "You don't forget a thing like that. You can't."

    "No," Arjun said. "You can't."

    And there was a thing that came to him then, whole and ready, the way a shape comes out of iron under the hammer. You don't owe a man your soul for saving your body. It was true. It was maybe the truest thing he had to offer, and Tom sat ten feet away with his hands quiet on the horn, and Arjun did not say it. A true thing said too soon just teaches a man how to argue against it. He put it away where he kept the other things he was not done with, and he waited.

    "Say something," Tom said.

    "You didn't ride down out of those breaks to hear me talk."

    "No." The almost-smile again. "I guess I didn't. You never did spend words like they were free. That's the one thing I taught you that you learned past me." He shifted in the saddle, and the leather creaked, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped. "You remember that night outside Matamoros. After the fight at the river."

    "I remember."

    "You carried me three miles with a hole in my shoulder, and we sat against that old woman's wall all night waiting to see who'd come up the road, and I talked. Lord, I talked. Things I never told a living soul before or since." Tom was looking at him now with the creek-water eyes wide open for once, nothing held back of them. "You're the only man I ever trusted with my back. The only one. I want you to know that's still so. Whatever this valley comes to. That's still so."

    "Then don't be on the other side of it."

    "It's not that simple."

    "It never is," Arjun said. "That's how men like Brandt make their living. Keeping it not that simple."

    Tom looked away. The hawk that had been working the ridgeline came over them high and slid down the wind toward the river, and both men watched it go the way men watch a thing so as not to watch each other.

    "There's something else," Tom said.

    Arjun waited.

    "The Vargas woman. The one you're working for." Tom's thumb moved on the saddle horn, back and forth, a small dry sound. "She tell you about her husband?"

    "Some of it. Enough."

    "Four years back, that was. I'd been in this country maybe a week. Wasn't riding for Brandt yet, just passing through, taking his measure like everybody does." He stopped. The thumb went still. "The night it happened, I was in that saloon. In town. And there was talk that evening, the kind of talk you hear in a saloon, and I."

    He stopped again. The wren started up in the breaks and fell down its scale and quit, and the silence after it was longer than the song.

    "Tom."

    "It doesn't matter." Tom's voice had gone flat, a door closing. "It's an old story and it ends the same no matter who tells it." He gathered the claybank and half turned it, and then held it there, quartered away, not going. "You should leave. That's what I came down here to say. Not for Brandt. For me. Take that storekeeper's wages through the end of the week and get your outfit and ride out of this valley, north or east, I don't care which, and don't stop at anything that has a name on a map. Because this thing is coming whether you stand in it or not, and if you stand in it, then one morning it's going to be you and me across a piece of ground, and I have thought about that morning every night for three weeks, and I can't find my way out the other side of it."

    "There's a way out of it."

    "Not for me." Tom said it without heat, the way a man says the river is high or the pass is closed. "You've got three years of walking in you that says a man can put a thing down. Maybe you even believe it. I'm forty-one years old, Arjun. I am what I've spent. There's no walking back up my trail. There's just riding for the brand I ride for and keeping the one promise I've got left in me, which is that whatever happens down there by that river, it won't be my hand. Not against you."

    "And if he sends you?"

    "Then I'll find out what I am. Same as you." He touched the claybank with his heel and brought it the rest of the way around. "Leave, Arjun. It's good advice. It's the last I've got."

    "You know I won't."

    "I know." Tom looked at him a moment longer, and whatever was in his face had gone somewhere Arjun couldn't follow, closed over like water over a dropped stone. "That's the hell of it."

    He rode back up toward the breaks the way he had come, letting the horse pick its own line through the rock, his back straight, his hat with its turned-up brim taking the morning sun. He did not look around. Arjun sat the mare and watched him climb, smaller and darker against the pale rock, until the cedars took him, and then there was just the country, and the wind in it.

    The mare shifted under him and blew.

    "All right," he told her, and turned her down the slope toward the pocket where the rest of the strays would be.

    He worked the broken country the rest of the morning and found eleven head in all and pushed them down onto the flat where the going was easy, and the work was good the way plain work is good, leaving no room in the hands for anything else. But riding home in the afternoon with the sun coming slantwise off the ridges, the other thing came back and rode with him, and he let it, because he had learned that too. You could keep a thing out of your hands. You could not keep it out of your saddlebags.

    Tom had been in town the night Rafael Vargas died and had started to say what that meant and had not been able to finish it. That was the shape of what had happened on that slope, and Arjun turned it over the whole way down the valley. Not the fact itself. The fact was half told and could wait for its whole telling. What he turned over was the trying. Six of them, one of me, that story had come out of Tom smooth as a rifle from a scabbard, every word worn round from handling. But the other one had broken off in his mouth twice, and then the man had reached for the oldest words in the drifter's book, you should leave, the way a man grabs a doorframe going down.

    A debt a man can name is a debt he can carry. It was the ones they couldn't say out loud that broke them.

    The town came up out of its own dust in the last of the light, lamps taking hold one window at a time. Beatriz was on the porch of the store with her arms folded, watching the road, and she did not wave, she was not the waving kind, but something in the set of her shoulders came down an inch when the mare turned in at the rail.

    "Eleven head up in the breaks," he said. "I pushed them down to the flat. Fence crew could turn them in an hour."

    "That's nine more than I figured to see again." She was reading his face while he said it, the way she read a bill of freight, fast and thorough. "You're back late for eleven head."

    "I met a man out there."

    "Whose man?"

    "That's what he's trying to work out."

    She held his eyes a moment. Whatever she found there, she let it be, and that was a kind of payment too. "Supper's cold," she said. "Stove isn't. Come in when you've seen to your horse."

    He put the mare up in the dark barn, and grained her, and rubbed her down slow with a sack while she ate, taking his time about it, and the sounds were the sounds of any barn on any night, the grain in the pan, the horse's breathing, the wind trying the boards. In the corner the trunk kept its own counsel. He passed it going out, close enough to touch, and what he carried out of the barn with him instead was smaller and heavier than anything in it: one true sentence, held back on a hillside, waiting like a round kept under the hammer for the day it would be needed.

    Chapter Seven

    The Forge

    The forge sat behind the livery, a lean-to of weathered posts and a tin roof that sang when the wind kicked up. Kai had built it in two days when he first came to town, and nobody had questioned it. A blacksmith belonged wherever he set his anvil. Men needed shoes on their horses and iron on their wheels, and they did not ask where a man came from if he could turn a shoe and temper a blade.

    Arjun found him there at first light, the fire already breathing in the stone hearth. Kai worked the bellows with one hand and fed coal with the other, each movement placed without waste.

    "You're early," Kai said without turning.

    "Couldn't sleep."

    "The ground again?"

    "The ground was fine."

    Kai lifted a piece of scrap iron from the pile and laid it in the coals. He said nothing more. That was his way. He let silence do the asking, and sooner or later the silence got its answer.

    Arjun leaned against a post and watched the iron change color in the fire. Dull gray at first, then a blush of red, then a deep orange with a pulse in it, like something waking. He had seen metal heated a thousand times. He had never paid attention before.

    "The wire's down again on Beatriz's south line," he said. "She's running out of wire and running out of patience."

    "And you?"

    "I'm running out of reasons not to do something about it."

    Kai drew the iron from the coals with long-handled tongs and laid it across the anvil. It glowed the color of sunset. He took up a hammer that looked older than the town and brought it down once, twice, a third time, each strike placed, unhurried. The iron flattened. The sound rang across the empty street and faded into the desert quiet.

    "You ever work metal?"

    "No."

    "Then pick up the small hammer. The one with the short handle. When I tap, you strike."

    Arjun stepped in under the tin. The heat came at him full and immediate, pressing against his face and chest. Kai touched the iron with a light blow and Arjun brought the hammer down where the tap had been. The shock traveled up his arm and into his shoulder. He had fired rifles since he was twelve years old, driven posts, swung an axe. This was different.

    The iron fought back.

    "Harder," Kai said. "It can't hurt you. It's iron."

    Arjun struck again. The iron gave another fraction. Kai turned it with the tongs and tapped, and Arjun struck where he pointed. They found a rhythm without speaking about it. Two men who had both done hard labor knew how. Tap. Strike. Turn. Strike. The iron spread and lengthened under the blows.

    "Metal has a memory," Kai said over the ringing. "Heat it and it forgets what it was. Work it and it remembers what you made it. But you can't make it into what it can't be. Strike all day. Iron won't turn to gold."

    He plunged the piece into the slack tub. Steam hissed and rose. When he lifted it out, the metal was dark and hard and had the rough shape of a hinge strap. He laid it aside.

    "That's the first thing. The iron doesn't care what you want it to be. It cares what it can be. Most men spend their lives striking iron and expecting gold."

    Arjun wiped sweat from his forehead. "What's the second thing?"

    Kai reached into the scrap pile and came up with a strip of spring steel that had been part of a wagon seat before the wagon broke and the seat was scavenged.

    "Knowing when to strike. Heat too long, the metal burns. Strike too soon, it shatters. Strike too late, it fights you, and you ruin the work and your arm both."

    He held the strip in the coals and watched it change. Arjun watched too. The steel darkened before it brightened, passing through its own shadow on the way to the fire.

    "How do you know when it's ready?"

    "You watch. You wait. There's a color that means now. A color that means not yet. A color that means you missed your chance." He glanced over. "Most men miss it."

    The strip reached the color Kai was waiting for. He pulled it and began to shape it, working alone now, his hammer moving in a pattern Arjun could not follow but understood was right. Effortless because every movement had been earned.

    "You're making a blade," Arjun said.

    "A tool."

    "Same thing, depending on how you use it."

    Kai said nothing. The hammer kept falling.

    .

    When the sun was high and the forge was unbearable, they stopped. Kai hung his hammer on a hook. Arjun's arms ached in places he had forgotten he had muscles. They sat in the shade of the livery wall and shared a canteen of water that was warm but wet, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

    A rider came down the main street, moving fast for the heat of the day. A south-valley man, young, one of the Riveras from the parish families on the southern edge. He pulled up in front of the livery with his horse lathered and blowing hard.

    "Mr. Tucker."

    Arjun stood. "What happened?"

    "They killed another cow. A Martinez cow, left on the Brandt road where everybody could see it. Cut its throat, dragged it through the dust, left it there like a message. Joaquin went out this morning and found it. He's still out there. He won't touch it. Nobody will."

    Arjun turned toward the north valley. The heat shimmered above the road and made the distance waver. Nothing but sage and rock and the dark line of the arroyo cutting across the flat.

    "Anybody see who did it?"

    "They don't leave witnesses."

    "Then it's words. Just words made of dead cattle."

    "Cattle that belong to people I eat with," Rivera said. "People with children and not enough money to lose another head. Words don't fill a pot."

    Kai had not moved from the shade. He watched the conversation the way he watched metal in the fire, patient and still, waiting for something to reach the right color.

    "Tell Joaquin to bury it," Arjun said. "Dig a deep hole off the road where nobody has to see it. If Brandt wants to send a message, the message was sent. Letting it rot in the sun doesn't help anybody."

    "And then what?"

    "Then nothing. Not today."

    Rivera studied him, something between disappointment and relief working in his face. He was maybe twenty, with the thin frame of a man who had never gotten enough to eat growing up. His hands on the reins were shaking.

    "My uncle says you were in the Border Wars. Says you were the fastest."

    "Your uncle remembers what he wants to remember."

    "He says a man like you doesn't have to take this. He could end it."

    Arjun walked to the horse and put his hand on the animal's neck. Still heaving. He could feel the pulse hammering under the skin.

    "Your horse is about to drop. Walk him out until he cools. Give him water slow. Too much too fast and you'll kill him, and you'll walk home."

    He went back to the shade. Rivera held his eyes a beat too long, then pulled the horse around and rode for the trough behind the livery.

    Kai was still watching. Still silent.

    "Say it," Arjun said.

    "I wasn't going to say anything."

    "You were thinking it."

    "Thinking isn't talking. If it were, every man in this town would be shouting all the time." Kai took the canteen, drank, handed it back. "The boy was right about one thing. You could end it. Faster than anyone expects, including you. But you won't."

    "Why do you think that is?"

    "Because you're still afraid you'd enjoy it."

    Arjun sat with that. The heat pressed down. Under the smell of coal smoke he could feel his own pulse in the arm that had swung the hammer all morning.

    "You're right," he said finally. "I would enjoy it. That's the problem."

    "There are worse problems."

    "Name one."

    "Being the kind of man who wouldn't enjoy it and wouldn't care either way. You're not that. You never were."

    .

    The afternoon settled over Perdido, heavy and still. The street emptied. Nobody moved in that kind of heat if they didn't have to. A dog lay in the strip of shade under the mercantile porch and did not lift its head when a wagon passed. Kai went back to the forge and Arjun went back to the shade and they didn't speak again for hours. The ring of the hammer kept its own time, and Arjun found he could tell from the sound alone when the iron was ready and when it wasn't.

    That evening Beatriz came to the livery with a pot of beans and a loaf of bread wrapped in cloth. She set it on the bench outside the forge and stood taking in the pile of scrap and the anvil and the cooling fire.

    "I heard about the cow," she said.

    "Everybody's heard about the cow."

    "A man came into the store this afternoon. One of Brandt's hands. He bought tobacco and paid with a silver dollar and smiled at me the whole time like we were old friends. He didn't say a word about the cow. He didn't have to."

    Arjun ate a spoonful of beans. They were good. Beatriz had a gift with beans, something patient built into the seasoning that didn't come from a recipe.

    "Rivera said I could end it."

    "Rivera is twenty years old and his uncle filled his head with stories. You're not a story."

    "No."

    "You're a man who showed up in my town with a tired horse and a canteen and didn't ask for anything except work. That's enough. That's more than most men bring."

    She sat on the bench beside him. The light was going out of the sky, the brief desert dusk that came fast and left fast. The mountains to the east were already dark.

    "My husband used to say a good man wasn't the one who never got scared. A good man was the one who got scared and did the thing anyway." She turned to him. "What are you scared of?"

    "Becoming what I used to be."

    "Were you so terrible?"

    "No. That's what scares me. I wasn't terrible. I was good at it. It felt like work. Like shoeing a horse or digging a post hole. You do the thing and you go home and you sleep fine and the next day you do it again." He set the spoon down. "I don't want to sleep fine after killing a man. Not anymore."

    Beatriz was quiet for a time. The first stars were coming out, the bright ones that always came first, low on the horizon, hard and white.

    "Maybe that's the difference," she said. "Maybe the man who doesn't sleep fine is exactly the man who should do it."

    She stood, took the empty pot, and walked back toward the store. Her footsteps were the only sound in the street until they faded and the desert silence came back, full and complete.

    .

    In the morning Arjun went back to the forge. Kai was already there, the fire already burning. A new piece of steel lay on the anvil, longer and thicker than the strip he had worked the day before.

    "You're going to make a knife," Kai said.

    "I didn't say that."

    "You didn't have to."

    He handed Arjun the tongs. Heavier than they looked, the handles worn smooth by years of other hands. Arjun took them and stood before the fire and waited.

    "The blade is already in the steel," Kai said. "You're not making anything. You're removing everything that isn't a blade."

    He showed Arjun how to read the color of heated steel. Not the surface color, which lied, but the color underneath, when the metal was ready to yield. Cherry red was too cold. Bright orange was right for forging. Yellow was too hot, the carbon burning out, the strength gone before you could work with what remained.

    "Work it at yellow, it'll hold an edge an hour and break. Work it at cherry, a day and bend. Work it at orange." He tapped the anvil once. "Lifetime."

    Arjun heated the steel and brought it to the anvil. His first strikes were clumsy. He missed the center, hit the edge, and the steel twisted in a way he didn't intend. Kai didn't correct him. He let him feel the mistake in his hands, let the wrong shape tell him what he'd done wrong.

    "You're fighting it," Kai said after a while. "The steel knows what it wants to be. You don't force the shape. You find it."

    Arjun tried again. He heated the steel to orange and laid it down and struck with less force and more attention. The hammer felt different when he wasn't trying to beat the metal into submission.

    It felt like a conversation.

    The steel pushed back, but it also listened.

    By midday he had something with a blade's rough proportions. One edge thicker than the other. The point uneven. The tang short by half an inch. But it was a blade. He had made a blade.

    "That's not a knife," Kai said. "That's the memory of a knife. Steel trying to remember something it used to be. But it's closer than you were yesterday."

    He took the steel from Arjun and heated it again and worked it himself, each strike exactly placed, and the blade that had been clumsy and uncertain began to sharpen and lengthen until it had the clean authority of a thing made right.

    "Tomorrow you'll do better. Tomorrow you'll remember more."

    He quenched the blade and set it aside and picked up a shoe that needed finishing, and for the next hour the forge was nothing but a forge and Kai was nothing but a blacksmith, and the smoke rose into the desert sky and vanished.

    .

    The news came that evening with another rider. Elijah Pfeiffer, the younger of the two brothers who ran cattle on the eastern edge of the south valley, seventy years old and riding a mule because he said horses were too proud for honest work. His voice was gravel in a bucket and it carried through the open door of the store before he was off the mule.

    "They burned Iris Barkley's hay. Twenty bales stored behind her barn. Twenty bales her horses and the milk cow were counting on to get through the winter."

    Arjun was at the counter when Elijah came in. Beatriz was measuring flour for Miriam Cohen, who stood with her arms crossed and her sharp face getting sharper as she listened.

    "How do you know it was Brandt?" Beatriz asked.

    "Same as the cow. Same as the wire. Nobody saw nothing, nobody heard nothing. But I been in this valley sixty years, and before Brandt came out here from the East we didn't have fires and we didn't have dead cattle laid out on the road. You know and I know and everybody knows. Knowing ain't the same as proving."

    "Twenty bales don't strike their own match," Miriam Cohen said. She took her flour and went out, and the door swung shut on the quiet store.

    "Iris can't buy hay," Beatriz said. "She's been trading eggs for flour and sewing for coffee since her husband died. If those horses starve, she loses the place. And she's got Etta."

    "I know what she's got," Elijah said. His hands had closed at his sides. "Everybody said a woman couldn't run a hundred acres alone. Two years she's been making liars of them. The hay was how."

    "And now the hay is ash."

    Elijah's pale old eyes were watery, but there was steel behind them. "A man like that, you give him an inch, he takes the whole valley and asks why you're still standing on his land. I seen it before." He turned to Arjun. "You got sons?"

    "No."

    "You got a wife?"

    "No."

    "Then what have you got to lose?"

    The question hung in the air of the store, among the sacks of flour and the barrels of nails and the jars of hard candy that children pressed their faces against when they came in with their mothers.

    Arjun didn't answer it. He walked out into the street and stood in the dust and faced north, toward the Brandt ranch, invisible behind the ridges and the dark, but there all the same. Breathing. Waiting. Growing bolder with every cut fence and every dead cow and every chance the south valley let slip away.

    He walked to the forge. The fire was banked for the night, coals glowing dull red under the ash. Kai sat on the bench outside, whittling a piece of juniper with a knife so sharp it made no sound as it cut.

    "You heard," Arjun said.

    "I heard."

    "What would you do?"

    "What I'd do doesn't matter. You're the one holding the hammer."

    "There's no hammer."

    Kai stopped whittling. The dim light of the banked fire caught his face strangely.

    "You've carried one your whole life. You've carried it so long you've talked yourself into believing you set it down."

    "I don't want it."

    "Iris Barkley didn't want her hay burned. Wanting and having aren't the same country."

    Arjun sat on the bench. The juniper Kai was whittling had taken the shape of a horse, small and rough and somehow alive in the grain of the wood. The knife moved through it, steady and clean, each stroke deliberate. For a while that was the only motion in the street.

    "Tomorrow you finish it," Kai said. "You heat it and hammer it and grind it and temper it. And when it's done, you'll have a knife."

    "A tool."

    "If you say so."

    Kai stood and walked toward the small room behind the forge where he slept. At the door he stopped.

    "The fire will be ready at sunrise. Bring your arms. They're going to hurt."

    They did. Arjun's arms and shoulders and back were sore in places the first day hadn't found. But he was at the forge before sunrise, and when Kai came out the fire was already lit and the steel was already heating in the coals.

    "You're learning," Kai said.

    "About forging?"

    "About showing up."

    .

    Arjun worked the blade all morning. Kai had been right about the memory of the metal. The second day was easier than the first, not because the work was different, but because his body had begun to understand what his mind was slow to accept. The hammer falls. The steel responds. You don't force the shape. You find it.

    He heated and hammered and heated again. The blade lengthened. The edge thinned. The tang took the shape it was supposed to have. Sweat ran down his back and dried before it reached his belt, and the morning went by without his noticing it go. By noon he had something that was almost a knife.

    "Now you grind," Kai said. "The forging gives it shape. The grinding gives it purpose."

    He showed Arjun how to use the grinding wheel, how to hold the blade at the angle, how to feel the edge emerging from the steel, the bevel thinning under his fingertip until it bit. The wheel sang and the sparks flew and the blade began to gleam.

    "This is the part most men skip. The forging is loud and satisfying. The grinding is slow and tedious. But the grinding is where the blade becomes a blade. The forging is just potential. The grinding is reality."

    Arjun ground until his fingers cramped and the edge ran true from heel to point. Kai took the blade and inspected it, turning it in the light, running his thumb along the edge with the delicacy of a man who had felt a thousand edges and knew exactly when he was about to be cut.

    "Now the tempering. This part you can't rush. You heat it to the right color and quench it in oil, and the steel comes out hard. Hard enough to hold an edge. Hard enough to do what it was made to do."

    He heated the blade one final time, not to orange now but to a color Arjun couldn't name, a deep red that seemed to come from inside the metal rather than from the fire. He held it there, judging, then plunged it into a bucket of oil. The oil smoked and flared and settled.

    When he lifted the blade out it was dark and hard and final. He wiped it clean and handed it over.

    "There. You made a knife."

    Arjun held it. The handle wasn't on yet, just the bare tang, but the blade was real. It had weight and balance. It had an edge that would cut. He had made it from scrap nobody wanted.

    "It's good," Kai said. "Not great. The point's a little thick and the bevel wanders near the heel. But it'll do what it's supposed to do."

    "What is it supposed to do?"

    "That depends on the man holding it. A knife doesn't know if it's a tool or a weapon. The steel doesn't choose. The man chooses."

    Kai clamped the blade to the workbench and fitted a handle from a piece of mesquite that had been drying behind the forge. He drilled the holes and set the pins and sanded the wood until the grain lay flat and the surface held no snag. Then he handed the finished knife to Arjun.

    It was beautiful in the way useful things are beautiful. No ornament. No decoration. A blade and a handle and the weight of purpose.

    Arjun slid it into his belt. It sat against his hip, solid and right, no adjustment needed.

    "It's a tool," he said.

    Kai turned back to the forge and laid another piece of steel in the coals, and after a while the hammer began to fall, and the sound of it rang out across the quiet street and was swallowed by the dust and the distance and the light.

    Chapter Eight

    Supper at the Store

    The store was empty when Arjun came in from the freight dock, dust in his throat and the day's heat still rising off his shoulders. He hadn't meant to stay. Meant to get a drink of water and walk back to the barn. But Beatriz was standing behind the counter with a crate of canned goods at her feet and a stillness in her face that stopped him at the door.

    "You want supper," she said. Not a question.

    "I can eat in the barn."

    "You've eaten in the barn for two weeks."

    "Barn suits me."

    She lifted the crate onto the counter and began setting the cans on the shelf above it, one by one, with the same motion she'd used a thousand times. She did not look at him.

    "There's a table in the back. I cook at seven. You'll eat at seven."

    She turned and walked through the curtained doorway before he could answer. The curtain swung once and was still.

    Arjun stood with his hat in his hands. Outside, the last light was draining out of the sky above the San Mateo range, and the street was settling into the quiet that came after the day's work but before the night's trouble. He could hear the wind picking up, the dry scrape of it against the adobe. He thought about the barn. He thought about the silence he'd been chasing for three years. Then he put his hat on the peg by the door and walked through the curtain.

    The back room was small and clean. A table of rough pine, two chairs, a stove that had been scrubbed until the iron showed through the blacking. A window looking out onto nothing but a patch of hard earth and a fence that needed mending. It was not a room for company.

    It was a room where one person ate alone.

    She was already at the stove, her back to him, working a skillet with the same efficiency she brought to everything. The store, the freight line, the ledgers and the accounts, she ran all of it alone. She had been running all of it alone for four years.

    "You don't have to do this," he said.

    "I know what I have to do."

    He sat at the table because there was nowhere else to sit. The chair was hard. The room was warm from the stove. After a minute she set a plate in front of him, beans, salt pork, a piece of bread still steaming. She sat across from him with her own plate and began to eat without ceremony.

    They ate in silence. The wind worked at the window. Somewhere down the street a shutter was pulled to, and after it the town seemed further away than before.

    "How long have you been drifting?" she asked.

    He looked up from his plate. She was watching him with eyes that had seen a great deal and judged most of it.

    "Three years," he said. "A little more."

    "Before that?"

    "Texas mostly. Some time in the Nations."

    "I mean before that."

    He put down his fork. "The Border Wars."

    She nodded. "Tom Harken was there with you."

    It wasn't a question. He said nothing.

    "I recognized him when he rode in," she said. "I recognize most of them. The Brandt outfit is full of men who learned their trade somewhere south of the border."

    "And you stayed."

    "I had a store."

    She said it flat, without self-pity. A store was reason enough. Arjun had known women who held ground because no one else would, because leaving meant letting the thing they'd built fall into hands that would ruin it. Some were still holding. Some were dead.

    "Why now?" he said. "The supper. Why tonight?"

    She pushed her plate aside and folded her hands on the table. She was not a young woman. The lines around her eyes were deep, and there was gray in the dark hair pulled back at her neck. But she wasn't old either. She was worn, not used up. There was a difference.

    "Because you're halfway gone," she said.

    "I'm standing right here."

    "You eat in the barn. You keep your bedroll tied. Two months, and you've not hung so much as a hat on a nail. I've watched men keep one foot in the stirrup for twenty years. I know the look."

    The wind gusted hard against the window and something loose rattled on the roof. Arjun waited. He had learned patience in hard places, and he had learned that when a woman like Beatriz Vargas decided to talk, the timing was hers.

    .

    "I told you once that you knew enough for that night," she said. "Tonight you'll hear the whole of it. My husband's name was Rafael. Rafael Vargas y Córdoba. His family had been in this valley since the Spanish grants. Before that, even. His grandfather's grandfather traded with the Apache and fought the Comanche and put the first irrigation ditch into this ground. This was his land. Not a claim he filed. Land he was born on."

    She stood and went to the stove. She poured coffee into two cups and brought them back. Her hands were steady.

    "When Silas Brandt came to the valley, he had money and papers and three men who'd ride with him anywhere. He bought the north range from a man who didn't own it. He filed claims on water that ran through six other spreads. When people complained, he offered to buy them out. Fair price, he called it. Less than fair. But some took it anyway. They didn't want trouble."

    She drank her coffee. Her eyes were on the window but not seeing the fence or the hard earth.

    "Rafael wouldn't sell. The water rights ran through our land. Without them, the south valley dries up. The Martinezes, the Cohens, the Pfeiffers, they all depend on that water. Rafael knew that. He told Brandt no. He was polite about it. He thought being polite mattered."

    Arjun knew what was coming. He had seen it before, in different places, with different names. The polite man who thinks the rules will protect him. The hard man who knows they won't.

    "Brandt came back a month later with a different offer. More money. Less polite. Rafael said no again. Brandt told him to think about it." A pause. "He said it very quietly. The quiet ones are always worse."

    She got up and refilled her cup. She did not sit back down. She stood by the stove with the cup in both hands. Her voice was flat and even, the same register she used behind the counter when she was tallying freight.

    "A week after that, three men rode up to the ranch house at dusk. I was in the kitchen. I heard the horses. Rafael went out to meet them. He went out alone. He didn't take a gun."

    She paused. A long one. Arjun did not move.

    "He thought if he went out unarmed, they'd talk. He thought that was how decent men behaved. He was a decent man. That was the trouble."

    The clock on the shelf ticked. The wind scraped at the adobe.

    "They didn't talk. They pulled him off the porch and they beat him in the yard. In front of me. I was standing in the doorway. One of them held a gun on me and told me to watch. So I watched."

    She set the cup down. Her hand was absolutely still.

    "When they were finished, Brandt rode up. He'd been waiting on the road. He got down off his horse and walked over to where Rafael was lying in the dirt. He crouched down next to him. I couldn't hear what he said. But I could hear Rafael trying to breathe."

    She was quiet a moment.

    "It was a sound I did not know a man could make," she said, in the same flat voice, and went on. "Brandt stood up and looked at me. He said he was sorry for the unpleasantness. He said he hoped I would reconsider the offer. Then he got on his horse and all four of them rode away."

    "And your husband?"

    "He lived for three hours. I held his head in my lap. He couldn't speak. The blood was inside him. There was nothing to do but wait." Her eyes went to the window. "I waited. He died. The sun came up."

    .

    She sat down again. She did not cry. She had done her crying, Arjun thought, in the first year or the second or the third, and what was left was something harder and drier and more permanent.

    "I rode to town that morning. Went to Judge Whitcomb. He was a good man. Everyone said so. He is still a good man. That is what everyone still says."

    She looked at Arjun directly for the first time since she'd started talking.

    "He told me he was sorry. He told me it was a tragedy. He told me he would investigate. He poured me a glass of whiskey. I sat in his office while he made notes. Very careful notes. He asked me to describe the men. I described them. He wrote it all down. Then he walked me to the door and told me to go home and rest. He would handle it."

    "Did he?"

    "He sent a letter to the territorial marshal in Santa Fe. The marshal sent a letter back saying he would look into it when he had the men. That was four years ago. He has not yet had the men."

    "What about the town?"

    "The town was sorry. The town brought food. The town said prayers at the church. Father Rojas, he came to the ranch every day for a month. He didn't tell me it was God's will. He didn't tell me anything. He just sat with me. He was the only one who understood that words weren't what I needed."

    "But he didn't act."

    "He's a priest."

    "There are priests who act."

    "Not in this valley."

    She stood again and began clearing the plates. The meal was over. The story was not. Arjun could feel it in the room, something still building, something that hadn't been said.

    She stacked the plates and wiped her hands on a cloth, and for a while the only sound was the clock.

    "Did Brandt ever come back?" Arjun asked.

    "Many times. He still comes. Every few months he rides into the store and buys a sack of flour or a box of shells. He's always polite. He asks how the business is doing. He offers to help with the freight. He has never once mentioned Rafael. Not once. As if the man never existed."

    She hung the cloth on a hook by the stove and turned to face him. The lamplight caught the lines of her face, the set of her jaw, the thing in her eyes that had been burning for four years without going out.

    "I don't want revenge," she said. "I want someone to say it was wrong. Not to me, I know it was wrong. I want someone to say it to him. To Brandt. To stand in front of him and say what he did and not look away. Not negotiate. Not make an offer. Just name the thing he did and make him hear it."

    "That won't bring your husband back."

    "No. It won't."

    "It won't fix the valley."

    "Probably not."

    "Then what do you want?"

    She crossed her arms and took him in, measuring, unimpressed, ready to be disappointed.

    "I want the world to be the kind of place where a man who does what Brandt did cannot ride into town and buy flour like nothing happened. This town calls itself peaceful. It is afraid. The judge calls it the law. It is waiting, dressed up. I want someone to stand in front of Silas Brandt and say what he did, plain, where he cannot buy his way past the hearing of it."

    "And you think I'm that someone."

    "I think you know how. That's different from being willing. I learned the difference four years ago. Half the men in this valley know how. Not one of them is willing."

    She came back to the table but didn't sit. She stood across from him with her hands on the back of the chair, and her voice was lower now, quieter, which made it worse.

    "When you walked into my store, you had the look of a man running from himself long enough to get tired of it. I know that look. I see it in the mirror every morning. The difference is, I can't leave. I have a store. I have freight accounts and ledgers and people who depend on me for flour and salt and cartridges. But you could leave. You were planning to. I saw it in your bedroll, still tied and ready. In the way you ate in the barn."

    "I told you. The barn suits me."

    "The barn is a way of not living somewhere. I know. I've been not living somewhere for four years."

    She pulled the chair back and sat down. She was tired, Arjun saw. Not the tiredness of a day's work. The tiredness of years. It was in the slope of her shoulders and the stillness of her hands in her lap.

    "When Brandt's men cut the wire, you found it. You followed the sign. You saw who did it. And you walked away. I'm not blaming you, you had your reasons. But I need to know what they are. Because if you're going to stay, I need to understand what kind of man is staying. And if you're going to leave, I need to know that too. I've had enough of men who leave."

    The wind had died. The room was silent except for the ticking clock and the faint rustle of the lamp flame. Arjun studied his hands on the table. They were good hands. They had built things and fixed things and killed men.

    "I've killed eighteen men," he said.

    She did not flinch. She did not look away.

    "Fourteen of them in three days, at a place called Devil's Arroyo. You'll have heard that part. Everyone has." He was quiet a moment. "Some of them needed killing. Some I'm not sure about. The ones I'm not sure about are the ones I see when I close my eyes. Not the faces. The hands. Before a man dies, his hands do something. They reach for something. They open. They close." He turned his own hands over on the table, palms up. "I see their hands.

    "I stopped carrying a gun because I didn't trust myself to use it right. Not the shooting, the deciding. I'd lost the knack of knowing when it was necessary and when it was just easy. That's a hard thing to lose. It's harder to get back."

    "Is that why you walked away from the fence?"

    "That's part of it."

    "What's the rest?"

    He was quiet for a moment. The clock ticked. The lamp hissed.

    "If I start," he said, "I don't know where I'll stop. That's the truth. I've been trying to be a man who doesn't start things. A man who works, eats, sleeps, moves on. That man doesn't hurt anyone. That man doesn't make anything worse."

    "And does he make anything better?"

    "No."

    "So you've chosen to be harmless."

    "I've chosen to be gone."

    "And yet you're still here."

    He looked up at her. The lamp was between them, the light making shadows on her face that moved when the flame moved.

    "I haven't figured that out yet," he said.

    "I have."

    She leaned forward. Her hands came apart, and she set them flat on the table, palms down, bracing against something.

    "You're still here because you know what I know. You know that walking away from the cut fence didn't make you harmless. It made you part of the thing you're walking away from. The fence didn't get cut by accident. It got cut because men like Silas Brandt have learned that no one will stop them. They've learned it from Judge Whitcomb's letters. From Tom Harken's silence. From every man who saw something wrong and kept walking. You didn't stay out of it. There is no out of it. You just picked a side without admitting it."

    Arjun sat still. The lamp flame leaned and recovered.

    "Rafael picked a side," she said. "He picked it when he said no to Brandt. He picked it when he went out on the porch without a gun. He thought being right was enough. It wasn't. Being right without being willing to back it up is just another kind of dying. It takes longer. It hurts more. But it's still dying."

    She stood up. The chair scraped on the floor. She went to the window and looked out at the patch of hard earth and the fence that needed mending.

    "I've been dying for four years," she said. "Slow. Polite. Keeping the store. Keeping the accounts. Keeping quiet. Watching Brandt ride in and out like he owns everything, because he does. The only thing he doesn't own is the truth of what he did. I've been holding that truth alone. I'm tired of holding it alone."

    She turned from the window. Her face was in shadow now, the lamp behind her.

    "I'm not asking you to kill him. I'm not asking you to fight my war. I'm asking you to stop pretending you don't have a stake in it. You do. Every man who knows the story and does nothing has a stake in it. You're in it whether you pick up a gun or not. The only question is whether you'll be in it as a man who acted or a man who watched."

    Arjun stood. He put on his hat. He walked to the door that led to the store and paused with his hand on the curtain.

    "I'll think about it," he said.

    "No you won't. Thinking is what Whitcomb does. Thinking is what this town has been doing for four years. Thinking keeps the fence cut and the widow grieving and the killer smiling. What I want is not your thoughts."

    He turned. She was still by the window, still in shadow, her shoulders set, her chin level, the same as the night three men rode up to her ranch and changed everything.

    "Then what?"

    "I want you to look at it. Not think about it. Look at it. The whole thing. What happened. Who knew. Who did nothing. The whole shape of it. Look at it with the same eyes you used to read men in the Border Wars. And then decide."

    "And if I decide to leave?"

    "Then leave. But don't tell yourself it wasn't your fight. Every fight is someone's. The only question is who decides to make it theirs."

    He didn't move for a while, still framed in the doorway. The store was dark beyond the curtain. The street was dark beyond the store. Somewhere out there was the barn where he'd been sleeping, and the horse he'd been ready to saddle, and the road that led away from Perdido to somewhere else that would turn out to be the same.

    "I'll be here in the morning," he said.

    "I know."

    He walked through the dark store, past the shelves of canned goods and the barrels of flour and the counter where she stood every day, selling cartridges to Brandt's men and sugar to the south-valley women and never once letting anyone see what he'd just seen. He opened the door and stepped out onto the street.

    The night was cold. The stars were out, sharp and close, the sky very clear after the wind. He could see the lights of Gus's place down the street and the dark shape of the church beyond it. Behind the livery, Kai's forge was banked down to a dim red eye.

    He walked to the barn and didn't sleep.

    Somewhere past midnight he rose and opened the trunk. He did not stop over what was in it this time. He took out the Winchester, still in its canvas, and unwrapped it and laid it on the straw beside his bedroll. He did not load it. Tom Harken had put that rifle in his hands in the war years, back when the debts between them all ran the other way. He lay on his back beside it and watched it in the dark, the way a man watches something he thought he'd left behind and found waiting for him anyway.

    The wind had quit, and the barn was quiet.

    Chapter Nine

    The Judge's House

    The judge's house stood on a low rise at the eastern edge of Perdido, a two-story frame building with a porch that looked back toward town and a window that looked out on nothing at all. Cottonwoods had been planted around it thirty years ago by someone who believed in shade. They had grown tall and close, and in the late afternoon they threw shadows across the house like bars.

    Arjun walked up from the road leading the buckskin mare. He had not ridden. It was only a mile from Beatriz's store, and he had wanted the time to think, though thinking had not gotten him far. Thinking rarely did. A man could think himself into any corner and out of it again without ever moving his feet.

    He tied the mare to the rail and stepped onto the porch. The boards were swept clean. A gray cat opened one eye from its place on a windowsill, judged him irrelevant, and closed it again.

    The door opened before he knocked.

    Judge Whitcomb was a tall man, thin at the shoulder and thick at the waist, with white hair that had once been red and a face that had settled into lines of perpetual consideration. He wore a black coat despite the heat, a white shirt buttoned to the throat, and spectacles that he pushed up on his forehead when he wanted to see a man clearly. He pushed them up now.

    "Mr. Tucker," he said. "I wondered when you'd come."

    "You knew I would?"

    "I know men. Come in."

    The front room served as both parlor and study. Books lined two walls, real books, leather-bound, spines cracked from reading rather than display. A desk sat beneath the window that looked out on nothing, piled with papers held down by a polished stone. Two chairs faced the desk. A sideboard held a decanter and glasses.

    Whitcomb gestured Arjun to a chair and sat behind the desk. He did not offer a drink. Not yet.

    "I've heard about you," Whitcomb said. "The Tucker man from the Border Wars. The one who walked away."

    "Walked away from what?"

    "From the business of killing. From a reputation most men would have ridden into the ground." He folded his hands on the desk. "I respect a man who walks away from things."

    "Most men don't have the chance."

    "No. They don't." He studied Arjun. "You're here about the trouble in the valley."

    "I'm here about Gutierrez."

    The name landed in the room and stayed there. Whitcomb's expression didn't change, but something behind his eyes went very still, a door shutting quiet and final.

    "Miguel Gutierrez," Whitcomb said. "A good man. I knew his father."

    "They beat him and left him in an arroyo. His boy found him in the morning. That was weeks back, and he still can't chew on the left side, and he may never walk right again. There was no doctor to send for. The nearest one is twenty miles off in Socorro, and the only one this valley ever had is two years in the ground. So he sits on his porch and carves, because carving is what his hands can still do."

    "I've heard."

    "Have you done anything about it?"

    Whitcomb removed his spectacles and polished them with a cloth from his pocket. A careful, unhurried gesture. He had learned long ago that ceremony could fill the space where action should go.

    "Mr. Tucker, what would you have me do?"

    "Your job."

    "My job," Whitcomb repeated. He placed the spectacles back on his nose. "My job is the law. The law requires evidence. A man beaten in the dark, by persons unknown, for reasons unstated, that is not evidence. That is a crime that may never be solved."

    "It's been solved. Everyone in this valley knows who did it."

    "Knowing and proving are not the same thing."

    "They never are. That's the problem."

    Whitcomb leaned back. The leather creaked. Outside, the cottonwoods stirred in a wind that did not reach the ground, and the shadows on the porch shifted and settled.

    "Let me tell you about this valley," Whitcomb said. "I came here in '58, when Perdido was twelve buildings and a hope. No law then except what a man carried on his hip. I helped write the first town charter. I presided over the first court. I married couples, buried the dead, settled disputes over water and cattle and fence lines, and I did it all without a badge or a gun because the people trusted me to be fair." He paused. "They still trust me. That trust is the only real authority I have."

    "And what have you done with it?"

    The question hung between them. Whitcomb held Arjun's eyes a moment, then stood and walked to the sideboard. He poured whiskey into two glasses and brought one to Arjun. This time he did not ask.

    "I've kept the peace," Whitcomb said. "For seventeen years, I have kept the peace in a country where peace is hard to come by. By being careful. By not rushing to judgment. By letting hot tempers cool before decisions were made."

    "The tempers aren't cooling. They're heating up."

    "Yes." Whitcomb sat. He held his glass but did not drink. "They are."

    "Silas Brandt has cut fence, killed cattle, burned a widow's hay, and put a man in the dirt of an arroyo for the crime of being Mexican on the wrong side of the river. Next time it will be worse. And you're sitting here telling me about process."

    "What would you have me do, Mr. Tucker? Arrest Silas Brandt on the word of a man who couldn't identify his attackers? Convene a grand jury with witnesses too frightened to speak? Ride out to the Brandt ranch with a warrant I know he'll ignore, because the only man in this territory who can enforce it is me, and I am sixty-three years old and I have never fired a gun at another human being?"

    "You could say it was wrong."

    Whitcomb's hand stopped halfway to his mouth.

    "I could say it was wrong," he repeated slowly, testing the words. "I could stand up in this town and say that Silas Brandt is a danger to every man, woman, and child in this valley. I could say that the law has failed and that good people must now do what the law cannot." He set the glass down. "And then what?"

    "Then they'd know."

    "Know what?"

    "That someone with authority sees what they see. That they're not alone in it."

    Whitcomb was quiet a good while. The cat on the windowsill stood, stretched, and jumped down to the floor with a soft thump. It crossed the room and settled at Whitcomb's feet.

    "When Rafael Vargas was killed," Whitcomb said, "I rode to the Brandt ranch myself. I questioned Silas Brandt for three hours. I took a statement from every hand on the place. Six men swore he was home the whole night. The one witness who could say otherwise was Beatriz, and any lawyer the territory sent would have called her testimony grief and been done with her in a morning. I wrote it all up and sent it to Santa Fe. Then I wrote a second letter, private, to a marshal I'd known before the war, and asked him to come see for himself. He wrote back that he'd come when he had the men. I wrote him twice more. He never had the men." He turned his glass on the desk without lifting it. "She thinks I took careful notes and poured her a whiskey and let her husband die a second time on paper. I did more than she knows. It amounted to the same nothing."

    "You knew he did it."

    "She watched him ride up after his men were finished. Watched him crouch over a dying man and offer his regrets. Six men swore he was home all night, and I knew every one of them was lying, and I could not prove a single word of it."

    "And you let it stand."

    Whitcomb picked up his glass and drank, a long swallow that emptied half of it. When he spoke again his voice was lower.

    "I let it stand because the alternative was worse. If I had arrested Brandt without the evidence to make it stick, he would have resisted. Men would have died. The valley would have split open. And for what? He would have walked free, and I would have lost the authority I'd spent a decade building, and the next time I needed it, it wouldn't be there."

    "So you traded one injustice now for the chance to prevent a bigger one later."

    "Yes."

    "Did it work?"

    Whitcomb looked at him. The question required no answer. They both knew. The bigger injustice had arrived anyway, in increments, a cut fence, a dead cow, a burned haystack, a man beaten in the dark, and each increment had been met with the same careful, measured, principled response, which was no response at all.

    "You think I'm a coward," Whitcomb said.

    "I didn't say that."

    "It's in the way you're sitting. In the way you haven't touched your drink." He gestured at the glass. "A man who won't drink with you is keeping himself apart."

    Arjun raised the glass and drank, once, and set it down.

    "I don't think you're a coward," he said. "I think you're a good man who's been careful so long you've forgotten what courage looks like."

    The words were not spoken in anger. Flat, direct, without ornament.

    Which made them worse.

    Whitcomb received them without flinching. He had spent years preparing for this conversation, Arjun realized, rehearsing his defenses, learning to turn every accusation before it was made. What he had not prepared for was the truth spoken simply, without accusation at all.

    "Let me tell you something about courage," Whitcomb said. "I have seen men die for principles. Hanged for cattle they didn't steal, shot for insults they didn't mean, beaten for the sound of their own names. At every one of those funerals, someone stood up and said the dead man was brave. Do you know what I thought, every time? He's still dead. The injustice he fought is still injustice. And the men who killed him are sleeping soundly, because being right doesn't protect you from being dead."

    "Courage isn't about winning. It's about standing."

    "Standing for what? The right to be shot down while your widow watches?" Whitcomb shook his head. "I've seen too much of that kind of courage. It burns hot and fast and it's gone, and the people left behind live with what it cost."

    "So what's the alternative?"

    "Patience. The long game." He leaned forward. "Silas Brandt is not a young man. He has no sons. His outfit is held together by money, not loyalty. In five years, ten at the most, his power will crumble. The valley will return to what it was. No one will have to die."

    "In five years, how many more Gutierrezes? How many more burned haystacks? How many more men who walk out of their own front doors and don't walk back in?"

    Whitcomb said nothing.

    "You're buying time," Arjun said. "But you're not the one paying for it."

    "No. I'm not."

    He stood and walked to the window. The light had begun to change, the long gold of late afternoon giving way to something softer, the first hint of evening. The cottonwoods were still. The cat had gone to sleep.

    "I had a son," Whitcomb said. His back was to Arjun. "His name was William. He was twenty-two years old when he died. He was not shot or hanged or beaten. He died of a fever, in this house, in the room upstairs. I sat with him for three days while he burned. On the last day, he opened his eyes and looked at me and said, 'Papa, I'm afraid.' And I said, 'Don't be. I'm here.' And then he died."

    He turned from the window.

    "I have spent every day since then trying to keep other men's sons from dying. That is what I do. That is what I am. If that makes me careful, if that makes me weak, then I am careful and I am weak. But I will not send young men to die for a cause that may not be winnable. I did it once, in the war, and I will not do it again."

    Arjun did not answer at once. He was thinking of Tom Harken, and of the men he had killed in the Border Wars, and of good reasons that had delivered men to the wrong place anyway.

    "I've seen men die too," Arjun said. "Because someone made the wrong call. Because they trusted the wrong man. But I've also seen men die because no one would stand with them. Because the people who should have been there stayed home. Because the judge was careful and the priest was silent and the good men of the town had business elsewhere."

    "Is that what you think I am? A good man with business elsewhere?"

    "I think you've been alone in this valley so long you've convinced yourself that being alone is the same as being wise."

    Whitcomb returned to his chair. He sat heavily, the full weight of him settling all at once, a man for whom every chair had grown more distant.

    "When I was young," he said, "I believed the law could fix anything. That if you wrote the right words and followed the right procedures and gave every man his day in court, justice would follow. It took me thirty years to learn that the law is not justice. The law is a tool. It works when good men wield it and fails when they don't. And sometimes." He paused. "Sometimes there are no good men. Or the good men are afraid. Or the good men have reasons, good reasons, reasons you'd respect, to stay out of it."

    "And then what?"

    "Then the law fails. And something else takes its place."

    "Something like Silas Brandt."

    "Or something like you." Whitcomb nodded slowly.

    Arjun studied the old man across the desk. The whiskey glass was empty. The light was going.

    "I'm not the law," Arjun said.

    "No. You're not."

    "I'm a ranch hand. I feed mules and mend fences."

    "You came here," Whitcomb said. "You asked about Gutierrez. You're sitting in my study drinking my whiskey and telling me I've failed. A man who isn't looking for a fight doesn't do those things."

    Arjun had no answer to that. Or rather he had an answer, but it was not one he was ready to give.

    "You know what I see when I look at you?" Whitcomb asked.

    "I don't much care."

    "I see a man who's been running from what he is. From what he knows he can do." He removed his spectacles and folded them on the desk. Without them, his face was older and more tired, the eyes deep-set and red-rimmed. "I've seen men like you before. After the war, they drifted through here, men who'd been soldiers, who'd done things they couldn't forget. Most of them kept drifting. A few stayed. And every one of them eventually had to decide whether to pick up what they'd put down."

    "And what did they decide?"

    "Some picked it up. Some didn't. The ones who didn't, they're buried here. Not because they fought, but because they wouldn't. The world doesn't let a man stay out of it forever."

    "The world doesn't get a vote."

    "No. But it gets a result."

    The room had grown dim. Neither man moved to light a lamp. In the shadows, the books on the walls looked less like learning and more like weight, all those words, all that wisdom, stacked up against a problem they couldn't solve.

    "When Rafael Vargas was killed," Whitcomb said, "I told myself the same thing I'm telling you now. That the law required evidence. That I couldn't act on what I knew. That the cost of acting would be higher than the cost of waiting." He turned his hands over and considered them. "I was wrong."

    "Wrong about the law?"

    "Wrong about the cost. The cost of waiting has been higher than anything I could have done. It has been paid by people who couldn't afford it, by Beatriz, by Gutierrez, by every family in the south valley living in fear while I sat in this house and polished my spectacles and told myself I was being prudent." He picked up his glass, found it empty, and set it down again. "I am not a coward. But I have done a coward's work. That is the truth, and I've known it a long time, and I've never said it out loud until now."

    Arjun said nothing. There was nothing to say. A man admitting his failure didn't need comfort or contradiction. He needed silence and a witness.

    After a while, Whitcomb spoke again.

    "What will you do?"

    "I don't know yet."

    "But you'll do something."

    "I didn't say that."

    "You didn't have to." Whitcomb almost smiled. "It's in the way you're sitting."

    The cat stirred, stood, and walked to the door. It glanced back at Whitcomb once, patient and unimpressed, and waited.

    Whitcomb rose. Arjun rose with him.

    "I can't help you," Whitcomb said. "Not the way you want. I can't ride out against Brandt. I can't rally the south valley. I can't be what this situation requires." He extended his hand. "But I can tell you the truth, which is more than I've done for anyone in a long time. Brandt is going to move against the south valley. Soon. He has the men and the will, and he knows no one will stop him, because no one ever has. If someone is going to stop him, it will have to be someone who doesn't care about the law, or process, or the long game."

    "That's not me."

    "Then who?"

    They stood facing each other in the darkening room. The cottonwoods moved outside, their leaves dry and constant in the dark.

    "I'll see myself out," Arjun said.

    He walked to the door and opened it. The evening air was cool and smelled of dust and sage. The first stars were coming out above the valley, white points in a sky that was still mostly blue. Somewhere in town, a dog barked. Somewhere farther off, a horse whinnied and was answered.

    "Mr. Tucker."

    Arjun turned.

    Whitcomb stood in the doorway, his tall frame outlined against the lamplight he had finally lit. He seemed older than he had an hour ago, or perhaps only more honest, the weight of his failure no longer hidden behind the weight of his office.

    "When Gutierrez was lying in the arroyo," Whitcomb said, "did I need proof?"

    "Yes."

    "Then why didn't I act?"

    "Because proof isn't what you needed," Arjun said. "Proof is what you wanted. What you needed was to decide what kind of man you were going to be. And you decided."

    He stepped off the porch and walked to the mare. The cat was sitting on the rail, watching him with eyes that showed nothing and saw everything. Arjun untied the reins and swung into the saddle.

    "Mr. Tucker," Whitcomb called again. "Whatever you decide, whatever kind of man you decide to be, I hope it's better than what I chose."

    Arjun took him in a last time, the old man in the lighted doorway, the books behind him, the cottonwoods above him, the gray cat at his feet.

    "So do I," he said.

    He rode back toward town without hurry. The road was pale in the starlight and the mare knew the way. Behind him, the light in Whitcomb's window burned on, one square of yellow under the cottonwoods, the last lit window between the town and the dark.

    Chapter Ten

    Three Riders at Noon

    He came at noon, when the sun stood straight overhead and the shadows hid beneath the horses. Three riders crested the north ridge and came down into Perdido slow, in no hurry about it, expecting to be watched.

    Arjun was stacking barrels behind the store when he heard them. He didn't turn. The first man to look was the first man to care, and caring before you knew what you were caring about was a fool's habit. But his hands slowed on the barrel staves, and he listened.

    Three horses. One of them heavy, a big animal carrying a big man who didn't ride hard because he didn't have to. The other two lighter, quicker. Working horses under working men. The rhythm said they'd been in no hurry all the way down from the north valley.

    Beatriz came to the back door of the store. She'd heard them too.

    "That'll be Brandt," she said.

    "You've seen him ride in before."

    "Not like this. Not in daylight, with men."

    Arjun set the barrel down. "You want me inside or outside?"

    "Inside. For now."

    He followed her through the store. Dim and cool, smelling of coffee and dry goods and the faint sweet sharpness of kerosene. Beatriz went to the front window and stood where she could see out without being seen from the street. Arjun stayed near the counter, close enough to move, far enough not to crowd her.

    Through the window glass, warped and rippled, thick at the center and greenish at the edges, he watched the three riders come down the street.

    The man in front rode a blood bay gelding, seventeen hands if it was an inch, with a sheen on its coat that said grain and grooming and time. The rider was tall, coatless despite the heat, in a white shirt and a gray vest with a watch chain glinting across it. Black hat, flat-brimmed, eastern. He sat his horse the way a man sits a chair he owns.

    Silas Brandt.

    Behind him and to either side rode two men Arjun didn't know. Hired men, from the look of them, capable, alert, watching the street with the flat competence of men paid to see trouble before it arrived. One carried a Winchester across his saddle. The other had his coat cut back on the right side, clear of the holster.

    The street was empty. It had emptied the moment the horses came into view. The town of Perdido knew when to disappear.

    Brandt reined up in front of the saloon. He studied it a moment, as if considering whether it was worth his time, and then he turned his horse toward the store.

    "Step back from the glass," Arjun said quietly.

    Beatriz didn't move. "He's not going to shoot me through a window."

    "No. But he'll see you waiting, and waiting is a kind of invitation."

    She stepped back. Stood with her arms crossed, facing the door.

    The bell above the door rang when Brandt pushed it open. A small bell, tinny, the kind that announced every stray dog and dust devil that wandered in off the street. Brandt ducked under the lintel, he was that tall, and straightened inside the store, and the bell kept ringing for a moment after he was still, as if it were more frightened than anyone.

    He took off his hat.

    That was the first surprise. Arjun had expected it to stay on. A man in Brandt's position didn't remove his hat for stores or saloons or the women who ran them. But Brandt held his hat at his chest and regarded Beatriz with an expression that might have been respect, or might have been the memory of how respect looked on other people's faces.

    "Mrs. Vargas," he said.

    "Mr. Brandt."

    The name lay between them like a stone.

    Brandt took in the store, the shelves half-empty, the flour bin only a third full, the canned goods stacked neat but sparse. He saw the prices chalked on the board above the counter, lower than they should have been for the distance the freight wagons had to travel. He saw everything, and Arjun saw him see it.

    "You've kept this place well," Brandt said. "Hard country to run a store in."

    "I manage."

    "I expect you do."

    He moved to the counter and set his hat down on it, crown up, settling in for a conversation. His hands were clean. His nails were clean. In New Mexico Territory in high summer, that meant he paid other men to get their hands dirty.

    "I've come to make you an offer," Brandt said.

    "I assumed you weren't here for the coffee."

    He smiled. An easy smile, practiced, the kind that had worked on men and women and testimony in courtrooms back east. "I'd like to buy you out. The store, the water rights, the grazing lease you hold with the south-valley families. All of it. I'll pay you a fair price, more than fair. Enough to leave this place and start somewhere better. Santa Fe. California. Wherever you'd like to go."

    Beatriz didn't answer right away. She walked behind the counter, her counter, her space, and put her hand on the ledger book that lay there, the one she'd kept for four years in her own careful hand.

    "And if I don't want to go?"

    "Then I'd ask why not."

    "Because this is my store. Because my husband built it. Because I have customers here who depend on me, and families who've traded with me for years, and because I don't sell things that aren't mine to sell."

    Brandt's smile held. His thumb found the watch chain and rested there. "I understand sentiment, Mrs. Vargas. I truly do. But sentiment won't water your cattle. Sentiment won't keep your shelves stocked when the freight wagons start having trouble on the north road, or when the south-valley families decide the price of standing with you is higher than the price of standing somewhere else."

    "Is that a threat?"

    "It's arithmetic."

    Arjun moved then. Not toward Brandt, just forward, into the light from the window, where Brandt could see him plainly. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to.

    Brandt's eyes found him and held. If he was surprised, none of it reached his face.

    "Mr. Tucker," he said. "Still passing through?"

    "Slower than I planned."

    "Five weeks is a slow pass." He said it mildly, a man remarking on weather. "I told you the first night that you were wasting yourself on freight. I see nothing has changed but the freight."

    "I've had enough of other men's wars."

    Brandt nodded slowly, as if Arjun had said something wise instead of something tired. "They're all other men's wars," he said. "Until they're yours."

    He turned back to Beatriz. "The offer stands. I'll give you until the end of the week to consider it. After that, I'll assume your answer is no, and I'll act accordingly."

    "And what does accordingly mean?"

    "It means I'll find another way to secure the water. There are always other ways."

    He picked up his hat, put it on, and walked to the door. At the threshold he paused, not toward Beatriz, but toward Arjun.

    "My first offer stands too, Tucker. I pay better than widows, and the work suits a man of your disposition."

    "I don't think you know my disposition."

    "I know men who've killed," Brandt said. "They come in two kinds. The ones who want to do it again, and the ones who are afraid they might." He tipped his hat, to Beatriz this time, and walked out.

    The bell rang. The door swung shut.

    Through the window, Arjun watched Brandt mount the blood bay and ride north with his two men behind him. The street stayed empty until the dust settled, and then it stayed empty a little longer.

    Beatriz hadn't moved from behind the counter. Her hand was still on the ledger book. Her face was the face of a woman who had learned, over four hard years, not to cry until the door was closed and the windows shuttered and the night was deep enough to swallow it.

    "He killed my husband," she said. "And then he walked into my store and smiled at me."

    "I know."

    "Would they call it murder, if I'd had a gun?"

    "Yes."

    "Would they be wrong?"

    Arjun didn't answer. He walked to the window and watched the dust cloud move north, shrinking against the brown flat of the valley floor. Brandt was riding back to his ranch, back to his hired men and his water and his arithmetic. He'd come into town easy, hat in hand, a man paying a social call. Somewhere behind that soft voice and clean collar was the man whose riders had cut the south-valley wire and burned Iris Barkley's hay and left Miguel Gutierrez in the arroyo, and beaten Rafael Vargas to death in the yard of the house he built.

    "I've seen men like him before," Arjun said. "They don't stop. They don't compromise. They take, and when they can't take, they break, and when they're done breaking they take the pieces."

    "Then what do you do with a man like that?"

    Arjun had been asking himself the same thing for three years. He'd stopped carrying a gun because he thought it would stop the question.

    It hadn't.

    "I don't know," he said. "But I know what happens if nobody does anything."

    .

    The south-valley families gathered that evening at the church. It wasn't a meeting, exactly. Father Rojas didn't call meetings. But word moved through Perdido fast and quiet, finding every doorstep, and by sundown everyone knew Brandt had been in town, and everyone knew about the end of the week.

    Esteban Martinez came first, with Joaquin beside him, the boy solemn as a deacon and watching the door. Miriam Cohen arrived with David, seventeen and trying to stand like thirty. Iris Barkley came alone, small and straight, her red hair pinned tight, and took a pew near the front as if she'd paid for it. The Pfeiffer brothers shuffled in last, smelling of pipe smoke and horses, and sat shoulder to shoulder in their usual pew, which was as close as the two of them ever willingly got. Maria Gutierrez came in quietly at the side, come for herself and for Miguel both, and folded her hands in her lap.

    Beatriz stood at the front, near the altar, arms crossed, chin up.

    Arjun stood in the back, near the door. He wasn't one of them. But Beatriz had asked him to stay, and he had stayed.

    Father Rojas came in from the sacristy, a small man with hands that seemed too thin for the work they did. He had been in Perdido twenty-three years. He'd baptized half the room and buried the other half's parents.

    "I understand Mr. Brandt paid us a visit," he said.

    "He wants to buy me out," Beatriz said. "The store. The water rights. The lease. He gave me to the end of the week. After that he finds another way. His words."

    "Another way." Elijah Pfeiffer's voice came up out of him like gravel turning in a bucket. "We know what his other ways look like. Miguel knows. Iris knows. Twenty bales and a milk cow scared half to death, that's one of his other ways."

    "We don't know it was his men burned that hay," Esteban said. He said it slowly, testing the boards before he put weight on them.

    "Now, Esteban."

    "Don't 'now, Esteban' me, Miriam. I'm saying what a judge would say."

    "A judge did say it," Iris said. She didn't raise her voice. She never had to. "Four years ago he said it over Rafael Vargas, and the men who did it are drawing wages this minute. I'm done waiting on what judges say."

    The quiet after that had weight. Amos Pfeiffer turned his pipe over in his three-fingered hand and studied the bowl of it as if the answer might be in there.

    "The end of the week," Esteban said. "And then what? He takes the water and the rest of us wither on our own land, one dry season at a time. Or we sell, one family at a time, each of us telling the others we had no choice. That is how he wants it. Quiet. Paper. No blood he has to explain."

    "Then we don't be quiet," David Cohen said. He was on his feet before his mother could reach him. "There's enough of us. We fight."

    The room turned toward the boy. Then, one by one, they turned toward the back of the church, toward Arjun.

    He felt their eyes come to him the way he'd once felt sights come to him, and his skin remembered it.

    He'd stood in front of men before. Men with guns, men with ropes, men who wanted to kill him or follow him or both. This was different. These were farmers and shepherds and widows, people who'd spent their lives building things, and they were looking at him the way a drowning man watches the bank.

    "You know how to do this," Beatriz said. "You're the only one here who does."

    "Knowing how and having the right are different things."

    "Name one time they were the same."

    He couldn't. He'd spent three years trying to forget every time he'd been sure he was right, and how little the being sure had mattered afterward.

    "Stand with us," Esteban said. "Be our man in this the way his dozen are his. We would pay what we could. It would not be much, but it would be honest."

    "You've heard what I did in the wars," Arjun said. "You think it means I can keep you alive. What it means is fourteen men in three days, and a medal I couldn't stand the weight of, and three years walking away from a thing that came along anyway." He took a breath. "You want the man in the stories. I'm not the man you're looking for. I'm the man he looks like."

    Nobody spoke.

    "Mr. Tucker," Rojas began gently.

    "Father, I'd take it kindly if you didn't." Arjun put on his hat. "I'm sorry. I am. But you'd be trusting your lives to something I don't trust myself."

    He went out into the dark and let the door close soft behind him. No one followed. Through the wall he heard the voices start again, low and urgent, going on without him, and that was worse than if they'd shouted.

    .

    He walked to the edge of town.

    The moon was high and thin, a paring of white against the black, and the desert lay still around him. He could see the lights of the Brandt ranch to the north, a cluster of warm yellow windows twelve miles distant, and the scattered lamps of the south valley, smaller and fewer.

    He sat down on a flat rock and tried to think, but thinking wasn't what came.

    What came was memory. Tom Harken, younger then, laughing in the firelight. You think too much, Arjun. That's your trouble. A man who thinks too much hesitates. A man who hesitates dies.

    Tom had been right, in his way. Arjun had thought too much and survived anyway. But surviving wasn't the same as living, and he'd had three years to learn the difference.

    They're all other men's wars. Until they're yours.

    What he had told the church was true. He sat with it a while, turning it the way Amos Pfeiffer had turned that pipe, and the longer he held it the less sure he was which kind of true it was. There was the true a man tells because it costs him. And there was the true a man tells because it lets him keep sitting on his rock while the lights burn twelve miles north.

    The moon moved. The lights in the Brandt ranch didn't. They burned steady, like a promise or a warning.

    He found Kai on the road at dawn.

    The blacksmith was coming back into town on the mule, unhurried, a sack of iron stock tied behind the saddle. He reined up when he saw Arjun, then swung down and walked, and Arjun fell in beside him. For a while the only sounds were the creak of the mule's tack and their boots on the hardpan.

    "You're up early," Kai said.

    "I haven't been down."

    They walked. The east was going gray, then rose, the ridgeline black against it.

    "They asked me last night," Arjun said. "At the church. To stand for them."

    "I heard."

    "I told them no."

    "I heard that too."

    The mule blew and shook its head. Somewhere off in the sage a quail called and got no answer.

    "You've been telling yourself that if you put the guns away, the things you did with them would go away too," Kai said.

    "I know."

    "Has it worked?"

    Arjun stopped walking. The question was simple and quiet and it landed harder than any blow.

    "No," he said. "It hasn't."

    Kai walked on, leading the mule, and Arjun caught him up, and they went the rest of the way into town without talking. The false fronts stood flat and gray in the early light. A door opened somewhere and closed. At the livery, Kai lifted the sack of iron down and set it by the wall, and it rang once on the hardpan, low, like a far-off bell.

    "I don't know if I can be what they need," Arjun said.

    Kai took the mule's headstall and started for the doors. Over his shoulder, without turning: "Neither does anyone else. That's not the question."

    "Then what is?"

    "Whether you'll try."

    The livery doors swung open and the mule went inside and Kai went with it. Arjun stood alone in the street while the sun cleared the ridge behind him, and his shadow ran out long and thin ahead of him, all the way down toward the river.

    Chapter Eleven

    View from the Ridge

    He woke to a hand on his shoulder and a voice pitched under the wind.

    "Get up," Kai said. "There's something you need to see."

    The barn was dark. Through the gaps in the plank wall the sky showed the deep blue-black that comes an hour before dawn. Arjun had slept short. Two nights since the church, since he had stood in front of the five families and told them no, and sleep had been coming to him in pieces since, like a debt paid grudgingly.

    "Now?" he said.

    "Now."

    The blacksmith stood in the barn doorway, already dressed, his leather apron slung over one shoulder, sleeves rolled despite the cold still clinging to the ground. He had not slept. It showed nowhere. Arjun had never seen him hurry, and he was not hurrying now. He was simply there, the way weather is there, and there was no arguing with weather.

    Arjun pulled on his boots and reached for his coat. The Winchester leaned against the wall by the bedroll where it had leaned for a week now, unloaded, oiled, and he stood a moment with the coat in his hands, studying it.

    Then he left it where it was.

    Kai saw the look and said nothing.

    Outside, the cold had settled into the low ground the way it does in that country, pooled in the alley like water. Arjun turned up his collar. Somewhere north a coyote called once and got no answer.

    They went up the main street in silence. Perdido at that hour was a different place, not hostile, not welcoming, simply absent. The false fronts stood flat and gray against the paling sky. A dog raised its head as they passed the feed store, considered them, and lowered it again. Somewhere behind the livery a rooster started to make an announcement and thought better of it.

    Kai did not take the road. He cut east through the sage and the sparse grama, picking a path Arjun could not see but that Kai seemed to know by heart. The ground rose. Stones turned under their boots. The air thinned and cooled and carried juniper and the faint mineral tang of the rock that lay under everything in that country.

    They did not talk. The climb was steep enough that talk would have cost breath, though Arjun suspected Kai could have talked all the way up without paying anything. The blacksmith moved uphill with the same unhurried economy he brought to iron, setting each foot where the rock would hold it, spending nothing. Not a young man. Not an old one. His age was one of the things Arjun had given up trying to pin down.

    The sky went from blue-black to gray, and the stars dissolved out of it one at a time, the small ones first. By the time the slope began to ease, the east was the color of a peach two days from ripe, and Arjun's legs had found the rhythm of the climb and stopped complaining about it.

    The ridge was a long backbone of limestone running north to south, dividing the valley from the higher desert beyond. Arjun had seen it every day since he had walked into town. He had never climbed it. Never had a reason to.

    They reached the crest as the sun broke over the mountains to the east. The light came all at once, no preamble. One moment the world was gray and indistinct. The next it was flooded with gold, every ridge and arroyo picked out sharp.

    Kai stopped at the edge of the rock shelf, hands loose at his sides, and faced the valley. Arjun came up beside him and did the same.

    For a while he could not have spoken if he had wanted to.

    Not because of the view. He had crossed the Sangre de Cristos and watched cloud shadows run fifty miles over the desert floor. Scenery did not do much to him anymore. This was something else. This was the whole shape of the thing laid out below him, and he knew the shape now. He knew the people in it.

    Perdido sat at the valley's center, a small cluster of adobe and wood, the bell tower of Father Rojas's church catching the first light. The road came in from the south, passed through the four streets, and ran north toward the Brandt place. He could pick out the Vargas store, the saloon, the livery, the well in the plaza where the women gathered mornings. Beatriz's freight wagon stood beside the store, the mules still in the corral, small as toys.

    North of town the valley widened, and the Brandt land lay green along the river, the main house a sprawl of whitewashed buildings behind split rails, the barn enormous, the corrals holding more horses than the south valley had cattle. From up here the design of it showed plain. Brandt had taken the best ground, and his fences ran down to the river in a way that left everything south of the water living on his patience.

    A fortress and a declaration, both at once.

    South of the river the valley was smaller and drier. The five families' places lay scattered along the creek. Esteban Martinez's cottonwoods stood over his home quarter, old trees, planted by men now dead. On the western bench Miriam Cohen's sheep were spread out gray as stones, already grazing. Iris Barkley's hundred acres of bottomland showed green in neat squares a woman worked alone because everyone had said she couldn't. The Pfeiffer brothers' cabin of railroad ties and river rock sat up its draw, and Arjun could see their two breakfast fires burning a hundred yards apart, one for each brother, the way they had kept it for forty years. Below the church, on the parish holdings, the goats were working the hillside.

    From above, the south valley looked like a fist that wanted to close and didn't know how.

    The river ran between the two halves, low this time of year, a silver thread through cottonwoods and willows. The only water that mattered for fifty miles in any direction, and worth more than gold on account of it.

    Arjun took it all in. The distances. The north in full sun and the south still half in shadow. The road that connected everything and divided everything.

    "How many times have you been up here?" he asked.

    "Enough," Kai said. "I like to know where I am."

    "And where are we?"

    "Standing on the only place a man can see both halves at once." Kai turned his head. "Though you're not."

    "Not what?"

    "Standing here. You're standing where your body is. Your mind is down there." He nodded at the valley. "In the store. In the street. In the church where they asked you and you told them no. That's where you've been living since you got here. In the small place. In the fear of what comes next."

    "It's hard to live anywhere else when what comes next is men with guns riding down on people who can't stop them."

    "I know," Kai said. "That's why I brought you up here."

    He turned and walked north along the spine of the ridge, not away from the valley but parallel to it. Arjun followed. The going was easier on the crest, and after a few hundred yards the ridge opened onto a broader shelf where wind had carved the limestone into shapes that looked almost deliberate, pillars, an arch, a flat table of stone that might have been an altar if anyone had wanted one.

    Kai stopped at the edge of the shelf.

    "Look."

    Below them the north valley lay open in its entirety. The Brandt headquarters in detail now, the porch of the main house facing the mountains, the bunkhouse behind it, the corrals stirring in the early light. Men moved between the buildings.

    "Keep looking," Kai said.

    Arjun kept looking. The men were saddling horses. More of them than he had counted in town, not just the riders who came in to drink, but others, men who kept to the north ranch. They moved with purpose. Not the loose ambling of hands starting a day's work. Something tighter.

    "How many?" Kai asked.

    "Fifteen. Maybe eighteen. Hard to say at this distance." Arjun watched them cinch and mount. "A dozen of those will be his real guns. The rest are cattle hands. They'll ride along and hope nothing happens."

    "And south of the river? If the five families stood together?"

    Arjun did the arithmetic he had been doing in his head for a week and had not said out loud. Esteban and Joaquin. David Cohen, seventeen and eager, which was its own kind of danger. The Pfeiffer brothers, old but steady. Whatever Rivera could bring. Iris Barkley could shoot, he had heard, better than she let on. Father Rojas would not fight, and Miguel Gutierrez could not.

    "Eight who could hold a rifle and mean it," he said. "Fewer who know what they're doing when someone shoots back."

    "And you."

    Arjun said nothing.

    "You've been counting since the day you walked in," Kai said. "You know the odds, the ground, and every man on both sides, what he can do and what he's afraid of. And you still think you're standing in the small place."

    Below, the riders were forming up in the yard. Arjun moved to the edge of the shelf. The drop was not sheer, but it was steep enough that a man would think twice.

    "There was a man I knew in the Border Wars," he said. His voice went flat, the way it did when he touched that country. "Name of Reyes. Best scout I ever saw. He could read sign that wasn't there yet. He could tell you how many men had crossed a trail, whether they were carrying water, how long since they'd eaten. He saw everything."

    Kai waited.

    "We got pinned in a canyon outside El Paso. Fourteen of us. Federales on both rims, and they were in no hurry, because there was no reason to be. Reyes went out before light to read the ground, and he came back and sat down and didn't say anything. That was the first wrong thing. Reyes always had something to say about ground."

    The riders below were crossing the green fields at their walk. Arjun watched them and saw the canyon.

    "I asked him what he'd found. He told me. Where every rifle sat. Which trails they'd stopped up. How much water they had against how much we had. He laid it all out in that flat voice of his, like a man reading a bill of sale, and it added up to nothing. No way out that didn't cost more men than we had. He saw the whole board, and the board was finished."

    Arjun turned from the edge.

    "An hour later he sat down against a rock and put his revolver in his mouth. He couldn't un-see it. Knowing everything broke him." He paused. "The rest of us got out that night through a flood cut he'd marked on his map and thought too narrow to matter. Nine of us, anyway. He was better than his own reading of the board, and he never knew it."

    Kai nodded, slow. "That's the risk."

    "You're telling me to see the whole picture. I'm telling you the whole picture might be too much for a man to hold."

    "Your scout saw the pieces," Kai said. "He never saw the pattern."

    "What pattern?"

    "The pattern is bigger than the man looking at it. It's the water that cut this river and the stone that wore down into this soil. It was here before Brandt and it will be here after him. And it holds every man who's ever stood on this ridge and had to decide whether to go back down."

    He said it and was done with it, and bent to pick up a flat piece of limestone as if the matter were closed.

    Below them the riders came out of the Brandt yard in a loose column and swung south toward the river. Arjun counted them again as they strung out along the fence line. Seventeen. The man at their head rode a blood bay, and even at this distance there was no mistaking the set of him, big through the shoulders, easy in the saddle, a man riding across ground he considered already his.

    "Brandt," Arjun said.

    "Yes."

    They watched the column move down the green fields. It did not hurry. That was the thing about it that Arjun marked. Men riding to a fight hurry, or they string out ragged with nerves. These men rode like a parade.

    "They won't cross," Arjun said.

    "No."

    "He's letting the south valley see them. Counting on somebody's boy at a window with his father's rifle, watching them come. Letting the fear do the work before a shot gets fired."

    "Fear is cheaper than bullets," Kai said. "He's a careful man with money."

    The column reached the river and stopped at the bank. The riders fanned out along the water, spacing themselves the way men do when they have been told how to do it. Brandt sat the blood bay at the center, and for a long minute nothing in the valley moved but the water.

    On the south side, small in the distance, a figure came out of the Martinez place and stood by the corral. Esteban, by the set of him, or Joaquin. Another showed at the Barkley fence line, and one of the Pfeiffer fires went suddenly to smoke, banked in a hurry. Nobody rode toward the river. Nobody ran. They just stood in their yards and watched seventeen mounted men look across the water at their homes, and the standing was all they had, and everyone on both sides of the river knew it.

    Arjun's hands had closed. He opened them.

    "Two days," he said. "That's what's left of his deadline. This is him keeping the account current."

    Kai crouched and set the limestone on the table rock. Then he searched a moment at the base of the shelf and came up with a second stone, darker, heavier-looking, flecked with something that caught the light. He weighed one in each hand, and held them out.

    Arjun took them. The dark one was heavier than it had any right to be. When he turned it, the flecks glittered.

    "This one is the valley," Kai said. "This one is the fear. From far enough away, they're both just stones. You can carry both. Most men do. The question is which one you let weigh more."

    Arjun stood with a stone in each hand and the valley below him and the riders strung along the river like beads on a wire. Then he set the stones down side by side on the table rock, the pale and the dark, and left them there.

    At the river, Brandt raised one hand. The line of riders held a moment longer, long enough that the holding was itself the message, and then wheeled and started back north at a walk. Unhurried going down. Unhurried coming back. Every part of it said the same thing. We can do this any morning we choose.

    "They'll come across eventually," Arjun said. "This week. Next. When the show stops working."

    "Yes."

    "And when they do, people will die."

    "Yes," Kai said. "Some things are true from up here too."

    The sun was well up now, the heat beginning to build off the rock. A hawk had found the first thermal of the morning and hung above the ridge, circling, watching the same valley they were watching. Arjun tracked the riders until the fence lines of the north ranch took them in.

    "Why did you come here?" he asked. "This valley. This town. A man with a forge on a cart can set up anywhere."

    Kai was quiet a while. The wind moved through the juniper below the shelf and went still again.

    "I go where the work is," he said. "That's the answer I give when people ask. It's even true."

    He watched the hawk turn on the air.

    "I've been walking longer than you'd believe if I told you. I'm tired of watching. You're the first in a long time I think might not break."

    Arjun turned and studied him. The blacksmith stood on the shelf of rock with his apron over his shoulder and the morning light on his face, and there was nothing in that face to catch hold of, no age, no strangeness, just a dark, quiet man who shod horses for both sides at the same price. A man could hear those three sentences and decide they meant nothing much. Miles walked. Work watched. A stranger's compliment.

    Arjun found he had decided nothing of the kind.

    "Kai."

    "Sun's up," Kai said, and there was the ordinary voice again, the forge voice. "Iron doesn't work itself, and Corby's gray mare has been standing on a bad shoe for two days because her owner is cheap."

    He picked up his apron where it had slipped and settled it on his shoulder, and that was the end of it, closed like a door you weren't sure had been open.

    Arjun let it go. He turned back to the valley instead, and stood taking it in the way a man takes in a piece of ground he may have to fight on, because that was a thing he knew how to do and had been refusing to do for a week. The wash south of the ford. The cutbank. The cottonwoods at the crossing where the road met the water. The distances between the houses, too far to help each other, near enough to hear it happen. Every place a man could hide and every place he couldn't.

    Reyes had seen a canyon that clear and it had killed him. But Reyes had been down in the canyon, and the rims had belonged to somebody else. Maybe that was the difference and maybe it wasn't. Arjun looked at the wash and the cutbank and the cottonwoods, and the fear was still there, all of it, every name it had. It had just stopped filling the whole of him. It sat where the dark stone sat, one thing on the rock, beside another thing.

    "I'm going down," he said.

    "Yes."

    "You coming?"

    "Directly. I'm not done looking."

    Arjun left him on the shelf and started down the way they had come, through the sage and the loose rock, the sun on his back now and the day warming. Partway down he stopped once and turned, and Kai was still at the edge of the rock, small against the sky, facing the valley the way a man faces work he has done before.

    He went on down. The town rose to meet him a piece at a time, the bell tower first, then the roofs, then the streets between them, until the valley he had held whole in his eyes was gone and he was back among the buildings and could smell woodsmoke and hear a door go somewhere and a well handle creak.

    Beatriz was in the street in front of the store, a shawl over her shoulders, looking north at what the buildings would not let her see.

    He walked down the hill and stood where she was standing.

    Chapter Twelve

    Rosa Gutierrez

    The day after Brandt's riders made their show at the river, nothing happened, and the nothing had a weight to it.

    Arjun spent the morning splitting stove wood behind the mercantile and the afternoon mending the corral gate that had sagged since spring. Work kept the hands busy and left the eyes free, and his eyes kept going north, to the road, to the gap in the hills, to the empty country where dust would rise first if riders came. No dust rose. The valley lay still under the heat, and the stillness was its own kind of message. Brandt was letting the fear do the riding for him.

    It was coming on late afternoon when Amos Pfeiffer walked into town.

    Arjun saw him from the corral. The old man came down the middle of the street leading his horse, and there was a shape in the saddle wrapped in his gray coat, small, bent forward over the horn. Amos walked with one hand on the reins and the other raised to steady the rider, bareheaded, his hat hung on the horn where it did no one any good, and he did not speak because there was nothing to say that would not make it worse.

    The town went quiet in front of him the way water goes quiet in front of wind. The woman sweeping in front of the dry goods stopped sweeping. Two men came out of the saloon and took their hats off without appearing to decide to. By the time Amos reached the mercantile, Beatriz was already in the street.

    Arjun came over the corral rail without touching it.

    He got close enough to see, and then wished he had not. Above the collar of the old wool coat was Rosa Gutierrez's face. He knew her. She worked the store three afternoons a week, stocking shelves, sweeping the plank floor, humming songs she only half remembered from the mission school in Santa Fe. Sixteen years old, Miguel and Maria's girl, with her father's wide-set eyes. The eyes were open now and fixed on nothing at all, and she was not crying, and her not crying was the worst thing on that street.

    "Found her on my road," Amos said to Beatriz. His voice was low and worn through. "Coming back from checking the spring. She'd been there a time."

    Beatriz did not ask anything. She reached up and Rosa came down into her arms light as a bird, and Beatriz carried her through the store and into the back room, and the door shut, and that was all the street was given to see.

    Amos stood holding his horse. He had buried both his parents and a brother besides Elijah and he had worked that ground for fifty years, and he stood in the street like a man who had just learned the country all over again.

    "Two of them," he said when Arjun came up beside him. He looked down at his left hand, the three fingers the mowing machine had left him in fifty-nine, and worked them slowly closed. "Sign said two. I didn't stop to read it careful. Her mule's still tied out there. Her goods are scattered in the sage."

    "I'll go."

    "Somebody ought to ride for her folks first."

    "Corby's boy is already gone south. Beatriz sent him from the porch."

    Amos nodded and kept nodding, small, like a pump handle winding down. "I'd take it kindly if you brought that mule in," he said. "It's not the mule's fault. A thing shouldn't be left tied all night for what it had to stand there and hear."

    .

    He rode the buckskin out the wagon road at a lope and the light was long and gold when he came to the cottonwoods at the turn. The road ran along the base of the ridge for two miles before it cut east toward the river, and there were cottonwoods at the turn. There were always cottonwoods at the turn.

    He made himself do it the way Tom Harken had taught him. Thirty yards out, get down, walk a circle, read before you touch. Two horses had stood in the trees long enough to leave sign and a cut branch where one had been tied short. Shod, both, good iron, the near hind of one worn to the inside. They had come down from the north and gone back north, unhurried, riding abreast. Men in no fear of being followed.

    The mule stood where Rosa had tied it, still tied. It had been alone there for hours with what it heard, and it was stiff-legged and white-eyed, and it shied from him twice before it let him have the lead rope. He stood with it a while, his hand on its neck, saying nothing, the way you gentle a thing that cannot tell you what it knows.

    The parcel had been dress goods, a length of calico Maria had put by for Rosa's own first grown dress, wrapped in brown paper. It lay unrolled and dragged across twenty feet of ground, printed flowers in the dirt. The tin of baking powder had rolled into the sage and fetched up against a root, upright, bright as a coin, absurd. He stood over it longer than he meant to. Then he picked it up, and gathered what could be gathered, and rolled the calico as well as calico rolls once it has been in the dirt.

    The rest of the sign he read once and did not read again.

    He rode back slow, leading the mule, and the sun went down behind him. Nothing had been stolen. That was the fact he kept turning over. Men who take a girl's goods are thieves, and a country knows what to do about thieves. Men who leave the goods are saying something, and what they were saying was meant for every house in the south valley, and it had cost Brandt nothing but an afternoon of two men's wages.

    He had no proof. He had two shod horses riding north unhurried through country where nobody else rode that way, and he had the tin in his saddlebag, and he had the mule.

    The lights of town came up out of the blue dark. He rode in past the church, where Father Rojas stood in the open doorway looking north, not moving, his hands empty at his sides.

    .

    Word went the way word goes in a small country. One rider, one lamp lit late, one neighbor standing in another neighbor's yard talking low with his hat in his hands. By full dark the wagons were coming in.

    Arjun was on the mercantile porch when the Gutierrezes came. Miguel first, moving the way he moved now, one shoulder low, each step placed and paid for. Behind him Maria, with Tomás's hand in hers. The boy was ten and trying to walk like his father, which was a hard thing to watch, considering how his father walked.

    Beatriz met them at the door. What passed between the two women passed without a word. Then Maria went in to her daughter, and Miguel did not go in. He lowered himself onto the chair beside the back-room door, in the lamplight, and took a block of cottonwood from his coat pocket and a short knife, and set to work. His hands were the only part of him the beating had left whole. He would be there all night. Anyone wanting through that door would step over him first, and every soul who saw him sitting there understood the arrangement.

    When Maria came out, her face was the face of a woman who has finished her crying somewhere private and locked the room behind her. Beatriz spoke to her quietly, and Arjun, in the doorway, heard the end of it.

    "Laudanum. A small dose, and she's sleeping. I will not leave the door, Maria. I give you my word I will not leave the door."

    "You will leave it for one hour," Maria said. "Miguel keeps it. You are coming to the church. They will need you to say what you know."

    And Beatriz, who took instruction from no one in that valley, glanced once at Miguel settled by the door with the knife and the wood, and went for her shawl.

    She came out onto the porch with it over her shoulders and stopped beside Arjun. She looked ten years tired.

    "They need to see you there," she said.

    It was not a question, and he had learned there was no arguing with Beatriz Vargas.

    .

    The church of San Juan Bautista stood at the south end of town, low adobe, a wooden cross over the door, a bell cast in Mexico City forty years before. It was the only building in Perdido that could hold the five families, and that night it held them all.

    Arjun stood at the back near the door, shoulder against the adobe, and watched them come in. Esteban Martinez down front with Marta, who was carrying again and sat with both hands folded over it, Joaquin solemn beside her, the three younger boys scrubbed and silent. Miriam Cohen with David, seventeen, sitting up very straight. Iris Barkley alone near the middle, small and still; she had left Etta sleeping at the Rivera place, and the Riveras who weren't minding her filled the pew behind. Harmon Bly stood along the side wall, his stiff right arm folded into his left. The Pfeiffers came last, Elijah first for once, and Amos behind him in shirtsleeves, because his coat was in the back room of the mercantile with a stain on the shoulder that would never come out of the wool, and no one in that church needed telling where the coat was.

    Maria Gutierrez sat in the front pew with Tomás against her side. In her right hand she held something small and would not let go of it.

    Kai was not there. Across the dark square the forge glowed through the livery door, one orange point, fixed and steady, and Arjun's eyes kept going to it without his deciding they would.

    Father Rojas stood at the front with his back to the altar. A small man, thin and dark, twenty-three years in Perdido. He had baptized Rosa Gutierrez. He had married her parents. He had buried her grandfather.

    "I have been asked to say words," he said. His voice was thin, but the thick walls kept it. "I have been asked to say words, and I find that I do not have many."

    He looked down at his hands. Small hands, a priest's hands, and they were trembling.

    "Rosa Gutierrez is alive. She will heal. The body heals. It is a gift God gives to the young, and Rosa is young." He paused. "The other things, the things that are not the body, those take longer. Some do not heal at all. Some we carry into the ground with us."

    Miriam Cohen began to cry, softly, and did not try to stop, and David put his arm around his mother and stared straight ahead with his jaw set.

    "I have been your priest for twenty-three years. I have baptized your children and married your sons and daughters and buried your dead. I have heard your confessions and given you penance and told you that God's mercy is infinite and His justice is perfect. I believe this with all my heart."

    He stopped. He looked out at the faces in the pews, and something behind his own face gave way, quietly, the way ground goes out from under a fence post.

    "But I have come to believe something else as well." He set each word down in its place. "I have come to believe that God's mercy and God's justice sometimes require human hands to carry them into the world. I do not know if that is heresy. I find that I do not care. What happened to Rosa Gutierrez did not happen because God willed it. It happened because men did it, and those men have not been stopped, and they will not stop unless someone stops them."

    A sound went through the church, low, out of many throats at once. Esteban Martinez shifted in the front pew.

    Rojas raised his hand.

    "I am not telling you to fight. I cannot tell you to fight. I am a priest. I am a man of peace. The Church teaches that violence is a sin, and I believe the teaching." His voice cracked, and he let it. "But there are things worse than sin. One of them is standing by while evil is done and calling it patience. Another is watching a child suffer and calling it God's will. I will not do these things anymore. I cannot tell you to fight. But I will not tell you not to. I will not stand between you and what your conscience demands. If that is a failure of my office, then I will answer for it gladly."

    He stepped down from the altar, walked to the side of the church, and sat in a plain wooden chair near the confessionals. He did not speak again.

    For a long moment, no one moved.

    Then Maria Gutierrez stood up.

    She was not a large woman. What was in her face was not grief and was not anger. It was the thing that comes when a person has walked through both and out the far side into somewhere colder.

    "My husband," she said. "You all know what was done to my husband. They beat him in the arroyo and he lay there six hours before anyone found him. His ribs were broken. His jaw was broken. He cannot work anymore. He sits in the house all day and carves crucifixes, because it is the only thing left that his hands can do."

    She held up the thing in her right hand. A small wooden cross, rough, the proportions wrong, sanded and sanded again until the cottonwood held a low shine.

    "He made this for me after they hurt him. He said it was to remind us that suffering has meaning." She considered it. Then she set it down on the pew beside her, carefully, the way you set down something that belongs to someone you love and is no longer true.

    "He was not right," she said. "Suffering does not have meaning. What we do about it has meaning. And we have done nothing. Two years we have done nothing. They cut our fences and we mended them. They scattered our cattle and we gathered them. They beat my husband and we prayed. Now they have taken my daughter on a road she has ridden all her life, and we are in this church, talking."

    She turned then, and found Arjun at the back wall.

    "You," she said. "I have heard what they say about you. That you were in the Border Wars. That you know this work. Is it true?"

    Every head in the church came around. He felt the weight of their eyes settle and stay.

    "It's true," he said.

    "Then why do you stand at the back of my daughter's church like a stray dog at a door?"

    "Because I don't belong here."

    "You are here," Maria said. "That means you belong here." She held his eyes a moment longer, then turned away, and the turning was worse than the question. She had not expected an answer. She had stopped expecting anything from anyone.

    Beatriz Vargas rose from the Vargas pew, where her husband's people had sat for three generations. She did not walk to the front. She simply stood, and the church turned to her, because the church always turned to her.

    "You all know what they took from me," she said. "You know what waiting bought me, and what the law bought me, and it comes to four years of nothing. I am done waiting."

    She sat back down. Three sentences, and they landed harder than the sermon, because every soul in those pews had watched her carry it four years and had never once heard her set it down.

    The quiet after her was a different quiet. Not shock. Waiting.

    Esteban Martinez stood. Sixty years old, broad and weathered, hands thick with callus. He had never fired a rifle at anything larger than a coyote. His voice was steady.

    "I do not know about rights," he said. "I do not know philosophy. I know cattle and water, and my family has been on this land longer than anyone in this room, and my father is buried in the ground behind this church. My sons will be buried there too, someday. I do not want that day to be next week."

    He faced the back wall.

    "Three nights ago in this church we asked you to lead us, and you said no. You said you were not the man we were looking for. All right. I am not asking that anymore. I am not asking you to fight my fight for me. I am asking you to show me how to fight it myself. That is different. Is that not different?"

    Arjun studied him. No guile in the man's face. Only the plain, used honesty of a man run clean out of everything but the asking.

    "It's different," Arjun said.

    "Then will you show us?"

    The church held its breath, all of it. Through the open door he could see the forge across the square, the small orange point, and Kai a slow shape moving against it, working, not watching. He was always working.

    Three years, Arjun thought. Three years of walking so that this exact hour could never find him, and it had come up through the floor of a church in a town he had meant to pass through.

    "Before I answer," he said, "you'll hear the terms, because they're not mine to bend. What I'd teach you is not fighting. Not to fight. To defend. You learn that difference before you learn to load, because it's the difference between living with yourself afterward and not. I learned it the most expensive way there is, and you'll take it from me at a discount or not at all."

    "We're listening," Esteban said.

    "You do what I say, when I say it, or you stay home. Nobody rides north. Nobody talks about evening scores. Any man comes to this wanting revenge, I'll send him back to his kitchen, because revenge makes a man stupid, and stupid gets the man beside him killed. What we build is one thing only. A price. We make this valley cost more than Brandt is willing to pay for it."

    "And if he pays it?" Miriam Cohen's eyes were still wet, and her voice had its edge back. "You've counted his riders same as the rest of us."

    "About thirty on his books. Maybe a dozen are gun hands, out of Laredo and El Paso, and those are the ones that matter. The rest draw wages for cattle work. A man who rides for pay will risk his skin. He won't throw it away for another man's feud." He let his eyes travel the pews. "You won't outshoot the dozen, and you don't have to. You have to be steady enough, and together enough, that when they study you across that river they see work instead of sport. That can be done. I have seen it done."

    "Then say it plain," Esteban said. "Yes or no."

    "Yes," Arjun said.

    The word was small in the big room. Nobody cheered. It was not a thing you cheered. But the whole church moved at once, one long breath let out in twenty places.

    "First light," Arjun said. "The dry wash south of the Vargas place, below the river bend. The banks will catch our lead and keep the sound our own business. Bring what rifles you have, and bring them empty. The loading comes after the learning."

    "My boys and I will be there," Esteban said.

    "David and I as well," Miriam said, and when her son turned to her she gave him a level look. "You'll shoot no straighter hungry. I'll see to that end of it too."

    Elijah Pfeiffer cleared his throat. "We're old," he said. "But we shot for the pot before your fathers were weaned, and we can sit a road all night without sleeping, which is more than the young ones can claim." Beside him Amos said nothing, and worked his three fingers slowly closed around the bowl of his cold pipe.

    Then Iris Barkley stood. Small-boned, red hair going dark in the lamplight, and the room quieted for her, because she spoke about as often as it rained.

    "My husband used to say a community that can't protect its own isn't a community at all. It's just people who happen to live near each other." She smoothed her skirt down with both hands. "I don't want to live near people. I want to live among them. I'll be at the wash."

    She sat down again, and that was the end of the talking, because nothing was left over that needed saying.

    They came out of the pews slowly and gathered in the aisle, the Martinezes and the Cohens and the Riveras and the Pfeiffers, Harmon Bly with his folded arm, Maria with her hand on Tomás's shoulder and the little cross left lying on the pew behind her. They did not look saved. They looked like people who had picked up a weight and found they could stand under it, which is a smaller thing than salvation, and more useful.

    Father Rojas watched from his chair by the confessionals and did not move and did not speak. What stood in his face now, Arjun had no word for, though he thought a man could travel a long way on being on the receiving end of it.

    .

    The forge was still lit when Arjun crossed the square. Kai was working, the hour late, iron in the fire, one hand on the bellows.

    "You were watching," Arjun said.

    "I was working."

    "You were watching while you worked."

    Kai drew the iron out and laid it on the anvil and struck, once, twice, three times, each blow ringing high and clear.

    "A girl was hurt. The families came together. You said yes." He turned the iron. "That is what happened."

    "That's all?"

    "That's everything."

    Arjun leaned against the post and watched the sparks climb and die. "Three years ago I swore I'd never touch a gun again. Come morning I put rifles in the hands of farmers."

    "And?"

    "If I do this, people could die. People I teach. People I've eaten with."

    "Yes."

    "That's all you have to say?"

    "The question is not whether people die," Kai said. "The question is whether they stand for something or for nothing. You cannot choose that for them. You can only make their standing worth more."

    "That's no comfort to the dead."

    "No." Kai took up the hammer again. "The dead don't need us. The living do."

    He struck once more, and the note came off the iron deeper than the others, and longer, and it went out over the dark town and did not come back.

    Arjun walked to the barn through the empty square. Behind him the forge burned steady, and in the mercantile a lamp showed at the back-room window, where Beatriz kept her chair, and a girl slept, and an old man sat carving by the door, the shavings curling down white around his boots.

    Chapter Thirteen

    Circle in the Dirt

    The last wagon rolled out of town an hour after the church went dark, and Perdido settled into the kind of quiet that comes after a decision, which is not the same as the quiet that comes before one.

    Arjun lay on his bedroll in the barn with his boots on and did not sleep. The Winchester lay beside him where it had lain for a week now, unloaded, the canvas folded under it. Through the gap beneath the door he watched the forge glow across the square, and he listened to the small sounds of the town letting go of the night: a shutter, a dog, the wind trying the adobe and giving up.

    The glow across the square dimmed, then steadied. After a while there were footsteps in the dust, unhurried, and the soft blowing of animals.

    Kai stood in the doorway with the buckskin mare saddled and his mule on a lead rope. He carried a canteen and no lantern.

    "You said yes tonight," Kai said. "Before you teach a man anything tomorrow, you ought to see what you said yes to."

    Arjun sat up. He had known the shape of the night before a word was spoken. He took his hat off the nail and followed.

    They rode out the north end of town under a moon two days past full. The ridge trail climbed away from the river in long switchbacks, pale where the rock showed through, and the mare took it without fuss, solid on the uncertain ground the way Beatriz had promised. The mule went up behind like it had been born on a grade. Neither man spoke. The air cooled as they climbed, and the smell of the town, dust and smoke and penned animals, fell away below them until there was only juniper and stone and the thin clean edge of the night itself.

    He had stood on this ridge two days before, at dawn, and seen the valley whole for the first time. Night made it a different country. The distances were gone. What remained was light: a scatter of it, small and far apart, and the dark laid down between like deep water.

    They dismounted at the rock shelf and ground-tied the animals. Kai walked to the edge and stood with his hands loose at his sides, and Arjun came and stood beside him, and for a time neither spoke.

    To the north, below the black bulk of the Brandt house, fires burned on the flat.

    Arjun counted them. Nine. He counted again and made it ten, one of them low behind a swell of ground so that only its breathing showed. They were spread wide across the flat, forty, fifty yards between them, each one banked down to a red bed with no flame standing.

    He knew the pattern. He had slept beside a hundred fires laid just that way.

    "They're set to move," he said. "Fires wide so no one bunches. Banked low so a man's eyes stay night-ready. That's a camp that means to ride at first light."

    "Look again," Kai said.

    Arjun looked again. And now he saw what he had counted past the first time. A pale row behind the fires, canvas, catching the moon. Tents, staked square. A picket line strung between two cottonwoods, longer than thirty horses needed. A wagon standing with its tongue down, and beside the nearest fire, cordwood stacked higher than any man stacks it for one night.

    "Tents," he said slowly. "And a woodpile."

    "Those are men who mean to stay awhile," Kai said. "He's hiring."

    Brandt's week had run out the day before, and no answer had gone north, and none had been asked for. This was the answer coming the other way, in canvas and cordwood.

    Arjun stood with that. Somewhere below, a horse on the picket line whinnied, and the sound came up the ridge sharp and small, and no one quieted it. Men at ease. Men being fed and paid and given time to settle in.

    In a way it would have been easier if the fires had meant dawn. A man knows what to do with dawn. This was something else. This was Brandt reaching into Laredo and El Paso and buying patience by the month, and patience was the one thing money bought that the south valley could not.

    "How many, do you figure?" Arjun said.

    "Count the fires."

    "Three men to a fire. Four if they're friendly." He worked it. "He had a dozen real guns on the payroll. Now he's building toward twenty. The rest of his thirty are cattle hands, and cattle hands won't die for another man's feud, but twenty who will is twenty." He heard his own voice go flat and level, the voice he had not used in three years, the counting voice. "Against that I've got Esteban Martinez and his boy. David Cohen, who is seventeen and has shot at coyotes. The Pfeiffer brothers, who between them make a hundred and forty years. Rivera. Iris Barkley, who shoots better than she lets on, if what they say is true. Maybe one or two more when word gets around. The numbers don't work."

    "Numbers never work," Kai said. "If men only fought when the numbers worked, there would be no wars."

    "That's not comforting."

    "It wasn't meant to be."

    Arjun turned south. The lights down there were fewer and dimmer than the fires, and farther apart. A lamp in the Martinez kitchen window, burning late, Esteban and Marta talking it through at the table, most likely, the way married people did the night after a church made up its mind. A single lantern in the Cohen barn on the western bench, where David would be sitting up with the sheep and the shotgun because his mother had told him to sleep and he was seventeen. A thin seam of light under the door of the Barkley cabin.

    As he watched, that door opened. A small figure crossed the yard with a lamp, out to the coop and around it, checking the latch, and stood a moment in the dark yard with the lamp down at her side before she went back in. Iris Barkley, minding her hundred acres in the dark. Her girl would be asleep behind that door. Five years old, and the whole of it moving over her head like weather.

    Beyond the Barkley place, two fires burned a hundred yards apart, one for each Pfeiffer, the same distance the brothers had kept for forty years.

    Five families. A handful of lights. Between them and the Brandt fires lay three miles of open dark and the river running through it, a gray thread where the moon found the water.

    Kai squatted on his heels and picked up a stick. He drew a line in the dirt, a straight furrow running north to south.

    "This is the valley," he said.

    Arjun nodded.

    Kai drew a second line, east to west, crossing the first. "This is the river." He tapped the stick north of the crossing. "Brandt." Then south. "The families."

    He looked up. The moon caught his face strangely, making him look older than he did in town, or younger. Arjun had never been able to fix the man's age. Sometimes Kai moved with the quick economy of a young man. Sometimes he sat still with a patience that felt older than the territory.

    Kai set the point of the stick where the two furrows crossed and drew a circle around the crossing, small, no wider than a man's hand. He tapped the center.

    "That's the field," he said. "Right there. That's where it will happen."

    "How do you know?"

    "Because that's where it always happens. Not on the north range or the south range. Not in the town. At the crossing. Brandt wants the water, and the water is there. The ford is the only crossing for ten miles that a horse takes without swimming. Everything that matters in this valley has always happened at the place where one thing crosses another."

    Arjun studied the marks on the ground. Two furrows and a circle. It could have been anything, a hoofprint, a snake's track, a child's game. It wasn't.

    "You could leave," Kai said.

    It was not a suggestion. It was a question wearing different clothes.

    "I could."

    "But you won't."

    Arjun didn't answer. Below them a Brandt man threw fresh wood on a fire, and the sparks climbed into the dark and went out.

    "What do you get out of this?" Arjun said.

    Kai regarded him.

    "You," Kai said. And then, when Arjun waited: "I get to watch."

    Arjun wanted to argue. He wanted to say he knew exactly who he was, a man who had killed eighteen men and spent three years trying to outwalk them. He knew his name, his history, the weight of the Colt in the trunk, which he had not taken out even the night he lifted the Winchester from above it. He knew all of it.

    But that was not what Kai meant, and they both knew it.

    "You know what I was," Arjun said. "Before."

    "I know."

    "You know how many men."

    "I know the number. I don't know the men."

    "What's the difference?"

    Kai stood. He was not a tall man, but he stood with his weight settled and his shoulders square, and that was enough.

    "The difference is whether you remember their names," he said.

    Arjun said nothing.

    "Do you?"

    "Yes."

    "All of them?"

    "Yes."

    "Then you're not what you were. You're something else. What you were doesn't remember. It counts. It doesn't name."

    Arjun felt his jaw tighten. The eighteen were there in the dark behind his eyes, where they always were. Some had names he had known at the time. Some had names he learned after, asking questions no one understood the reason for. A few he could not look at straight even now.

    "You think remembering makes it better?"

    "I think remembering makes it possible. A man who forgets has no reason to do anything different. A man who remembers might."

    The moon climbed. The fires below shifted as men moved among them, and a horse stamped somewhere on the long picket line, and the sound carried clean across the valley and died.

    Arjun thought about Tom Harken. Tom was down there among those fires, or up at the big house, or riding a dark circle around the herd the way he liked to when he couldn't sleep. Tom had taught him to shoot, to track, to read a lie in the set of a man's shoulders. Tom had been closer than a brother. And Tom had chosen Brandt.

    "Why do men pick the wrong side?" Arjun said.

    Kai followed his gaze north. "They don't think of it as the wrong side. They think of it as their side. There's a difference."

    "He knows what Brandt is."

    "Knowing and admitting are not the same thing. One costs nothing. The other costs everything a man has built his life on."

    Arjun let it lie. There was more there, he could feel the shape of it, the way you feel a stone under a river's surface by the way the water moves. But it was Tom's stone, and no man could lift it for him.

    "Beatriz asked me weeks ago to teach them," Arjun said. "Tonight I finally said I would. And now you show me this." He nodded at the fires. "Twenty guns and a woodpile."

    "Yes."

    "Is that why you brought me up here? To show me it's hopeless?"

    "I brought you up here so you could see the whole field. You've been looking at it from the ground, one fence line at a time, one man at a time. A man can't make a choice he can't see." Kai turned the stick in his fingers. "And no. Those fires are not hopeless. Those fires are time. A man who hires by the month plans by the month. You have weeks now that you did not know you had. What you do with them is the choice."

    Weeks. Arjun ran it forward the way he had once run campaigns forward, and saw mornings at the wash, empty rifles snapping till their hands cramped, the dinner plate at fifty yards, then sixty. It could be done. Not gunmen. But men who would not scatter at the first volley, and that was worth more than gunmen.

    "How do you ask a man to stand in front of that?" he said. "Farmers. A boy of seventeen. Two old men. If it comes to the crossing, some of them die. Good people, who never wanted any of it."

    "You don't ask them to die. You ask them to stand for something. Whether they die is not in your hands."

    "That's easy for you to say. You don't carry a gun."

    Kai looked at him then, and the moonlight was full on his face, and his eyes were dark and still, and he said nothing at all. The silence went on a beat too long, and then another, like a well you drop a stone into and never hear the bottom.

    "What are you?" Arjun said.

    "A blacksmith."

    "That's not what I meant."

    "I know what you meant. It's the answer I'm giving."

    Arjun studied him a moment longer, then let it go. Whatever the man was, Arjun trusted him more than he had trusted anyone since Tom Harken, and that was its own kind of answer, and it would have to do for tonight.

    Kai crouched again by the marks in the dirt. He did not add to them. He simply looked at them, the way a smith looks at iron before he commits the first blow.

    "Tell me about the first time," he said.

    "The first time what?"

    "The first time you picked up a gun knowing you might have to use it."

    Arjun was quiet. The memory was not far down. It was never far down.

    "I was sixteen. My father had a place in south Texas, on the Nueces. Comanche raiders had been hitting the settlements all that spring. My father was away with the wagon. My mother and my sister were in the house." He watched the fires and saw none of them. "I saw their dust an hour before they came. Longest hour of my life. I took the old rifle down and stood on the porch and waited."

    "Did you know what you were doing?"

    "No. I knew I had to stand between them and the door. That was all. I didn't think about killing. I didn't think about anything except the space between them and my family."

    Kai nodded. "That's the difference."

    "What difference?"

    "You know it. You just told it to me."

    Arjun turned his hands over and considered them. Rough from the forge and the fence work, callused where a rifle stock fit. Three years of trying to forget, and the shape of it still in the skin.

    "I was that man once," he said. "At sixteen, on that porch, I was him. Somewhere in the wars I became the other one. I don't know the day it happened. There should be a day, you'd think. A man should at least get to know the day."

    "You were tired, and it was easier to count than to name. That is how it happens to every one of them. Not a day. A slow bleed." Kai rose and brushed the dirt from his hands. "You've been walking three years to get back to that porch. Tomorrow you start teaching other men to stand on it. Teach them what you knew at sixteen and none of what you learned after, and they'll do."

    Arjun almost smiled. "That's a strange way to run a war."

    "It's the only way to keep one from being a war." Kai gathered the mule's lead. "The man who taught me iron told me you cannot beat a thing into shape. You can only beat away everything that is not the shape. Men are the same. What those farmers need is not in them yet. It is in them already. Your work is the removing."

    The wind came up the face of the ridge, cool now, carrying the smell of the Brandt camp, woodsmoke and horses and coffee gone bitter in the pot. A smell Arjun knew from the wars. But under it tonight was rain somewhere far off to the west, a promise the sky might or might not keep, and under that the river, running the way it had run before any of them and would run after.

    He took a last look at the whole of it. The wide fires and the little lights, and the dark between them, and the gray thread of water where one thing crossed another. Then he scuffed nothing, touched nothing, and left the map where Kai had drawn it, the furrows and the circle plain in the moonlight, and went to the mare.

    They rode down the switchbacks in silence, the animals picking their way, the town rising slowly to meet them, small and dark and holding its handful of sleepers. At the trough Kai stripped the mule's lead and turned toward the forge, where the banked coals still breathed the faintest red.

    "I'll be at the barn," Arjun said. "If you need me."

    "I know where you'll be."

    Arjun put the mare up by feel, working in the dark, brushing the dust from her coat until her warmth was in his palms and her breathing had gone long and slow. Then he went in to his corner of the barn.

    The Winchester lay by the bedroll. He picked it up and worked the lever once, twice, the mechanism sliding home with the same smooth click he remembered, muscle and iron finding each other in the dark. He did not load it. He stood it against the wall by the door, where his hand could find it without his eyes, and he lay down on the bedroll with his boots on and his hands folded on his chest.

    In a few hours the families would come to the wash with their rifles out of trunks and closets, and the work would begin. Weeks of it, if Kai was right, and Kai had not yet been wrong.

    Through the open door he could see a single star, bright and low. He watched it until his eyes closed, and when he slept he did not dream of the eighteen men.

    He dreamed of a circle drawn in the dirt, and a stick lying beside it, and a voice that said: This is the field. This is where it will happen.

    Chapter Fourteen

    Volunteers

    They came at first light to the south end of Beatriz's property, where a dry wash cut through the scrub and left a dirt bank tall enough to stop a bullet. Arjun had walked the ground the evening before, checking angles, marking distances with stones. He had done this work in other places. Different country, different faces, same mathematics.

    Seven came that first morning. Esteban Martinez, who was sixty if he was a day and had farmed that valley longer than anyone still breathing, and his son Joaquin, twenty, watchful and close-mouthed. David Cohen, seventeen, with good eyes and a mustache that was still mostly an intention. Rivera, who farmed the quarter east of the Martinez place and had never fired anything heavier than a scattergun at crows. The Pfeiffer brothers, Amos and Elijah, who between them had a hundred and forty years and one bad mowing-machine hand and had teamstered through the war with Mexico. And Iris Barkley, who rode in on a mule with her husband's rifle across the pommel and her red hair tied back and did not ask anyone whether she was welcome.

    They stood in a loose half circle, holding weapons they had pulled from trunks and saddle scabbards and the backs of closets. A Winchester 73 with a cracked stock. A Spencer carbine that had seen the war. A Remington rolling-block that Rivera kept wiping with his sleeve, his thumb going back to the breech every few seconds, unable to stop. Amos Pfeiffer had a Henry rifle older than David Cohen, cleaned so many times the bluing was worn silver at the muzzle.

    Arjun walked the line and examined each weapon. He worked the actions, checked the bores, tested the triggers with his thumb.

    "This one's fouled," he said, handing the Spencer back to Joaquin. "Breech needs scraping. Bring it back clean tomorrow."

    Joaquin nodded once. He did not speak. He was a young man who spent words the way his father spent money.

    "Rest of these will do. The weapon matters less than the man holding it. A clean rifle in a coward's hands is worth less than a rusty one in a man who'll stand."

    Elijah Pfeiffer shifted his weight. "We ain't cowards, Tucker."

    "I didn't say you were. But standing still and standing firm are different things. One's posture. The other's decision. You don't know which you are until the first shot's been fired and you're still there."

    He let that settle. Then he looked down the line at the Pfeiffers, at Amos's three-fingered left hand curled around the Henry's forestock, and said what had to be said.

    "You two could hold the wagon and the water. Nobody would think less."

    Amos took the pipe out of his mouth. He was the quiet one, and when the quiet one spoke, his brother let him.

    "I ain't much for dying in bed," Amos said. "Never saw the appeal."

    Arjun held his gaze a moment. Then he nodded, and that was the end of it.

    "Line up," he said. "We start with what you don't know."

    What they didn't know was nearly everything.

    Esteban could hit a rabbit at thirty yards if the rabbit held still and he had time to squint, and he canted his head sideways when he aimed, which threw everything off. Joaquin was steady but slow, a full ten seconds between settling his sights and squeezing off. In a fight he would be dead before the third count. David had good eyes and quick hands but jerked the trigger, his whole fist closing at once instead of just the finger. Rivera flinched before every shot, his body tensing against the recoil before it came, which sent his rounds high and right every time. The Pfeiffers had the opposite trouble. They fired too fast, old teamster instincts from the Mexican campaign telling them volume mattered more than accuracy.

    Iris Barkley put five rounds inside a spread hand at fifty yards and lowered the rifle and waited for the rest of them to finish.

    Arjun watched them shoot for an hour, standing behind the line with his arms crossed, saying nothing. The dirt bank behind the targets was pocked with strikes, most of them scattered wide. David's grouping was the size of a washtub. Rivera's was worse.

    Amos emptied the Henry and lowered it, breathing hard.

    "Well?" Elijah said.

    "You'd all be dead. Except her."

    Nobody spoke. Elijah studied the bank as if the bank had cheated.

    "Where'd you learn?" Arjun asked her.

    "Nobody taught me. Coyotes were coming for my chickens and my husband was in the ground. You learn or you lose chickens." She fed cartridges into the loading gate without watching her hands. "Got one at two hundred yards from the porch rail last spring. He was the last one that tried."

    "You don't need any of this."

    "No," Iris said. "But they'll shoot better with a woman beating them. Men can't abide it. It's the most useful thing about them."

    Arjun turned back to the line. "David. You're yanking the trigger. Your finger moves alone or nothing works. Watch." He took the boy's Winchester and raised it to his shoulder. He didn't fire. He showed the motion, the slow, steady squeeze, the knuckle curling by itself. "The rifle should surprise you when it goes off. If you know the exact instant, you're doing it wrong."

    He handed the Winchester back.

    "Rivera. You're fighting the gun before it fights you. The recoil is going to happen. You can't stop it. Let it come."

    Rivera nodded, pale. "I don't care for guns much."

    "Neither do the Brandt men you'll be pointing that at. Doesn't make the bullet go around you."

    He turned to Joaquin. "You're steady and you're calm. That's better than good. But you're taking too long. Speed and accuracy. You need both. You can't trade one for the other."

    Joaquin considered it the way he considered everything, like a man checking a fence post. "I'll work on it."

    "I know you will."

    Arjun stepped back and addressed all of them. "Here's what you're going to do. Every morning, same time, same place. You're going to snap that trigger on an empty chamber a hundred times before you load a single round, until your finger knows the letoff without your head getting involved. Then you'll shoot twenty rounds, slow. I want every shot inside a dinner plate at fifty yards. When you can do that, we move the targets back. When you can't, you go back to the empty chamber."

    "Fifty yards," Esteban said. "That's a fair distance."

    "It's nothing. A man on horseback covers fifty yards in six seconds. If you can't hit a dinner plate at that range, you can't stop a rider."

    He let them chew on that. Beyond the wash the valley ran north toward the Brandt land, pale green and brown in the summer heat. Somewhere on that range Brandt's hired men were doing their own practicing. They would not be starting from an empty chamber.

    "One more thing," Arjun said. "If you're doing this for revenge, leave now. Revenge will make you stupid. It'll make you take risks you shouldn't take, shoot before you're ready, stand when you should move. I'm not teaching you to kill men. I'm teaching you to protect what's yours. If you can't tell the difference, my time's wasted and so is yours."

    He read each of them in turn.

    "Anybody feel like they're in the wrong place?"

    Somewhere a meadowlark called, then stopped. Nobody left.

    "Then reload. We go again."

    .

    On the second morning, Tomás Gutierrez was standing at the end of the line.

    He was ten years old and he had walked four miles carrying his father's old single-shot, which was nearly as long as he was. He had his father's jaw, the one that was still wired half shut, and he stood the way boys stand when they have decided to be men by force of will alone.

    "Go home, Tomás."

    "My father can't hold a rifle. Somebody from our place has to."

    "Somebody from your place is your mother and your sister, and they need you whole." Arjun crouched to his level. "You'll be a man in this valley a long time. Not this month."

    The boy's face went through several things and settled on the last one, which was hatred, clean and bright as new tin. He turned and walked back up the road stiff as a ramrod, the long gun over his shoulder, and he did not look back once. Arjun watched him the whole way. There was nothing to say that the boy would believe for years yet.

    .

    At midmorning Arjun went up to the store for the water bucket. He was at the corner of the porch, at the rail, when he heard them. Kai was shoeing Father Rojas's bay mare in the shade of the livery wall, close enough that his hammer had been keeping time with the rifle fire all morning, and Beatriz had come out with a pitcher.

    "He's good at that," she said.

    "He's had practice." Kai did not look up from the mare's hoof.

    "At teaching, or at killing?"

    The hammer paused. "Those are the same trade, if you follow it far enough. Teaching a man to shoot is teaching him when not to. The second is harder than the first."

    "Did you teach him that?"

    "No," Kai said. "I reminded him he already knew."

    The hammer took up its rhythm again. Arjun stood with the empty bucket and did not announce himself, and when Beatriz went back inside he filled the bucket at the trough and carried it down to the wash, and if Kai knew he had been standing there, and he probably did, he gave no sign but a half turn of his head as Arjun passed, the kind a man gives the weather.

    At noon the men broke for water. They came up gray with dust, their shoulders powdered where the stocks had rested. Rivera sat down heavily on the bank and stared at his hands.

    "They're shaking," he said.

    "They'll stop. That's nerves burning off. Happens to everyone the first days."

    "Did it happen to you?"

    Arjun drank before he answered. "No. I was too young to be nervous the first time. That came later."

    David was watching him with the particular intensity of a boy who has just understood that a grown man has a past. "You've killed men," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

    "Yes. That's why I'm teaching you and not leading you. A man who's killed knows the cost. A man who hasn't thinks it's cheaper than it is."

    David looked at his own thumb on the hammer of his rifle and said nothing more, and that was the right answer.

    "Enough rest. Back to the line."

    .

    By the fourth day the groupings were tightening.

    David had stopped jerking the trigger. Rivera had stopped flinching, mostly. Joaquin had cut his time to under five seconds. The Pfeiffers were learning patience, though Elijah muttered about damned standing-around every time Arjun made them wait between volleys, and Amos smoked his pipe through the waits like a man who had never been in a hurry in his life.

    Esteban was the surprise. Poor eyes, the sideways cant, everything against him. But he was steady, and he was stubborn, and Arjun worked with him two extra hours on the third afternoon, squaring his cheek to the stock, teaching him to trust the front sight and quit hunting detail downrange.

    "You don't need to read the brand on the cow," Arjun told him. "You just need to know it's a cow."

    By the fifth day Esteban was putting eight of ten inside the plate at sixty yards, and Joaquin was watching his father shoot with something on his face that had not been there before.

    The cracks came at regular intervals now, not the scattered popping of the first morning. It wasn't marksmanship yet. But it was competence, and competence was the whole secret. A frightened man with the finest rifle ever made was less dangerous than a calm one with a rusty carbine. Fear made you stupid, and stupid got you killed.

    Arjun had spent three years trying not to think about guns. Now he spent every morning squaring cheeks to stocks and gentling trigger fingers, and the strange part was how natural it felt. His body remembered. His hands knew the weight of a rifle before his mind caught up. The old knowledge was still there, waiting under the silence he had wrapped around it, patient as water under ice.

    .

    On the sixth morning, Tom Harken rode in.

    He came alone, on the claybank, and he stopped on the rise above the wash where the road curved east toward the Brandt range. He didn't come down. He sat his horse and watched, the turned-up brim of his hat sharp against the sky.

    Arjun saw him at once. He had been waiting for this. Not Tom in particular, but someone. Brandt was not a man to ignore a thing growing at his border.

    "Keep shooting," Arjun said. "Don't look at him. Don't give him the satisfaction."

    The men kept shooting. Their groupings widened some, they knew they were watched, but they held. Arjun had told them this would come. The real test was never the drill, it was the drill with an enemy's eyes on it. A man alone in a wash who could hit a target had learned a skill. The same man hitting it while Brandt's foreman watched had learned to fight.

    Tom sat motionless on the claybank. A hundred and fifty yards, close enough to read posture, too far to talk without shouting. Arjun kept him in the corner of his eye the way you keep a rattlesnake there while you work.

    The sun climbed. The rifles cracked. The dust rose and settled.

    Then Tom turned the claybank and rode north, slow and unhurried, back toward Brandt land. He had not said one word, and he had not needed to.

    Amos lowered his Henry. "He'll tell them."

    "He was always going to tell them. Brandt's known since the first shot."

    "Then why send Harken?" Elijah said.

    "Because he wanted me to know he's watching. And he wanted Tom to see for himself who's standing on this side of the line."

    Rivera wiped his forehead. "What do you figure Harken saw?"

    "Six men who can shoot. A woman who shoots better. And one who taught them."

    .

    That evening Arjun walked to the forge.

    Kai was working late, drawing out a bar for wagon hinges. The night was coming in cool off the mountains, carrying pinon smoke and the smell of distant rain, and thunder muttered somewhere up the valley and didn't commit. Arjun leaned against the doorframe and watched him work. The hammer moved the way it always moved, unhurried, each stroke landing where it was meant to.

    "Harken came by today," Arjun said.

    "I saw."

    "You didn't come out."

    "No need. He wasn't here for me."

    Kai drew the bar from the fire and laid it across the anvil. One end of it was dark, barely warmed. He tapped the dark end with the hammer, a ringing little blow that did nothing at all.

    "Cold iron," he said. "Hit it all day. It won't move. It'll dull your hammer and crack before it bends."

    He turned the bar. The tip was yellow-white, spitting small stars.

    "Too hot. It'll go anywhere you push it, and it'll go places you didn't push it. It splits. It burns. It moves stupid."

    Between the two, a hand's width of the bar glowed orange, deep and even as a coal.

    "There," Kai said. "Working heat. It takes the strike it needs. No more and no less."

    He set the orange section over the horn and struck twice, and the iron bent smooth and clean, exactly as far as the hammer asked.

    Arjun watched the colors fade. "You're not talking about iron."

    "I'm always talking about iron." Kai quenched the bar, and the steam went up between them. "But a man heats the same way. Cold, he won't act at all. He knows what's right and he sits in it. Burning, he acts, and ruins what he touches."

    "I've been both," Arjun said. The words came before he had decided on them. "There was a night in the wars, on the stage road out of Santa Fe. Three men came at us and I shot two of them out of their saddles before the driver had the team stopped. They lived, the pair of them, and that was luck and nothing else, because I wasn't clear. I was burning. The old heat came up and it felt good, and that was the worst part. I was sick for a week after." He paused. "And since I quit, I've been the cold bar. Three years of knowing what's right and sitting in it."

    "And now?"

    "Now I don't know what color I am."

    Kai laid the finished hinge on the bench. "The gate won't care. It'll swing straight or it won't. That's how you'll know."

    He began banking the coals for the night, raking gray ash over the red.

    "Go sleep," Kai said. "Tomorrow you'll teach them again."

    .

    The second week brought rain.

    It came in hard from the west, a gray wall of water that turned the wash to a broad brown stream and sent everyone into Beatriz's barn. Arjun kept them working, snapping empty rifles in the hay-sweet dimness while the rain hammered the tin roof and the horses stamped in their stalls.

    "Trigger squeeze. Again. Again. Do it until your hand cramps and then do it ten more. Your body learns slower than your head, but it learns truer."

    David's hand was shaking after the first hour. Arjun made him switch to his left.

    "If your right arm gets hit, you shoot with the other one. You won't hit much, but you'll hit enough to keep a man's head down while you get to cover."

    "Has your right arm ever been hit?"

    "Twice. The second time, I was glad I'd practiced."

    The rain fell two days. When it cleared, the valley was washed green and the arroyos ran the color of coffee with too much milk, and the air smelled new. Arjun moved the targets to seventy-five yards. The men groaned. Elijah swore. Iris moved her mule to the shade and waited. But they lined up and shot, and by the end of that week even Rivera could put eight of ten inside the plate at the new distance.

    Something had changed in the town, too. People had been holding their breath since the wire was first cut, moving through their days with their shoulders up and their eyes down. Now the gunfire from the south wash had become a fixed point in the day, regular as a church bell, and folks had stopped flinching when they heard it. They set their work by it instead.

    "A man stopped by the store yesterday," Beatriz told him one evening on the porch. "Harmon Bly. You know him?"

    "The wheelwright."

    "He wants to join the drills. He's sixty and his right arm doesn't straighten all the way, from when a wagon tongue broke on him years back."

    "I'll take him. If he can hold a rifle, he can learn."

    "You're building something. You know that?"

    "I'm teaching men to shoot."

    "You're giving them a reason to stand up. That's different." The evening light came through the store window and found the gray at her temples. She had been alone in this fight longer than anyone, and it showed in her the way weather shows in wood, worn, not used up.

    "Why didn't you leave?" he asked. "After. You could have sold the store, gone east. Nobody would have blamed you."

    "I thought about it. Had the papers drawn up and everything." She was quiet a moment. "But leaving would have meant he was right. That he could take what he wanted and the only answer was to run. I couldn't give him that."

    "So you stayed."

    "I stayed. And I waited. I didn't know for what. Maybe just for someone to say it was wrong and mean it." She turned and faced him square, and there was nothing soft in it. "Understand me, though. I don't want him dead for my sake. I want you to make it so a man like him can't do what he does. Here or anywhere he goes next. That's a different thing than revenge, and I need you to know I know the difference."

    "You'd have made a fair sergeant."

    "I'd have made a fair lot of things," Beatriz said, and went inside.

    Bly came the next morning, a thick-shouldered old man with an arm that stopped a hand short of straight. He couldn't raise a rifle to his shoulder the ordinary way, so Arjun taught him to shoot from a rest, the barrel laid across the crook of the bad arm, and off a wagon box or a cutbank Bly was as good as any of them. In a fight there would be wagon boxes and cutbanks. Arjun had already seen to that. All that week, between shooting, he had them digging into the wash, cutting a shelf in the cutbank, dragging the old freight wagon into the brush above the crossing and sighting from behind it, until every man knew which piece of ground was his and could get to it in the dark.

    .

    Tom Harken came back on a Tuesday, the third week.

    This time he didn't stay on the rise. He rode down into the yard while the men were shooting and dismounted by the water trough, and he dropped the claybank's reins in the dust and the horse stood, head low, still as a hitching post. Tom never tied his horse. He never had to.

    Arjun told the men to keep shooting and walked over.

    "Tom."

    "Arjun."

    Up close he looked older than the war years, the iron-gray mustache, the lines around his mouth that hadn't been there at Matamoros. Riding for Brandt was wearing on him. Or riding away from what he used to believe was.

    "Brandt wants a parley," Tom said.

    "He knows where to find me."

    "Neutral ground. The church, noon tomorrow. Father Rojas will hold it. Judge Whitcomb will be there too."

    "What's he offering?"

    "He didn't tell me. I'm just the messenger."

    "You're never just the messenger, Tom. You're too smart for that."

    Tom's jaw tightened. He glanced past Arjun toward the wash, where the shooting had stopped and the line stood watching. David Cohen had his Winchester lowered, finger resting on the guard, ready without threatening, the way Arjun had taught him. A small distinction, and Tom read it in one pass, because Tom read everything in one pass.

    "You've got them looking like soldiers. Almost."

    "They're farmers and shopkeepers."

    "I know what they are." He came back to Arjun. "You're going to get them killed."

    "Maybe. But they'll die standing up. That's more than Brandt was offering."

    "You should take Beatriz and go. He'd let you."

    "Brandt doesn't let people do anything. He permits it until it's inconvenient."

    Tom looked at the ground a moment, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quieter. "Why you? You walked away clean. Three years. Nobody walks away clean and then walks back for strangers. It's not owed."

    "You never owed anybody anything either," Arjun said. "That's your song. You owe one man, so you owe nothing to anyone else."

    "That's not what I said."

    "It's what you live." Arjun let the shooting silence stretch behind them. "Wrong, anyway. I owe everybody everything. That's why I'm still here."

    Tom stared at him a moment out of that lean, watchful face, and whatever was working behind it did not come out. He gathered the reins and swung into the saddle in one motion, smooth and economical, nothing wasted, the way he had once taught a sixteen-year-old to mount under fire.

    "Noon tomorrow," he said. "The church."

    "He's had four years to talk."

    "He's ready now."

    "That's what worries me."

    Tom turned the claybank and rode north and did not look back. The men at the wash watched him go. From behind the livery, Kai's hammer never paused.

    .

    That night Arjun sat on the porch of the store and cleaned the Winchester.

    It was a ritual from the war years and he had never broken it, even in the three years he hadn't fired a shot. Take the weapon apart. Clean every piece. Put it back together. Slow, methodical, the same order every time. By the end your hands were steady and your breathing was slow and you knew what you were going to do.

    Beatriz came out and sat beside him. For a while she just watched his hands move over the action, lifting away carbon, laying oil down thin.

    "You think he means it?" she said. "The parley."

    "I think Brandt means whatever serves Brandt. Right now that's keeping me off balance while he counts what we are."

    "And what are we?"

    He slid the bolt home and worked the lever, twice. No catch, no grit, no drag. "More than we were three weeks ago. Less than we need to be."

    "Is that enough?"

    "It's what we've got."

    She nodded and looked out at the dark street. "Rafael used to say the only wrong choice was the one you didn't make. He wasn't a philosopher. Just a man who'd rather be wrong than frozen. Not a fighter. He believed in things instead. Fairness. Standing by your word." Her voice caught, barely, and steadied. "It got him killed."

    "No. Silas Brandt got him killed. Your husband being a good man is a separate thing. Don't let them run together."

    She studied him in the starlight. "You really believe that."

    "I have to. Otherwise the wrong people win twice."

    She stood, and paused at the door. "You're not much for comfort, are you."

    "I'm out of practice."

    He heard the bolt slide, and her steps on the stairs, and then the town was quiet. He stayed on the porch with the rifle across his knees. Tomorrow at noon Brandt would offer something, money or terms or a peace that was just surrender with better manners, and he would have to answer. He thought of the bar on the anvil, the dark end and the burning end and the narrow band of orange between them that took the strike it needed and held its shape after.

    He sat a long while with that, and the stars wheeled slow over the street, and the rifle lay quiet across his knees, ready without asking anything of him yet.

    Chapter Fifteen

    Banked Fire

    The forge had been banked for hours, but the heat still came off the stones in slow waves. Arjun felt it on his face as he crossed the yard behind the livery. The moon was up, thin as a skinning knife, laying blue light over the anvil, the slack tub, the rows of finished shoes on their pegs.

    He had not meant to come. He had eaten supper at the store and gone over the morning with Beatriz, who would walk to the church with him at noon, and then he had gone to his bedroll in the barn and lain there listening to the wind work at the boards until lying there was worse than walking. The day behind him had been powder smoke and the dry snap of empty rifles and Elijah Pfeiffer arguing with his brother across a hundred yards of bench land. The day ahead of him was a table in a church and Silas Brandt's smile across it. Between the two days there was this, the road out past the livery, and his own feet taking it without being asked.

    Kai sat on an upturned bucket with his back against the forge stones, a tin cup in his hand, watching the dark settle into the arroyo beyond the last buildings of town. He did not turn when Arjun came through the gap in the corral fence, and he did not turn when Arjun stopped six feet off and stood in the open, which was a thing Arjun never did. But the town was asleep, the valley was holding its breath, and tonight the old habits seemed like clothes cut for another man.

    "Sit," Kai said.

    Arjun did not sit. Whatever had walked him out here had been building for weeks. Every question that was not a question. Every time he had walked away from this yard and found himself walking back.

    "I've done what you said," he told him. "Taught those men. Stood where I said I'd stand. Tomorrow I sit down across from Brandt in a church and speak for people I didn't know two months ago." He stopped, brought his voice down. "And I still don't know who you are."

    The forge stones ticked, cooling. Out in the dark a coyote called once and nothing answered it.

    Kai drank from the cup and set it on the packed earth beside the bucket. "Has anything I've said been wrong?"

    "No."

    "Has anything I've told you to do made things worse?"

    "No. But that's not the question."

    "No," Kai said. "The question is who's saying it. Not what's said."

    Arjun stood there. It was the right question, and Kai had set it down the way he set down everything, bare on the air between them, no ornament to it.

    "Where I come from, a man who won't give his name has something to hide."

    "I've been here every day for two months," Kai said. "I've worked iron in front of the whole town. I've answered every question you've asked except the ones you weren't ready for."

    "I'm ready now."

    Kai looked up at him for the first time. His face was hard to put an age to, anywhere from thirty-five to fifty-five, the kind of face the desert makes when it has had time to work. His eyes were older than the face. He studied Arjun a moment longer, then rose from the bucket and went to the scrap pile beside the anvil.

    He came back with a piece of iron in his hand. A broken chain link, rusted, sprung open at the weld. He turned it over once in the moonlight, the way a man turns a coin to see if it rings true, and held it out. Arjun took it. Heavier than it looked. The rust was rough against his palm, and the break in it was old, the metal gone dark all the way through the wound.

    "That was good iron once," Kai said. "Somebody made it to hold something together. A gate. A trace. Then it broke, and now it sits in a pile waiting to be melted down. The iron doesn't know that. It just waits."

    "Men aren't iron."

    "No. Men get to choose what they're made into next." Kai bent to the pile again and came up with a second link, the mate of the first, broken the same way. He set it on the anvil face. "A man can say, I was a killer, and that's true. And I'll be something else now, and that's true too. He doesn't get to pick one. He carries both."

    Arjun looked at the link in his hand. Somewhere under the rust was the bright iron it had been, and the bright iron it might be again, and neither one was visible from here.

    "I'm afraid," he said.

    The words came out before he could stop them. He had never said them to anyone. He let them stand.

    "Good," Kai said.

    "Good."

    "If you weren't, you'd be a fool or a liar. Fear is the body knowing what's coming. Courage is standing anyway."

    "That's what you've been telling me."

    "That's what I've been showing you. Every horse that comes into this yard smells the fire and feels the iron coming. The ones that trust me stand. They walk out better shod than they walked in."

    "I'm not a horse."

    "No," Kai said. "You're harder to shoe."

    The surprise of it caught Arjun off guard. He had never once heard the man joke. He almost smiled, and the yard felt different afterward, the silence lighter than the one before.

    He walked to the slack tub and stood over it. The water was black in the moonlight, still and depthless, and his own face floated on it, faint and broken by the surface.

    "In the wars I was fast and I was accurate," he said. "For a while that was who I was. The man you sent for when you needed a problem to go away. Then one morning I looked at my hands and didn't know whose they were. So I stopped. Put the revolver in a trunk and put the trunk in a corner and went to work. Freight. Horses. Fence lines. I told myself that if I quit the killing, the killing inside me would quit too."

    "But it didn't."

    "No."

    "Because it was never in the gun," Kai said. "It's in what you saw and can't forget. The gun was a tool. The willingness was the weapon, and you've been trying to fit the willingness in that trunk for three years, and it won't go."

    Arjun turned from the tub. His hands hung at his sides, and they were steady. They had always been steady. That was part of the problem.

    "You asked me once what kind of man I wanted to be," he said. "That first week. I didn't answer."

    "I remember."

    "The kind who stands. Not the kind who kills. The kind who stands, for something, with someone, in a place. Beatriz has stood alone four years. The families have been standing apart, which is not the same thing. Rojas stands in his church praying for men who won't pray for themselves." He stopped there. The judge's name was in the sentence somewhere and he left it out.

    Kai took that in without comment. What he did was pick up the small hammer from the anvil's step and hang it back on its peg, squaring it with the others, a man closing up shop.

    Arjun set his link on the anvil beside its mate. Two broken halves of something that had held. The moon had moved while they talked; the anvil stood in full light now, black iron on pale dirt.

    "The men I've been teaching," he said. "They're not ready."

    "They're readier than they were."

    "Iris shoots better than any of them and she'd tell you so. David Cohen flinches before the hammer falls. Esteban thinks glaring at the target will bring it down. Harmon's arm won't straighten and he came anyway." Arjun shook his head slowly. "They've never had a man shoot back at them. Some of them could die."

    "Some of them could." Kai looked out at the dark line of the ridge. "But dying isn't the worst thing on the table. The worst is living to be old knowing they didn't stand. I've watched that. It's slower."

    Arjun thought of Tom Harken, who was not dead, though something in him was. Tom had brought the summons himself, standing by the trough with the claybank's reins dropped in the dust, saying the words like a man reading them off a paper someone else had written. Noon. The church. The judge and the priest to witness. He had mounted and ridden north without hurrying, and there had been nothing in the set of his shoulders a stranger could have read. Arjun was not a stranger. He let the thought come and let it go.

    "Tomorrow, in that church," he said. "Brandt will offer something that sounds like peace. And the families will look at me. And I keep asking myself what happens if I'm not the man they think they're looking at."

    "What are you afraid of? Name it."

    "Failing them. Getting them killed. Coming up short."

    "That's three things," Kai said. "Which one keeps you up at night? Them dying, or you coming up short?"

    Arjun stood very still. The question worked through him the way Kai's questions always did, not like a blade but like water, finding the cracks.

    "The second one."

    Kai picked up the cup from beside the bucket and stood turning it in his hands, and when he spoke it was without weight, the way a man mentions weather. "What I told you on the ridge holds."

    "You don't know that."

    "I've been wrong before. Not often."

    The coyote called again, closer. The heat was nearly gone from the stones now, and the deep cold that comes before dawn in high country was moving into the yard. Arjun pulled his coat closed. He had come out here for an answer and he had not gotten it, and he understood that if he walked back down the road without asking again he never would.

    "You never answered me," he said. "Who you are."

    Kai ran his hand over the anvil's face, tracing the dents where the hammer had come down a thousand times. The iron was cold. When he looked up his eyes were steady and there was no deflection in them, none of the sidestep Arjun had learned to expect. Whatever this was, it was the answer, the only one there would be.

    "I'm the man who was here when you came," Kai said. "The man who'll be here when Brandt's riders come down from the north. I've asked you the questions you didn't want, and I'll ask more before this is done. Names tell you where a man came from. They don't tell you what he's for."

    "Then tell me what you're for."

    Kai picked up the tin cup and drained the last of the water. He looked at Arjun across the anvil, across the two broken links lying on the cold iron, and there was nothing in his face but plain fact, the way a man says his horse is a mule or the river runs south.

    "I'm yours," Kai said.

    He set the cup down on the anvil between the two links, iron ringing faintly on iron, and walked to the livery where his bedroll lay in the hay, and was gone into the dark of the doorway.

    Arjun stood alone in the yard. The moon was almost to the ridge. He did not understand the words and he understood them completely, and both things were true at once, which was a thing that had started happening since Kai came and had never happened before.

    After a while he turned and walked back down the road toward town. The dust of it was cold underfoot and pale in the last of the moonlight, and his boots made the only sound in the valley. He passed the corral, where the horses stirred and blew softly and went still again. He passed the well. A light was burning in the window over the store, and he stood a moment in the street below it without knowing why, and then went on to the barn and his bedroll.

    He did not sleep. He lay with his eyes open and heard the wind pick up out of the north, working at the shutters, carrying the first grit of tomorrow's weather. Behind him in the dark yard the forge sat quiet, and the tin cup stood on the anvil between the two broken links, on the cold iron, waiting for the fire.

    Chapter Sixteen

    Wind from the North

    Silas Brandt came to the parley in his town suit.

    He arrived at noon exactly, which was its own kind of statement, riding the blood bay down the center of the street at a walk with two riders behind him. The riders stayed with the horses. Brandt came up the church steps alone, took off his hat at the door the way a man does who was raised right and wants you to remember it, and walked in among the pews like he was thinking of buying them.

    Father Rojas stood at the front, not at the altar but below it, on the level floor. He had set out chairs in a rough half circle, and the families had filled them by half past eleven. Esteban Martinez sat in the middle with his hat on his knee. Miriam Cohen sat to his left with her arms folded. Iris Barkley had taken the end chair, nearest the door, and Elijah Pfeiffer sat beside his brother Amos, who was working an unlit pipe around in his fingers because there was no smoking in the church and his hands did not accept the ruling. Beatriz Vargas sat apart from the half circle, by the wall, in the pew where she sat on Sundays.

    Judge Whitcomb had come too. He sat in the last pew but one, spectacles folded in his hand, and he had not spoken to anyone.

    Arjun stood at the back, by the door, where he could see the whole room and the street through the gap. He wore no gun. Neither did anyone else inside the church. That had been Rojas's one condition, and even Brandt had honored it, or appeared to.

    "Mr. Brandt," Rojas said. "You asked for this meeting. It is yours."

    "Thank you, Father." Brandt set his hat on the front pew, crown down, careful of it. He turned and faced the half circle, and he took his time about it, letting his eyes rest on each face the way a man counts cattle he expects to own. "I want to say first that I'm glad you came. All of you. It speaks well of the valley that its people will still sit in a room together."

    Nobody answered him.

    "I'll be plain, because you're plain people and you'd smell anything else." He clasped his hands in front of him. "There's been trouble. Fences. A fire. Other things. I regret every bit of it, and I won't insult you by pretending my men had no part. Hard country draws hard men, and hard men get ahead of their orders. That's a thing I mean to correct." He paused. "But the trouble isn't the disease. It's the fever. The disease is that this valley is divided against itself, and a divided valley is a poor one. I'm here to offer the cure."

    "Here it comes," Miriam Cohen said, to no one.

    Brandt smiled at her, unbothered. "I will buy your parcels. Every one, at a price I'll put on paper today. It's not what you'd call a rich price, but it's an honest one, more than the bank would give you, and the bank is the other buyer, sooner or later, for some of you. You keep your houses. You stay on the land, those who want to. You work it as you work it now, except the water is managed from one head, sensibly, for the whole valley, and the quarrels are done. Wages for any man who wants them. A cooperative arrangement." He let the phrase sit out where they could all see it. "Your children grow up in their own beds. Nobody buries anybody. That's the whole of it."

    The wind had begun outside, the first of it, a low push against the north wall of the church, and the building ticked and settled under it.

    "And the price," Esteban Martinez said, "is yours to set."

    "The price is fair."

    "You said honest. Then you said fair. You have not said what it is."

    "It's on paper, Mr. Martinez. You'll have it before I leave."

    Iris Barkley spoke from the end chair without uncrossing her arms. "My husband's buried on that hundred acres. Does your paper say what he goes for?"

    "Mrs. Barkley, nobody is asking you to leave your land."

    "No. You're asking me to start paying you for it. There's a difference between a home and a tenancy, Mr. Brandt, and I know which one I've got, and I know which one you're selling."

    "What I am selling," Brandt said, and for the first time there was iron under the cloth of his voice, "is the only version of next year in which everyone in this room is alive to complain about it."

    The room went still. Amos Pfeiffer's pipe stopped moving in his fingers.

    Judge Whitcomb rose from the back pew. He put his spectacles on. He looked older standing than sitting, and his hands were not quite steady on the pew rail.

    "Gentlemen," he said. "It seems to me that there is perhaps a middle course. Some form of arbitration. A schedule."

    The sentence went out from under him. He stood there while it failed, a man on ice hearing the first crack, and then he took the spectacles off again and folded them and sat down, and he did not try any more sentences.

    Brandt did not even glance at him. That was the cruelest thing in the room, and Arjun marked it. A man he had bothered to break, he would at least have hated.

    Beatriz stood up in her pew. She did not raise her voice. She had never needed to.

    "I want to be sure we understand the offer," she said. "You cut our fences, and burned Iris's hay, and beat Miguel Gutierrez so that he walks the way he walks now. You did to Rosa what was done to Rosa. And now you come in a good suit and offer to make it stop, at a price you set, on land we already own, with water that has run with the Vargas grant for a hundred years. You are asking us to pay you for the rope."

    "Mrs. Vargas," Brandt said, gently, "I had hoped you of all people would want no more funerals."

    "You are the only man in this valley who decides whether there are funerals," Beatriz said. "You have just told us so. That is the whole of your offer, whatever the paper says."

    Brandt studied her for a moment. Then he bent and picked up his hat, and squared it on his head, and the manners came back down over him like a shade drawn on a window.

    "I'll leave the paper with Father Rojas. The offer stands until sundown tomorrow. After that, I stop being the buyer." He turned to the room once more. "I came here in a suit, to a church, with my hat off. Remember that I did."

    He walked down the aisle toward the door. The families sat where they were. At the back, level with Arjun, he stopped. Up close the good cloth smelled of cedar and horse, and his eyes were mild and pale and did not match the rest of him.

    "You had your chance to be rich, Mr. Tucker."

    "I've been rich. It didn't take."

    Brandt looked at him a heartbeat longer, the way a man reads a brand he can't place. Then he went out and down the steps, and the wind took the door out of Rojas's hand and shut it for him.

    Nobody said anything. Through the window Arjun watched the three horses go up the street at a walk, unhurried, the blood bay's tail streaming sideways in the wind, and he understood that the last talking was done.

    .

    The wind came down from the north the rest of that day, carrying grit and the smell of dry sage. It pushed through the streets of Perdido in gusts that rattled shutters and lifted dust off the road, and the few people still abroad pulled their collars close and hurried. It had been warm that morning. Now it was cold, and it meant something.

    Elijah Pfeiffer rode in from the north road an hour before dusk, his hat tied down with a bandanna, and what he said he said once, in the store, with the door bolted behind him.

    "Twenty that I counted, and that's grown since Tucker saw the camp from the ridge. Riders, not hands. The last of them came down from the north range this morning, and the remuda's been grained and shod." He accepted the cup Beatriz put in his hand and drank it standing. "He wasn't waiting on our answer. He was waiting on his men."

    "The offer until sundown tomorrow," Beatriz said.

    "The offer was so he could say he made it." Elijah set the cup down. "I'll tell the others."

    He went back out into the wind. Beatriz stood at the window and watched him go. She had not sat down since noon.

    Arjun was behind the counter with the Colt broken open on a cloth, working a cleaning rod through the barrel. She hadn't asked him to. She hadn't asked him not to.

    "What did you make for them?" he asked.

    "Beans and salt pork. All I had that would travel. Miriam sent bread." She turned from the window. "They're not soldiers, Arjun."

    "No."

    "David Cohen is seventeen. He'll stand with the men tomorrow and his mother knows better than to forbid it and she hasn't stopped moving since noon. Cleaning things that are clean."

    The cleaning rod moved steady in his hands.

    "You could tell him to stay home," she said.

    "I could."

    "Will you?"

    He set the Colt down on the cloth. "No. If I tell him to stay home, he stays home. And if Brandt wins, the boy lives the rest of his life knowing he hid while it happened. That's a long time to carry something."

    "And if he's killed?"

    "Then he's killed. But it will have been his choice, and he's old enough to make it. His mother knows that too. That's why she's cleaning."

    Beatriz said nothing. The wind pushed at the window frame and something loose banged twice in the street and quit. Arjun picked up the Colt and went back to work.

    Father Rojas came at full dusk, carrying a wooden box that rattled when he set it on the counter. He was a small man with hands that looked too thin for the work they did, but Beatriz had seen him lift a fallen horse off a man's leg once, and he hadn't seemed to notice the weight.

    "You're cleaning guns," he said.

    "Someone has to."

    "I didn't say it was wrong." He opened the box. Inside were four bottles of sacramental wine and a dozen candles, good beeswax that filled the room with a clean sweet smell before they were ever lit. "I'm not supposed to use these for anything but the altar. I thought the altar might need to travel tonight."

    Arjun looked at the wine, then at the priest.

    "You're coming tomorrow."

    "I'm a priest. I don't fight."

    "That's not what I asked."

    Father Rojas closed the box. His hands were steady. "Yes. I'm coming. There will be men who need to make confession before. And there may be men who need last rites after. Either way, they shouldn't have to look far."

    Nobody spoke for a moment. The wind worked at the door. Beatriz lit a lamp against the gathering dark.

    "Judge Whitcomb sent a letter," she said. She reached behind the counter and brought out a folded paper, the seal already broken. "A boy brought it an hour ago."

    She handed it to Father Rojas. He read it aloud, his voice flat and careful, the way he read the names of the dead at funerals.

    "To the families of the south valley. I have been informed of the gathering of armed men at the Brandt ranch, and of similar preparations to the south. I urge all parties to stand down. Violence will serve no one. I remain hopeful that reason will prevail. God willing, cooler heads will govern the morning. I remain, in hope and prayer, Judge Amos Whitcomb."

    The priest folded the letter and set it on the counter.

    "God willing," he said. "God is often willing. The judge is not."

    Beatriz took the letter and dropped it in the stove. The paper curled and blackened and turned to ash that rose on the draft and vanished. None of them watched it go.

    "He sat in my church at noon," Rojas said, as if testing the words. "He is a good man."

    "He's a comfortable man," Arjun said. "That's different."

    "Is it?"

    The question hung in the lamplight. Arjun checked the Colt's action and set it down.

    "I don't know," he said. "I used to think it was."

    .

    The families gathered at the church after full dark.

    They came in ones and twos, wrapped against the cold, their boots loud on the wooden floor. The Martinezes arrived first, Esteban and Marta and Joaquin, who was twenty and solemn and carried his rifle like a tool he respected, and the three younger boys, scrubbed and silent and herded into a pew. Miriam Cohen came with David, who had combed his hair for it. Harmon Bly came alone, his stiff right arm held close, and took a seat near the stove without a word.

    The Gutierrezes came together, Miguel walking the way he walked now, slow, one shoulder low, Maria beside him, and Rosa between them. The bruise along Rosa's jaw had gone yellow. She sat in the front pew with her hands folded and her eyes on the crucifix above the altar, one of her father's carvings, and she did not speak. She had not spoken much since the road.

    Iris Barkley came in with her rifle in the crook of her arm and Etta asleep on her shoulder, and she laid the child down on a folded blanket in the second pew before she sat, and kept one hand on her the whole night. The Pfeiffer brothers came last, side by side, and Amos had his pipe lit now, church or no church, and Rojas looked at it and let it be.

    Arjun stood at the back and made his count without meaning to. Esteban and Joaquin. David. The Pfeiffers. Rivera. Bly. Iris, who shot better than any of them. Eight who could shoot. Nine with him. Against Elijah's twenty, and whatever had come down from the north range since.

    Esteban stood up and faced the room. He was a cattleman, not a speaker, and his voice came out rough.

    "I sat in this church at noon," he said. "I heard the man out. I have been turning it over all afternoon, whether there was something in it we were too proud to see." He looked down at his hat in his hands, then up. "There wasn't. He offered us our own valley back at rent. I wanted to say it out loud once, so that no man here thinks we didn't look for the other door. We looked. The room has one door and Brandt is standing in it."

    He sat down. Marta put her hand on his arm and he didn't pull away.

    "He'll come with hired guns from Laredo and El Paso," Arjun said from the back wall. "They ride for pay, not for ground. A man who rides for pay will risk his skin. He won't throw it away. That's the first thing to know about tomorrow."

    "And the second thing?" Elijah asked.

    "They think you're farmers."

    "We are farmers."

    "Yes. And farmers know ground." Arjun pushed off the wall and came up the aisle a few steps. "You know every draw and every cutbank between here and the north road. You know where the wind carries the dust and where the sun stands at first light. They'll be riding into country they never studied, against people they never met, for wages. You'll be behind cover you chose, on land your dead are buried in. Hold where I put you, wait for my signal, and when you fire, fire to hit. Men like that don't stay to die for another man's feud. They scatter."

    "And if you're wrong?" Iris said. Her hand was still on her daughter's back.

    "Then I'm wrong. But I've been wrong before, and I'm still standing. That counts for something."

    The room was quiet. Beatriz was watching him from her pew by the wall with something in her face he didn't try to name.

    Father Rojas opened the wooden box and began lighting the candles. One by one the small flames caught and held, and the church filled with the smell of honey. He set them along the altar rail, twelve candles in a row, then turned to face the families.

    "I can't tell you what is right. I'm a priest. I'm supposed to tell you to turn the other cheek. That's what the book says." He paused. The candles moved with the draft. "But the book also says there is a time for every purpose under heaven. A time to plant and a time to reap. A time to love and a time to hate. A time for war and a time for peace." He did not look at the front pew when he said it, which was how everyone knew for whom it was said. "I think you all know what time it is."

    He began the Mass. The Latin came low and steady, the words old as the desert, and the families knelt in the candlelight and said nothing. Arjun stayed standing at the back. He hadn't knelt in a church since his mother's funeral, and that was twenty years gone and another life. But he bowed his head. That much he could do.

    The Mass ended late. The families filed out into the cold, their breath smoking. Some went home to sleep what could be slept. The Martinezes spread blankets at the back of the church, and Marta held her youngest, and Esteban sat with his back to the wall and his rifle across his knees, staring at nothing.

    .

    Arjun walked out through the wind to the barn behind Beatriz's store.

    The Winchester leaned against the wall by his bedroll where it had leaned for weeks now. Tom Harken had put it in his hands in the war years and never let him pay for it, and it was the only thing Arjun owned that had come to him as a gift. He took it up and sat down on the bench with it across his knees.

    He field-stripped it in the near dark, working by feel, the way he'd learned when you couldn't risk a light. Breech bolt. Extractor. Firing pin. The parts came apart in his hands, each one loose and clean, nothing seized. He went over each one anyway, checking for rust, for wear, for anything that might fail when failure meant death.

    There was nothing. The rifle was as sound as the day it was given.

    He put it back together the same way, by touch, the metal clicking home piece by piece. When it was done he worked the lever. Smooth. The sound was smaller than he remembered, almost quiet. A rifle didn't care who held it. It only cared that it was held right.

    He opened a box of cartridges and began loading. Fifteen rounds. He pushed each one in with his thumb, feeling the spring tension build, and when the tube was full he set the rifle across his knees again. Then he loaded the Colt, five rounds, the hammer down on the empty chamber, the way Tom had taught him.

    Three years. He'd let men shove him. He'd let Brandt's riders cut wire and beat Miguel Gutierrez and take a girl off the road for nothing but being on it. He'd told himself it was principle.

    He wasn't sure anymore what it had been.

    Something moved in the barn door. He didn't turn. He knew the footsteps.

    "You're still here," Arjun said.

    "I don't sleep much," Kai said. "Never have."

    The blacksmith came in and sat on an upturned crate near the door. He didn't look at the rifle. He looked at Arjun, steady and unhurried, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the lantern hissed, and the wind ran its hand along the outside of the wall.

    "In the old country," Kai said, "they tell of an archer. The best of his age. A war came that had been building his whole life, and the two armies stood up on one field at first light. He had his driver take the chariot out into the middle ground, so he could see the men he was to kill."

    "And did he?"

    "He saw his teachers. His uncles. Boys he'd grown up swimming the river with. He put his bow down in the chariot and said he wouldn't do it. Said he'd rather be killed himself than spend one arrow on his own blood."

    Kai leaned and turned the lantern wick down a little, and the shadows in the barn moved and settled.

    "The man who drove his chariot stayed with him. Talked with him, out there between the armies. All that night, some say."

    The wind had died outside. The desert was still.

    "What happened?" Arjun said.

    "He fought. It cost what it cost. But it was his to fight, and he knew it by morning."

    Kai stood. The crate creaked under him and then was quiet. He paused at the door, a dark shape against the darker street.

    "Get some sleep," he said, and was gone.

    Arjun sat with the rifle and listened to his footsteps fade toward the forge. He thought about the Border Wars. He thought about the men he'd killed, their hands, when he could remember them, which was less often now than it used to be. He thought about Tom Harken, twelve miles north tonight among Brandt's fires, a good man who'd made a bad bargain and couldn't find the way out of it.

    The lantern burned low. He didn't sleep.

    .

    Deep in the night, Beatriz came to the barn. She carried a tin pot of coffee and two cups, and her hair was tied back with the same leather cord she wore every day. She looked tired. Not the kind sleep fixed. The kind that had settled into the lines around her eyes and stayed.

    "I saw your light," she said.

    She poured the coffee and handed him a cup. Their fingers touched and neither of them moved away.

    "How many do you think there really are?" she asked.

    "More than Elijah counted. Brandt's not stupid. He'll have men in the draws we can't see. Maybe thirty."

    "Thirty."

    "Give or take."

    She drank her coffee. She didn't seem surprised. "And if it goes badly?"

    "If it goes badly, Brandt takes the valley. He'll offer terms after, the same terms, and they'll sound kinder over fresh graves. Some of the families will take them. The ones who don't, he'll push out. In five years there won't be a south-valley ranch he doesn't own."

    "And if it goes well?"

    "Then he pulls back across the river, and the thing goes on from a different line. Nothing's settled. It just gets moved."

    "That's not much of a reward."

    "It's a tomorrow. Sometimes that's enough."

    Beatriz set her cup down on the bench. The coffee still steamed, but the cold was getting into it. "You asked me once why I stayed. After Rafael. Why I didn't sell the store and go back to my people in Santa Fe."

    "I remember."

    "I stayed because leaving felt like letting them win. Brandt's men. Whitcomb's silence. All of it. If I left, then they were right, and what they did in my yard didn't matter, and what they did to Rosa doesn't matter." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the bench without lifting it. "I stayed because I was too angry to leave. I still don't know if that was courage or just spite."

    "Maybe it's the same thing, depending on the light."

    She almost smiled. "You sound like Kai."

    "I've been talking to him too much."

    "Or listening for the first time."

    The night outside the door was still full black, no gray in it yet, the stars hard and close. Arjun stood and took up the gun belt.

    "Beatriz."

    She looked up.

    "If it goes badly, if they get past us, you take the buckskin and you ride south. Don't stop for anything. There's a stage station at Socorro. You'll be safe there."

    "I'm not running."

    "I know. But if you have to, you will. Promise me."

    She studied him. Somewhere south of town a dog barked once, an ordinary sound, and quit.

    "I promise," she said.

    She took the pot and the cups and went back across the yard, and he heard the store door close behind her, and the bolt.

    There was not much night left. He turned the lantern down to a bead and sat back against the wall with the rifle across his knees, and let what remained of the dark pass through him, minute by minute, like the cold.

    Chapter Seventeen

    The Watch

    Arjun did not sleep.

    He sat against the barn wall with the Winchester across his knees and watched the darkness thin toward something that was not yet morning. The barn smelled of hay and leather and the quiet heat of horses breathing in their stalls. Outside, Perdido held itself still. No dogs barking, no lamps burning, nothing moving but the wind working through the cracks in the adobe and the slow creak of the sign above Beatriz's store.

    He had been sitting there two hours. Maybe three. Time had stopped meaning much around midnight, when the Mass ended and the last of the south-valley people went out into the cold and the church door closed behind Father Rojas. The priest had not blessed the rifles. He had stood in the doorway and studied each of them as they passed. Esteban Martinez with a hand on Joaquin's shoulder. Miriam Cohen, and David behind her, seventeen and trying hard not to look it. The Pfeiffer brothers side by side, Amos with his pipe cold in his teeth. Iris Barkley carrying her daughter asleep against her shoulder, the child's red hair the one bright thing in all that candlelight. Rojas had watched them go and turned away without a word. Arjun understood. There were things a man of God could not say. There were things he could not unsay.

    So they were alone now. All of them.

    He worked the Winchester's lever halfway open and eased it closed. Smooth, clean, oiled. Tom Harken had put this rifle in his hands in the war years, the bluing deep as well water then, and said only that a man ought to own one thing that would not quit on him. It had not quit. Arjun had. Three years, and in all that time he had still kept it clean, because a man who lets his tools rust has given up in ways that go deeper than any promise not to fight.

    He remembered the last time he had fired it. After Devil's Arroyo, after the three days that emptied him out, there had been one more. A ridge above a dry creek bed in West Texas. A man on a gray horse riding into the open because he thought the shooting was over. Arjun had known it wasn't. He had called out, stay back, stay down, but the wind took his voice, and the man in the rocks above who meant to kill the rider did not hear him either.

    Arjun killed him first.

    What stayed with him was not the killing. It was the face of the man on the gray horse when he understood what had happened. Not anger. Not fear. Confusion. As if he could not work out why a stranger he had never met would choose to save his life.

    Arjun could not work it out either. He had ridden away from everything he knew how to do, and he had not fired at a man since. There had been comfort in that once. Tonight it was only a fact, like the cold.

    He set the rifle against the wall and stood, and took the gun belt from where it lay and buckled it on. The holster settled against his hip, the leather exactly where it had always been, and he slid the Colt into it. A man dressed at last for the work he had walked three years to get away from.

    The barn door opened without a sound. He had oiled the hinges two days ago. A small thing, the kind of thing a man does when he is getting ready for war whether he admits it or not.

    The night was cold and clear and thick with stars, the moon long down. The only light in town was the low red of the forge behind the livery, where Kai kept his fire banked through the dark hours. The wagon was a shape, and the anvil was a shape, but the man was not to be seen.

    Arjun walked.

    Past the store. Past the church, where one candle still burned behind the glass. Past the well at the center of town, where the dust lay thick and the hoofprints of Brandt's riders from the noon parley still held their edges. He walked until the buildings fell away behind him and the road opened into the country between the valleys. North and south. The river between them, and the ford, and the dawn coming to it.

    He stopped.

    There was a rider on the road ahead.

    Neither of them moved. The horse shifted its weight and blew through its nostrils. Arjun did not reach for the Colt on his hip. He knew the set of that rider the way he knew his own hands.

    "You're a long way from the north valley," Arjun said.

    "So are you."

    Tom Harken stepped down. Deliberate, unhurried, a man who had learned early that hurry killed more men than it saved. He dropped the claybank's reins in the road and left them lying, and the horse stood over them like it had been built there. Tom came forward until the starlight found his face. The turned-up brim. The iron-gray mustache. The lines around his mouth had gone deeper since the trail, and the trail was not two months back.

    "You're not armed," Arjun said.

    "No."

    "That's not like you."

    Tom stood with his hands loose at his sides, palms forward, empty. Arjun had seen those hands kill faster than most men could clear leather. Tom let them hang there in the dark and said nothing, and that was answer enough.

    "They'll come at first light," Tom said. "Down to the ford. You know that."

    "I know."

    "He'll bring eighteen, maybe twenty. He's got thirty on the books but most of them are cattle hands, and cattle hands don't cross rivers for another man's feud. The ones who will are out of Laredo and El Paso, and they've done this before."

    "You ride out here to count for me?"

    "No." Tom's hands went to his pockets, an old habit, a way of keeping them still when what he was saying cost him. "I came to finish what I couldn't finish on the trail. I started it that morning and I choked on it and I told you to leave instead. You remember."

    "I remember."

    "I've had three days to get it in order. If I stop now it stays stopped."

    Arjun waited. The wind moved through the sage and the dry grass at the edge of the road.

    "When Brandt hired me, I told myself it was a roof. A man has to eat. After a war there's nothing waiting for you, no home, no ground that wants you standing on it. He offered a bunk and wages and I told myself I wasn't choosing anything. I was just getting out of the weather."

    "You were choosing."

    "I know it. I knew it then. The truth was ugly and I was tired, and a tired man can lie to himself near about forever." His voice had gone flat, stripped of anything that might soften it. "First time the wire got cut, I told myself it was business. Water and grass, the oldest quarrel there is. When they brought Gutierrez back from the arroyo I didn't see it happen, but I saw Dobbs's knuckles at breakfast, and I ate my breakfast. When the Gutierrez girl was run down on the road I wasn't there and didn't know till after, and when I knew, I wanted to kill the men that did it. Brandt sat me down. Said it was drink, said it got away from them, said he'd handle it." Tom's mouth twisted. "He handled it with bonus money and a word to keep quiet. I found that out two days ago."

    "And you're still wearing his brand."

    "I am."

    "Why?"

    Tom turned and walked a few paces down the road, his back to Arjun, his shape black against the stars. When he turned around his voice was older.

    "You know why. I told you about the canyon. Six rustlers and one of me and no way out, and Brandt rode in with no reason in the world, and killed two of them himself, and carried me to a doctor and paid the bill and never asked for a thing." He paused. "That story's true. Every word. I've been leaning on it for four years. But it's not the whole of it, and the whole of it is what I came to say."

    Arjun stood very still.

    "Four years ago I was passing through this country. Not his man yet. Just a rider with a played-out horse and enough money for a bottle, and the bottle was in Gus's place, right here in Perdido. That was the night Rafael Vargas died." Tom's voice did not change, and that was the worst part of it. "There were Brandt men in the saloon that evening, talking the way men talk when they think everybody in the room draws the same pay. Three of them were riding out at dusk to the Vargas place. To help a stubborn man see sense. That was the phrase. I heard it, and I told myself it was ranch talk. Men lean on each other over water and it mostly comes to nothing." He breathed once, slow. "I drank three whiskeys and I went to my room, and before dawn I rode out. A week later the word had gone all the way around. What they did to him in his own yard. His wife stood there with a gun on her and made to watch, and he lasted hours with his head in her lap, and was gone before sunrise. I heard all of it. And that same week I rode up to the Brandt ranch and took his wages."

    The rooster that would crow at dawn was still hours from crowing. There was no sound at all.

    "I didn't know what they'd do," Tom said. "That's the sentence I've slept on for four years. I didn't know. But I knew they were riding, and I knew what kind of men they were, and I drank my whiskey. There's a difference between not knowing and not wanting to know, and I've spent four years pretending I was on the right side of it. I wasn't. I knew, Arjun. And I let it happen. And then I took the money."

    Arjun looked at his oldest friend, and his oldest friend looked back and did not flinch, which was its own kind of courage, the last kind a ruined man has left.

    "Why tell me?" Arjun said. "You could have carried it over the ridge tomorrow and nobody living would ever have known."

    "I'd have known." Tom's jaw worked. "And I already lost you once. I wasn't going to stand across a river from you tomorrow with a lie between us on top of everything else."

    "You didn't lose me."

    "Four years. First I thought you were dead. Then I heard Texas. I stopped looking." He said it evenly, like a man reading a bill of sale. "That was worse."

    "Worse how?"

    "Looking for a dead man is grief. Quitting on a live one is something else. I never did find the name for it."

    Arjun took that standing, the way you take a blow you have earned. "I walked away from the killing," he said. "Not from you."

    "From where I stood, was there a difference?"

    "No." The word came out plain. "There wasn't. Every time I saw your face I saw what I was. The gun, the count, all of it. I thought if I stayed away long enough I'd forget how to be that man. I didn't forget. You don't forget. You only choose."

    Tom nodded slowly, and some of the iron went out of his shoulders. "Me neither," he said.

    The wind came down off the mountains, colder now, the cold that runs ahead of morning. Somewhere far off a coyote called, once, twice, and quit.

    "The canyon was real," Arjun said. "What he did for you was real. I'm not taking it from you. But you've been paying on that debt for four years with other people's money. Rafael Vargas paid on it. Gutierrez paid on it. The girl on the road paid on it." He let a breath go. "You don't owe a man your soul for saving your body."

    Tom was quiet. His thumb moved along the seam of his trouser leg, back and forth, an old tell Arjun had watched across a hundred campfires.

    "I've known that a long while," Tom said at last. "Knowing never moved my feet."

    "Then what are you doing, Tom? Tonight. Standing on this road. What is it?"

    "Trying to be honest. Maybe for the first time since he pulled me out of that canyon." He raised his head. His eyes were wet and steady both. "I can't fight against him. I can't put a sight on the man who carried me out. It doesn't matter what he's become. The debt doesn't care what he's become. But I can't fight for him either. Not after tonight. Not saying out loud what I just said."

    "Then don't."

    Tom studied him.

    "Don't fight for him. Don't fight against him. Don't pull a trigger tomorrow at all."

    "That's not how it works. I ride under his brand. When they cross that river I'll be in the line, and my men look to me. If I don't lead, Brandt reads it off me before the first shot."

    "Then don't be in the line."

    "Desert."

    "Call it what you want."

    "Men get hanged for deserting."

    "Men get hanged for a lot of things. Including standing in a line they don't believe in and watching their friends die on the other side of it." Arjun stepped closer. The east had begun to gray, the stars going out along the ridgeline one by one. "You came out here in the middle of the night to tell me the truth. That's more than most men manage in a lifetime. But saying you were wrong isn't the same as doing something about it."

    "You're asking me to quit my post."

    "I'm asking you to choose."

    "I can't. I told you that."

    "You told me you can't shoot at Brandt. I heard you. I'm not asking you to shoot at him." Arjun's voice was steady and quiet, the voice he used on the training ground when a man's hands were shaking. "I'm asking you not to shoot at us. That's a different thing. That's a third thing."

    Tom stared at him.

    "Tomorrow morning his riders come down to the ford. On the south bank there'll be farmers behind cover, waiting. Men who never did you any harm. A widow who runs a hundred acres alone. A boy of seventeen still working on his first mustache. You can put a rifle on them. Or you can sit your horse and do nothing."

    "If I do nothing, Brandt will know."

    "Let him."

    "He'll call me a coward."

    "Is that worse than being one?"

    Tom rocked back on his heels as if the ground had moved. His face went pale, then dark, then settled into something that was neither, something only tired and old.

    "You always did know where to hit," he said.

    "I learned it from you."

    Something twitched at the corner of Tom's mouth, there and gone, a crack in a wall three years in the building.

    "Just don't shoot at us," Arjun said again. "That's all I'm asking. You don't have to turn on him. You don't have to ride with us. You sit your horse and let the fight happen without you. Can you do that?"

    Tom studied the dust at his feet, the hoofprints running north toward Brandt's ground and south toward the families sleeping their last night before the war. The gray in the east had climbed a hand's width.

    "If I do that," he said finally, "Brandt might kill me himself."

    "He might."

    "And you'll be across the water. You won't be able to stop it."

    "No."

    "You're asking me to bet my life on people I don't know."

    "I'm asking you to bet it on yourself." Arjun closed the last of the distance between them. Face to face now, near enough to see the gray in the stubble and the years in the eyes. "You rode out here because you couldn't keep lying. That's the first step. The second one is harder."

    "What's the second one?"

    "Acting like you know what you know."

    Tom held his gaze a long moment. Then he nodded, small, barely there, but there.

    "I won't shoot at them," he said. "And I won't order my men to. That's all I have to give you."

    "It's enough."

    "It might not be."

    "It's enough," Arjun said. "It's more than I had before you rode out here."

    Tom turned his head toward the east, where the gray was going pale at the rim of the world. "Dawn's coming."

    "I know."

    "I should get back. He notices things."

    "He notices everything. Go anyway."

    Tom walked to the claybank and gathered the reins off the ground and stepped up. He sat the saddle looking down at Arjun, and for a moment the years fell off him, the hard lines, the gray, the weight, and underneath was the young man who had taught a rancher's son to shoot on the Nueces and been better company than any man since.

    "If this goes bad tomorrow," Tom said. "If it goes bad for you out there."

    "Then it goes bad."

    "I don't want to lose you again."

    "You lost me once because I walked off without a word. I've been walking back ever since." Arjun laid a hand on the claybank's neck, once, and took it away. "Tomorrow I figure to arrive."

    "Don't die tomorrow," Tom said.

    "I'll do my best."

    "That's not what I asked."

    "No," Arjun said. "It's not."

    Tom turned the horse north. Arjun watched him go, the rider's shape climbing away against a sky that was giving up its stars. At the top of the first rise Tom stopped and turned in the saddle and raised one hand. Arjun raised his.

    Then the rise was empty, and Arjun stood alone on the road between the valleys while the light came up the eastern ridge, gray going to silver, silver thinking about gold.

    He turned back toward town.

    Kai was at the forge when Arjun came up the street. The fire was alive now, orange and breathing under the bellows' slow stroke, and Kai stood over it holding a length of iron in the coals, reading the color. He did not look up.

    "You spoke with him," Kai said.

    "Yes."

    "And?"

    "He won't fight against us."

    Kai nodded. He turned the iron in the fire. "That is not a small thing."

    "It doesn't feel like much."

    "It is more than you had last night. It is more than he had." Kai drew the iron out, orange gone almost to white at the edges, and set it on the anvil. "He made a choice. Most men only do what they are told. And a man who has made one true choice can make another. The door is open now. It was closed for four years."

    Arjun took in the forge, the tools in their rows, the water bucket, the hands that seemed to know what the metal needed before the metal knew it. "You knew he'd come."

    "I hoped."

    "You could have told me."

    "No. You needed it from him." Kai picked up his hammer. "Go rest. There is not much night left, and none of it to waste."

    Arjun turned toward the barn. Then he stopped.

    "Kai."

    "Yes."

    "Tomorrow. When the shooting starts. What will you do?"

    Kai brought the hammer down. The ring of it went out across the empty street, clear and bright, startling in the stillness, and the sparks jumped from the anvil and died in the dust.

    "I will be here," he said. "At the forge. Where I have always been."

    He brought the hammer down again, and the iron took the blow and bent to it, and in the east the ridge showed its edge.

    Arjun walked back through the graying street to take what dark was left. Behind him the hammer kept its slow time, ringing the morning in.

    Chapter Eighteen

    The Choice at the River

    The cold came first.

    It rose off the arroyo floor before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, a dry cold that had nothing to do with winter and everything to do with waiting. Arjun Tucker had come back from the road in the last of the dark, and he had not slept at all. He had cleaned the Winchester again instead. Not because it needed it. His hands needed something to do that wasn't shake.

    The barn smelled of hay and old leather and the sharp copper of gun oil. Thirty cartridges lay on a feed sack in rows of five, each one set down with the kind of care a man brings to a thing he might not need and cannot afford to need and not have. He fed them into his belt loops and his coat pocket, counting without meaning to. The buckskin mare watched him over the stall rail, patient, indifferent. She had seen men get ready for things before and held an animal's opinion of it.

    The Colt rode his hip. Five rounds, hammer on the empty chamber, the way Tom had taught him half a lifetime ago. He picked up the rifle and stepped into the yard.

    Dust rose around his boots and settled. Everything in this country did that. The wind blew, the dust lifted, the dust fell. Men came, made their noise, and went, and the dust remained. He had thought about that more than he wanted to. Kai had seen to it.

    The forge was lit. Orange light through the open side of the lean-to, and against it Kai's shape, broad and still, turning something in the fire. He did not look up when Arjun came near. He never seemed to need to.

    "You're early," Kai said.

    "You're earlier."

    "I have work." He drew a length of iron from the coals and laid it on the anvil. A hinge, plain and honest. He began to shape it with small exact strokes. "Esteban asked me to mend his gate. I told him I would."

    "Today?"

    "Today seemed like a good day for gates." The hammer turned the iron, struck again, and the ring of it went down the empty street. "Gates keep things in. Keep things out. They swing both ways. A hinge doesn't care who opens the door."

    Arjun watched the north ridge. Still dark, but the sky behind it was thinning, a gray line that would go pink and then gold and then the hard white of a New Mexico morning. "They'll come with the light."

    "Yes."

    "You could be wrong."

    "Men who have spent the night talking themselves into a thing they fear come at first light. Any later and they start to hear themselves." Kai took the hinge up in the tongs and sank it in the bucket. Steam rose between them. "Eat something."

    "Not hungry."

    "Eat anyway. Thinking is done. Feed the body that has to do the rest."

    Arjun stood a moment more in the heat that came off the fire. Then he walked toward the store, and behind him the hammer took up its beat again, steady as anything left in the valley.

    Lamplight in the store window. Beatriz was behind the counter with the shotgun laid across it and a plate of cornbread and beans set beside the shotgun, as if the two things had always gone together. She read his face the way she read a bill of freight.

    "You didn't sleep."

    "Some."

    "Eat."

    He sat on a crate and ate. The cornbread was dry and the beans were cold and he tasted neither. Outside, wagon wheels somewhere, low voices, a door. The town waking for something that wasn't morning.

    "Beatriz. If it goes wrong today."

    "It won't."

    "You can't know that."

    "I know you." She came around the counter and stood in front of him, plain gray dress, sleeves rolled, the gray showing at her temples. "You think I kept you on for the freight?"

    "You said as much."

    "I said what you needed to hear. Gus came by that first night before I locked up. He told me how you looked at Brandt and his men in his place. The way a wolf looks at dogs, he said. You didn't do anything. Didn't say anything. But you looked. And I thought: there's a man who remembers how."

    "Remembering how and wanting to are different."

    "I know. I've spent four years remembering how to want things." She broke the shotgun, checked the loads, and snapped it shut. "I'll be at the wagon."

    "Behind it. Low."

    "I know where behind is." She took her hat off the peg. "Finish the plate."

    The street was filling in the gray. Esteban Martinez with his rifle in the crook of his arm and Joaquin beside him, silent as ever. David Cohen, seventeen, the mustache still losing its argument, carrying his rifle too carefully. Iris Barkley with her own rifle slung easy, the only one of them who wore it like a tool. Rivera. Harmon Bly, whose right arm would not straighten and who had come anyway. The Pfeiffer brothers side by side, Amos with his pipe cold in his teeth, Elijah for once not talking.

    Father Rojas stood in the church door in his plain brown clothes.

    "I rang the bell," he said.

    "I heard."

    "I don't know if I was calling them to prayer or to war." He paused. "Maybe this morning they're the same thing."

    "Maybe they always are."

    Rojas took in the yard, the rifles, the faces. "I have buried three men from this valley. Two of them weren't fighters. I don't want to dig any more holes."

    "Neither do I."

    "Then what are we doing?"

    "We're doing what's left," Arjun said. "When everything else has been tried."

    Rojas nodded slowly and stepped aside. "Church is open. For anyone who wants it."

    Some went in. Most stood where they were.

    David Cohen came over holding the rifle stiff-armed, muzzle pointed at nothing, trigger hand nowhere near the guard. "Mr. Tucker. I never shot at a man."

    "I know. Center of the chest. Not the head, not the legs. And squeeze it, the way you did Thursday. It's the same rifle."

    "It's not the same target."

    "No. But it's the same you."

    The boy swallowed and nodded and went back to stand near Iris, who moved half a step so their elbows almost touched and said nothing at all.

    Arjun led them south out of town, down through the fields toward the river, and nobody talked on the way.

    The ford lay where the valley narrowed, the river running wide and shin-deep over gravel there, with dark seams of deeper water above and below. Cottonwoods on both banks. On the south side the wash came down at a slant, chest-deep and dry, and they had spent two weeks of evenings making it look like nothing. The freight wagon stood where it had stood since the rains, tipped on its side and filled with rock, thirty yards off the water. Downstream the cutbank made a wall a man could shoot over kneeling.

    He put the Pfeiffers in the wash with Rivera and Bly. Esteban and Joaquin at the cutbank. Iris and David behind the wagon, and Beatriz behind the wagon, and he did not tell Beatriz to stay low a second time because a thing said twice to her stopped being true.

    He stood at the head of the ford under the big cottonwood and said it once more, plain.

    "Nobody fires until I fire. When you fire, shoot at the men shooting at you. Not the ones holding back, not the ones turning away. A man who rides for pay will risk his skin. He won't throw it away. When they break, let them go."

    "And if they fire first?" Esteban said.

    "Then you don't need my permission."

    The old man nodded once. "Good."

    After that there was nothing to do but wait, and the cold came up off the water, and a killdeer walked the gravel bar crying about its ordinary business, and the sun cleared the ridge and lit the tops of the cottonwoods first, the way it always did.

    They came with the light.

    Eighteen riders off the north slope, walking their horses, spreading as they came. Elijah had counted twenty and more coming, and some of the bought patience had evidently thought better of the river in the night. Silas Brandt in the center on the blood bay, town coat, clean hat, sitting his saddle like a man riding out to view his own land. And slightly apart from the line, on the claybank, the turned-up hat brim catching the first sun, Tom Harken.

    Near the front rode Dobbs, big and fist-faced, and beside him Kid Lawry, narrow-faced, holding his reins in too tight a fist. Arjun marked him the way you mark a green horse in a corral full of steady ones. Behind them, strung loose, the rest. Perhaps a dozen were guns. The others were cattle hands, and they rode like men who wished they were anywhere with cattle in it.

    The line halted at the water's edge. The river ran between them, talking to its gravel.

    "Mr. Tucker." Brandt's voice came smooth and easy across the water. "You've picked the wrong side of a simple piece of business."

    "It's simple. That's why I picked it."

    "These people will sell inside a year, at half what I offered. All you're buying them is a hard morning." Brandt shook his head, almost kindly. "You fought in the Border Wars. You know how this ends. Why die for a valley that won't remember your name?"

    "Maybe I'm not doing it for the valley."

    "For what, then?"

    "For the dust. It's all going to be dust anyway. Might as well be dust that means something."

    Brandt stared at him. The easy smile flickered, just once. He had expected fear, or bargaining. He took his time with the south bank instead, the tipped wagon, the wash that looked like nothing, the woman standing behind the wagon with a shotgun and a face four years in the hardening. Then his eyes went to the center of his own line.

    Tom Harken sat the claybank still and quiet, and he was not advancing, and he was not going to.

    Tom raised his right hand. Slowly. Deliberately. He raised it to his hat and touched the brim. Not a surrender. Not an allegiance. Just a recognition, one man to another, across a distance no bullet or word or year could bridge.

    Arjun nodded once.

    Tom turned the claybank and rode away, up the north slope, not fast, not running, the way a man rides when he has made his peace with what he is and what he isn't.

    Brandt's jaw tightened. "Harken."

    Tom did not turn. He kept riding until his shape went into the morning light and was gone.

    The line shifted. Saddles creaked. The hired men had come for a fight that had changed its shape under them, and every man there could do the arithmetic of one rider leaving and what he had taken with him.

    Brandt's face closed like a ledger.

    "Take the ford," he said.

    For one breath nothing moved. Then the first horses stepped down into the shallows, and the killdeer went up off the gravel bar screaming, and Kid Lawry's nerve broke. He fired at nothing, high through the cottonwoods, cutting leaves, and the leaves were still falling when the morning came apart.

    Arjun brought the sights level on the man beside Lawry, a Laredo gun already swinging his rifle toward the wagon. Something Kai had said to him his first week in the valley passed through him and was gone. You're tired of carrying something heavy. He pressed the trigger the way you close a door.

    The man went backward out of the saddle and the river took him.

    The south bank spoke all together then, the wash and the cutbank and the wagon, one long ragged sound. Horses screamed and spun in the shallows. Two more saddles emptied. A horse went down thrashing at the lip of the deep seam and its rider clawed loose and floundered away downstream, and nobody shot at him.

    The cattle hands never reached the water. At the first lead that came back they wheeled and were gone up the slope, quirting, and the slope was empty behind them before the second volley.

    The ones who stayed were the guns, and they came on shooting from horseback, which is mostly noise. Lead knocked long splinters off the wagon box and slapped dirt from the cutbank. David Cohen fired and worked the lever and fired, and his cheek was bleeding from a splinter and he did not know it yet.

    Pruett got his horse to midstream and steadied it crosswise and shot at the wagon, steady, workmanlike, the way he had once beaten a man to death in his own yard. Arjun laid the front sight on his chest. At Devil's Arroyo there had been a roaring in him for three days. There was no roaring now. He fired once, and Pruett folded over the horn and slid down into the river, and the current turned him over and took him.

    A rifleman had gained the gravel bar and was shooting long into the wash. In the wash, Amos Pfeiffer sat down like a man easing into a chair. Elijah caught him by the shoulder and the front of Amos's shirt was already dark. Amos looked at the sky over the cottonwoods.

    "Well," he said, as if a question had finally been answered to his satisfaction.

    He was gone before Elijah got his hat off. Elijah laid him back against the sand. Then he fired his own rifle dry, took up his brother's Henry, and went on shooting, tears cutting through the dust on his face and his aim never moving. Iris put the man on the gravel bar down with one round, the way she had once taken a coyote at two hundred yards, and did not look at what she had done.

    And then it broke. Between one breath and the next there was no fight, only men wheeling horses out of the shallows and spurring north, and riderless horses lunging up both banks with their stirrups flying, and the river running loud in the new quiet.

    Brandt was shouting names. Names of men who no longer answered to them, sitting his blood bay alone at the water's edge with his line dissolving up the slope behind him.

    Then the shouting stopped, and for a moment he sat still, and Arjun watched him tally it, because Silas Brandt tallied everything. The men gone. The water lost. The inquest that would come now, with nobody left afraid enough to lie for him. A man who had bought his way through every door in the territory, sitting his horse in front of the one door that had no price on it.

    Then he put the bay into the river.

    He came across at a lunge, water bursting white at the horse's chest, coat dark with spray, hat gone, a man who had never once in his life been told no and had no place inside himself to put it. Arjun stepped out from the cottonwood into the open.

    "Brandt. It's done."

    Brandt drew. He was fast, off a plunging horse at close range, and none of it mattered. Arjun fired once, clean, and the sound went up the valley and came back off the ridge alone.

    The bay lunged on without its rider and came ashore shaking. Silas Brandt lay in the shallows at the edge of his neighbor's land, and the river moved around him the way it moved around any stone, and what it took from him it carried south.

    From Lawry's first shot it had been perhaps ninety seconds. The valley took its quiet back as though it had only lent it out.

    Arjun lowered the Winchester. His hands were steady. He had waited three years for what would come up in him when he fired at a man again, and it had come, and it was grief, and it was bearable, and it was not the arroyo. He stood in the open with the smoke thinning off the water and let each of those be true.

    Five of Brandt's men lay in the river and on the gravel, and Brandt. In the wash, Amos Pfeiffer lay with his hat over his face and his cold pipe still in his shirt pocket. Rivera's shoulder was ploughed open and bound with his own bandanna. David Cohen found the blood on his cheek and stared at his fingers. Somebody was sick in the willows, quietly, and nobody spoke of it then or after.

    They caught the horses. They carried the dead ashore and laid them in a row on the grass, Brandt's men and Brandt, because the river was nobody's grave. Beatriz came down from the wagon and stood beside Arjun, and neither of them said anything, and the shotgun hung unfired at her side.

    They buried Amos that afternoon in the churchyard, next to the Vargas stones, where the ground looked east. The whole valley came. Father Rojas said the words, and kept them few, which Amos would have counted a mercy.

    Elijah stood at the head of the grave with his brother's hat in his hands.

    "He told me once he wasn't much for dying in bed," he said. "That's all the speech he'd have stood for."

    He put his own hat back on and picked up a shovel, and the sound of dirt on wood went on in the sunlight, which is the oldest sound there is.

    Judge Whitcomb's buggy raised dust on the town road while the grave was still filling. He came through the stones slowly, hat in his hands, white hair bare in the sun. Nobody reproached him. Nobody looked up from the work.

    "There will have to be a finding," he said, to no one in particular. "An inquest."

    "Then hold one," Beatriz said, not unkindly, and turned back to the grave.

    He stood at the edge of the burying until it was done, and no one needed anything of him, and he took that home with him in the empty buggy.

    Toward evening a boy who had been up the valley after strays said he had seen a claybank horse going north through the gap, traveling easy, and gone. Arjun heard it and let it be. The bonds that matter don't break, he thought. They stretch.

    He sat on the store porch with Beatriz while the light went long and red across the street. Two cups. The coffee was hers, which meant it was good.

    "They'll want to make you something now," she said. "Marshal. Something with a word to it."

    "I unload freight."

    Something eased at the corner of her mouth, not quite a smile. "Freight comes Thursday."

    "I'll be here."

    She sat with him until the first stars came, and then she rose and went in, and she left the door open behind her, and the lamplight came out through it and lay across the porch boards, and he sat inside the edge of it a while before he went to the barn.

    In the morning the forge was cold.

    The lean-to stood bare, the ground under it swept, the tools gone into the cart, the mule standing in the traces switching its tail at nothing. Kai was lashing down the last of the load. Everything he had brought into the valley was on the cart, and nothing the valley had given him, except one thing he was leaving.

    On the anvil stump, small and dark in the early light, lay two links of chain. The broken ones from the training weeks, reforged and closed into each other, joined so clean you could not find where either one had failed.

    "You're going," Arjun said.

    "The work here was never mine to finish. Only to tend. It's tended."

    "Just like that."

    "I came the same way."

    Arjun stood by the stump. He did not pick up the links. Not yet.

    "Yesterday," he said, and stopped, because he did not know which part of it he meant.

    Kai drew the last rope tight and turned to face him, and his eyes were dark and still, the way they had been at the well the first morning. "A man who does the work without needing the harvest is free."

    "And Amos?"

    "Freer than most."

    Arjun looked north up the wagon road, the way the cart would go, the way Tom had gone. "Who are you?" he said. "Once. Plain."

    Kai regarded him. Then he took up the mule's lead rope, and clucked to the mule, and the cart began to move, and that was the whole of his answer.

    Arjun walked with him as far as the end of the street and then stopped and let him go on. At the edge of town the cart drew up beside Beatriz's fence, where the gate latch had hung crooked since spring and everyone had learned the trick of it and no one had fixed it. Kai stood at the gate perhaps five minutes with a file and a pair of pliers from the cart. When he went on, the latch dropped home behind him with a small clean sound, like a word at the end of a sentence.

    The cart went north up the valley road, creaking, the mule's ears loose, the man walking beside it at the pace of a man with far to go and no hurry in him, and the distance took them both.

    Arjun stood in the street until even the dust of them had settled. Then he went and got the buckskin mare.

    Riding out he passed the mercantile, and through the open door he heard a broom working the plank floor, and under the broom, half to itself, a girl's voice humming a song from the mission school, losing the tune and finding it again.

    He rode south with a coil of new wire and a stretcher and a sack of staples, down past the wash and the ford, where the river ran clear over the gravel as if it had never carried anything anywhere, to the south line where Brandt's men had cut the fence three times in the spring. The cut ends had rusted where they curled in the grass.

    He pried the old staples and drew the ruined wire and cut the frayed ends clean with the knife from his belt, the knife he had made with his own hands at Kai's forge, the blade that still showed his hammer marks. It cut clean. It had never once been asked to do anything else.

    The sun climbed. The mare grazed on her ground-reined patch and moved with the shade. He stretched the new wire and stapled it and went on down the line, post by post, and the work was slow and plain and asked nothing of him but his hands.

    The wind came up the valley and moved the grass. Out on the road a wagon was going somewhere, and the dust rose behind it, and hung, and settled.

    He bent to the wire and went on working.
